# Chapter 2 newcomer audit

Source: Clara E. Mattei, The Capital Order (2022).

Chapter 2 as recorded loses newcomers for three reasons:

- **It's fast.** Malina speaks at 164 words a minute; the project plans for 140.
- **It starts mid-conversation.** The opening only makes sense if you've seen chapter 1.
- **The rule comes too late.** It's said once, three minutes in, after the cases meant to test it.

A newcomer mostly can't say back the main claim afterwards.

The full notes, with every sentence of every page marked, are in `33 Audit - chapter 2 script - newcomer listening.md` in the work folder. The speed, pauses and word counts are measured from the recording's word timings. The three listeners are imagined, and no real newcomer has heard the film yet.

The three listeners:
- **S**: a 17-year-old school student.
- **B**: a 60-year-old retired builder who left school at 16.
- **E**: an adult whose first language isn't English.

## 1. What the recording shows

**Speed**
- Malina averages **164 words a minute**. Chapter 1 ran at about 156.
- The pages that matter most are the fastest: page 5 at 173, page 8 at 175 (it holds the main rule) and page 10 at 176.

**Pauses**
- Each "..." lasts about half a second to a second, counting the word itself. That's no longer than an ordinary full stop.
- No pause inside a page is longer than about a second. Page turns give 1.4 to 1.6 seconds.

**Length**
- The script is **689 words** as spoken (the brief said about 620).
- At 140 words a minute, the same 4 minutes 12 seconds of speech holds about 587 words.
- So the script must lose about 100 words before adding anything. With the explanations below added, about 180 to 220 words of today's content must go: that's two or three whole cases. The other choice is a longer film.

## 2. Page by page: where each listener gets lost

**Page 1**
- "So... won't creating new money just cause inflation?" (All newcomers)
  - "So" points back to chapter 1, which they haven't seen.
  - Who creates new money, and why would anyone? B already answers "yes".
- "That's inflation... prices in general" (E): "in general" can sound like a passing remark rather than the definition.
- "Not one price tag. The whole basket." This is set up but never paid off. Page 7's fuel point needed it.
- "go back to the shell factory" (All newcomers): which factory? E may think of a sea shell. "Drives it" is a figure of speech.

**Page 2**
- "Britain, 1915" (S, E): nobody has said there's a war on yet.
- "Unemployment, the share of workers without a job, is under one percent."
  - The definition is squeezed into the middle of the sentence.
  - E: "share" (a part) clashes with "shares" (in companies) on page 5.
- "paid for partly with new money" (All)
  - Which war? "The First World War" is never said.
  - Money from whom? That idea comes from chapter 1.
- "turned over to war work" and "more money is chasing fewer things" (E): figures of speech.
- "by the end of the war... since it began": an unsaid jump to 1918 and back to 1914.
- Risk: this first case seems to answer page 1 with "yes".

**Page 3**
- "New money arrives" (All): from where, brought by whom? How the money gets spent is never said.
- E: "idle", "shells", "flat out", "bid against".
- "buyers bid... prices climb" (All): the factory makes shells, but page 1 was about the shopping basket. The link between the two is never made.
- **The rule is shown but never said.** The page file's notes suggest no words on screen say it either. A listener can't carry a picture forward the way they can carry a sentence.

**Page 4**
- "Homes work the same way" (All): the same way as what? The point ("can't be built in a hurry") comes 17 seconds later.
- Glasgow (S, E): where is it? (Scotland. Not said.)
- E: "poured", "as much as a quarter", "the rise" (as a noun), "households", "froze".
- Which Parliament? A New Zealander may think of ours.
- The rent strike is a second story whose point is never said.
- There's no new money anywhere on this page, so a newcomer can't see why it's in the film. The missing link: more people chasing the same number of homes.

**Page 5 (the heaviest page)**
- "So test it" (All): test what? The rule was never stated.
- Japan 2013 (All): a 98-year jump with no bridge.
- The 21-word Japan sentence has a definition squeezed in, and "the bank that creates the yen" rests on chapter 1. It has to be heard twice.
- E: "if... they'd soar".
- Japan's lesson is missing: *why* didn't prices move? The ledger gives the mainstream answer, said as the economists' view: most of the money sat in the banks and wasn't spent (C2-C06, C2-XB03). B is left with a puzzle, not a reason to change his mind.
- "After the 2008 crash" (All)
  - Back five years and back to Britain, with no bridge.
  - S was born around then. E may hear a car crash.
  - Another squeezed-in definition, and "hundreds of billions" is too big to picture.
- "pushed up the prices of shares and other investments... whoever owned the most"
  - S, E: "shares", "investments", "gains". The most *what*?
  - **The worst risk on the page:** the film has used "prices" to mean shopping prices, so a newcomer can hear "so it did cause inflation". The lesson goes unsaid: the money went into things the richest own, not into the shopping basket.

**Page 6**
- No bridge from 2008 back to 1923, and "second test" is never said. Three new places at once.
- E: "racing", "heartland".
- "one American dollar costs more than four trillion German marks" (All)
  - That's the value of German money, not a shop price. The step between them is missing.
  - S, E: "marks" sounds like school marks.
  - "Trillion" can't be pictured. The ledger allows something that can: "a loaf of bread cost billions" (C2-C03).
- The lesson ("less and less was being made") is said, which is good, but it isn't tied back to the second factory.
- Four trillion is the most vivid number in the film, and it backs the claim the chapter questions.

**Page 7**
- "Late 1973" (All): where? No place is named.
  - Accuracy: the ledger supports "prices already climbing" for the US only (C2-XC01).
- B, E: "quadruples". E: "dearer".
- Two different "prices" sit in one breath: shopping prices and the price of oil.
- "the cause isn't spending" (All): "spending" is a new name for new money.
- "It's costs" (All): costs of what?
- "And here?": a 53-year jump in two words.
- B, E: "in the year to June", spoken decimals.
- "Leave fuel out... two point nine" (All): what does that mean? Is 2.9 good or bad? The lesson ("one price tag, fuel, did much of it") and the payoff for page 1 are both missing.

**Page 8**
- "So yes... new money can push prices up, when..."
  - This is **the chapter's main rule**, said once: 28 words, at 175 words a minute, three minutes in.
  - "So yes" — yes to what? It makes the rule sound like the narrator giving ground.
  - It has to be heard twice.
- "In twenty twenty-one..." (All): why 2021? The pandemic and the money put in then are never mentioned.
- House prices aren't in the shopping basket, and the film doesn't say so.
- The review sentence (26 words)

  - "the support" — for what? "the bank" — which one? "too strong" — a strong what?

**Page 9**
- Back 101 years, no bridge.
- "interest rate, the price of borrowing": a squeezed-in definition, and the fifth kind of "price" in the film.
- A step is missing between "people spend less" and unemployment: businesses sell less, then hire fewer people (C2-XD01, C2-M17).

  - Who is Mattei? Chapter 2 never introduces her.
  - "That" could mean the falling prices or the lost jobs.
- "Ask for more" — more what? (Pay. Unsaid.)

**Page 10**
- The opening sentence is the longest in the film, at 30 words: a change of place, a squeezed-in definition, two numbers, a year and a reason. The Reserve Bank is finally explained, 40 seconds after it was first used.
- "a quarter of a percent" (E).
- B, E: two pairs of decimals back to back.
- Put next to the interest rate, the unemployment rise sounds wholly caused by it. The ledger says not to imply that (C2-XD05).
- "is raising its interest rate again"
  - The ledger asks for "has started raising" (C2-XD04).
  - Why is it rising? Fuel (C2-N12), but it's never linked to page 7.
- "Spent on what?" is the rule squeezed into three words. A newcomer who missed page 8 can't unpack it.

## 4. The main claim, and whether the script lets them say it

> **Creating new money only pushes up everyday prices when it's spent on things we can't make more of in a hurry, because the workers and machines are already flat out, or because homes can't be built fast; so the real question is "spent on what?"**

The last two pages add a follow-on: the usual cure, dearer borrowing, is paid for with people's jobs, and Mattei argues that's part of the point.

**Verdict: mostly no.** The rule is said in full only once, late, fast and as a concession. The tests come before it, two cases never say what they show, and "Spent on what?" can't be unpacked.

My predictions (untested):
- **S** gets "it depends whether the factories are busy", but probably not the homes half.
- **B** probably keeps "printing money causes inflation" (Germany is vivid, and Japan is a puzzle with no reason), but keeps "homes can't be built in a hurry" and "the cure costs jobs".
- **E** loses the thread at page 5 and keeps separate stories without the rule that joins them.

2. **Say the rule in plain words at the two factories, then repeat it word for word.**
   - New money *mostly* makes more things when workers and machines are idle, and higher prices when they're flat out, or when it's spent on things we can't make more of in a hurry, like homes (C2-XA03, C2-M09).
   - Repeat it after each test and just before "Spent on what?".

   - Cut or merge cases. The strongest candidates are the Bank of England after 2008 (it needs the most explaining and repeats Japan's point) and 1973 oil (no place named, and New Zealand's fuel figure makes the same "costs" point at home).
4. **Slow down: write for 140 words a minute.**
   - Plan the length from the arithmetic in section 1.
   - End each lesson on its own short sentence plus "...".
   - If the voice settings offer a slower speed, test it on one page first. I haven't checked whether they do.
5. **Say each example's lesson right after it.**

6. **Set up New Zealand's own story before judging it.**

   - Give the basket figure: seven point three percent by mid-2022 (C2-N01).
   - Say house prices aren't in the basket (C2-N08). Keep "in hindsight" (C2-XC04).
7. **One name for each thing.**
   - No "spending" or "support" for new money.
   - Whenever a "price" isn't in the shopping basket, say so or use another word.
   - Don't define the interest rate as "the price of borrowing".

   - "Mattei argues the lost jobs were part of the point" (C2-H18, C2-XD02).

   - "A loaf of bread cost billions" (C2-C03).
10. **Plain words; define first, then use.**
    - Replace the figures of speech and two-meaning words. "Flat out" can stay, explained once.
    - Split each squeezed-in definition into two sentences.
    - Break up the 25-to-30-word sentences on pages 5, 7, 8 and 10.

**Accuracy notes to carry into any rewrite:**
- Name America for "prices already climbing" in 1973 (C2-XC01).
- Keep "in hindsight" in the review line (C2-XC04).
- Say "has started raising" (C2-XD04) and don't imply the rate rises caused all of the unemployment rise (C2-XD05).

**A listener test to run once on real newcomers** (after one watch, no replay):
1. Does new money always push prices up? When does it, and when doesn't it?
2. Japan doubled the money its central bank had created. What happened to prices, and why?
3. *(The most useful question.)* Builders are out of work and are paid with new money to build houses. Do prices rise much? What if every builder is already busy?

## 3. What the viewer should be able to do afterwards
**Afterwards, the viewer can** say whether new money is likely to push prices up, and why, **when given**
a plain description of what it's being spent on (for example: paying people to build houses while every
builder is booked up, or while builders can't find work), **without** economics words.

Smaller goals:
- Tell a price rise from spending apart from one from a cost such as fuel.
- Say who pays for the usual cure (a higher interest rate), and tell the agreed part apart from Mattei's
  argument.

## 4. The central claim, in one sentence
Whether new money pushes prices up depends on what it's spent on: when there are spare workers,
materials and machines it mostly gets more made, and when they're all busy, or the money is chasing
things we can't make more of in a hurry, it mostly pushes prices up.

Stakes, said in the first 30 seconds: the usual cure works by making us spend less, and it costs some
people their jobs. [C2-M17, C2-XD01]

## 5. The chain of causes, one link at a time
1. New money only affects prices once it's spent. [C2-C06, C2-XB03]
2. Spending means orders for things and wages for the people making them. [framing of C2-M05]
3. With spare workers, materials and machines, the orders get filled: more is made, prices mostly hold.
   [C2-XA03, C2-M09]
4. With none spare, the orders can't be filled faster: more money meets the same things, so prices rise.
   [C2-XA03, C2-M05; Britain: C2-H01, H02, H03, XA01]
5. Some things can't be made more of in a hurry, such as homes. Money aimed at them mostly pushes their
   price up. [C2-M10 "in part", C2-N08, C2-XC03]
6. Unspent money pushes no shop prices up. In Japan, mainstream economists say much of it sat in the
   banks. [C2-XB03, XB04, C06]
7. Prices can also rise with no extra spending, when a cost in almost everything rises, such as fuel.
   [C2-M06, N05, XC05]
8. The usual cure: raise the interest rate (the price of borrowing). Loans cost more, people spend
   less, businesses hire fewer, and prices rise more slowly. [C2-XD01, M17]
9. The cost of the cure falls on people with loans and people who lose jobs. Central banks call that an
   unfortunate cost. Mattei argues it's how the cure works: fewer jobs, so workers can't ask for more.
   [C2-M17, H18]
10. So ask: spent on what? And who pays to stop it?

## 7. Wrong ideas viewers may bring, and how the chapter meets each
- **"Printing money always causes inflation."** True part: it can. Put Britain and Japan side by side,
  then the rule. Say "mostly", never "never". Say "new money", never "printing".
- **"The government is like a household."** One attributed sentence (C2-M12); don't argue it further.

## 9. The two factories: does the comparison hold?
**What it teaches:** whether extra spending becomes more things or higher prices depends on spare
workers, materials and machines.

**What maps to what:**
- factory → all of a country's businesses at one moment;
- workers at the gate → people who want work;
- still machines and piled materials → unused ones;
- coins → new money **spent**, as orders and wages;
- shells → things made;
- the price counter → prices in general;
- the quiet factory → spare room;
- the flat-out factory → Britain 1915, New Zealand late 2021.

**What it lets the viewer work out:** "Will this new money push prices up?" becomes "Is anything spare,
and what is it spent on?"

**Where it breaks, and the fix for each:**
1. Money only counts once it's spent. In Japan, most never reached a factory. Say the money arrives "as
   new orders", so Japan fits the picture.
2. An economy isn't one factory. Prices can rise before everything is busy (C2-M09): say "mostly".
3. The picture is instant; real room takes time to build. That's why homes get their own line.
4. Costs (fuel) push prices up in either factory. That's why page 10 is there.
5. The government was the only buyer of shells. Never say "buyers bid for shells". Say "more money, but
   no more things, so the price goes up".
6. It leaves out expectations and the pricing power of big firms (C2-M16, M07). Deferred.

**Verdict:** keep it. Name the two **the quiet factory** and **the flat-out factory**, and never change
the names.

## 1. What makes Naomi Klein recognisable (my own description)

This describes her public books, essays and talks in my own words. It quotes nothing of hers, on purpose.

1. **How she opens.** She puts you somewhere first: a named place, a date, a group of people, often a scene she saw as a reporter, or a news moment the reader lived through and still remembers. The big idea waits until you are standing in the scene. In talks she often begins by naming what the room is worried about that week, so the audience feels spoken to, not lectured.
2. **How she makes the stakes personal.** Early on she shows what the issue does to a home, a pay packet, a town or a child's future. The reader is treated as someone with something to lose, not a spectator. She uses "we" for the people who share the consequences, and she will put herself in the story (her doubts, her family) to close the distance.
3. **Who gains and who pays.** Her signature move: follow a decision to its results and set the winners and the losers side by side, by name where she can. She often puts the official reason for a policy next to what the policy actually did, and lets the gap make the argument.
4. **Concrete scenes, places and people.** Her arguments are built from things you could photograph: a building, a document, a queue, a price, a meeting and who was in the room. She likes to quote officials and experts in their own words, especially when their words give the game away.
5. **From a scene to a pattern.** One case is a story. She lines up a second and a third, from other places and decades, then names what links them, then brings the pattern home to the present. The pattern is the payoff.
6. **Rhetorical questions.** She asks the question the reader is already half-forming, or one that exposes a contradiction ("if that's true, why...?"), and then answers it. Questions mark the turns in her argument. In talks she often leaves the audience with a question to carry out the door.
7. **Rhythm.** A longer sentence that gathers detail, then a short one that lands. Lists of three. Fragments for emphasis. A key phrase repeated like a refrain, especially in speeches. Plain words, very little jargon, now and then a dry, ironic aside.
8. **Moral clarity.** She says plainly what she thinks is wrong, why, and who has the power to change it. She doesn't hide behind "some say". But she usually states the other side's case in its own terms before taking it apart, and she tends to end on agency: people who organised and won something, or a choice that is still open.
9. **Evidence and uncertainty.** Her books are heavily sourced, leaning on documents, statistics and interviews, and she uses numbers to make scale land. Her confidence is high: she states a thesis and drives it home. Some reviewers have argued that she sometimes draws the line from a crisis to a deliberate plan more firmly than the evidence allows. Whatever one thinks of that, it is the one move this film must never copy.
10. **How she sounds when she speaks.** Measured and conversational, rarely loud. The urgency is in what she says and how the argument moves, not in volume. That suits Malina exactly.

## 3. Do and don't

**Do**
- Open on something the viewer has felt, in the first sentence.
- Say why the answer matters to them in the first twenty seconds.
- Restate the question so someone who missed chapter 1 can follow.
- Give each case its own page, then one sentence on what it shows.
- Use active verbs with named actors: who raised the rent, who raised the interest rate.
- Put who gains and who pays side by side, each with its ledger id.
- Quote institutions' own findings only where the ledger has them, and keep their wording close.
- Ask the viewer's question, then answer it within a breath or two.
- Keep one idea and at most one number per sentence; round only as far as the ledger allows.
- Explain every needed term in a few plain words the first time, then use the same word every time.
- Mark Mattei's views every time: "Mattei argues", "she says", "Mattei sees it differently".
- Give the other side one fair line before making the point.
- Keep the honest edge: new money can push prices up when it chases things we can't make more of in a hurry.
- End on agency or a question the viewer can carry away, as long as the ledger supports it (Glasgow's tenants won a rent freeze).
- Read every page aloud at Malina's pace before it goes to the voice.

**Don't**
- Don't use her titles, coined phrases or near-copies (section 4). Never quote her or name her.
- Don't use the word "shock" at all, nor "disaster" or "doctrine".
- Don't claim intent the ledger doesn't hold ("on purpose", "engineered", "it's no accident"), except as Mattei's argument, said as hers.
- Don't say a rate rise caused a slump where the ledger says that's debated: say what followed.

## 6. Where her campaigning tone and the toolkit's plain style pull against each other

**What "classic style" means here:** the toolkit's word (Thomas and Turner's idea, which Pinker promotes) for writing that points an equal's attention at something true in the world, calmly, without showing off or arguing for a side. Klein's tone is campaigning: she argues, takes sides and asks you to care. The toolkit itself says not to force classic style wholesale onto campaigning writing. So the job is to keep both, with the facts in charge.

**Where they already agree** (why the blend works): both start from something concrete; both make the actors visible (who did what to whom); both treat the audience as intelligent equals; both cut jargon and throat-clearing; both use repetition on purpose (her refrain, the toolkit's one fixed name per thing).

**Where they pull apart, and how to keep both**

| Pull | Her tone wants | The plain style and the ledger want | How to keep both |
|---|---|---|---|
| Certainty | Firm, unhedged claims | "About", "up to", "partly", "can" kept | Get the confidence from concrete detail, not from dropping a qualifier. Put the qualifier early and small: "about twenty thousand households". |
| Intent | Outcomes read as aims: who benefited must have planned it | What followed, not what caused it, where cause is debated (ledger Traps on C2-H08) | Tell the sequence plainly; let Mattei carry the claim about intent, said as hers, with the other side in one line (passage 4). |
| Number of cases | Line up case after case for force | Few cases, each fully explained, with what it shows | One case per page. One sentence after each saying what it showed. The pattern repeated in the same words. |
| Labels | Vivid new names and slogans | One plain name per thing, never a synonym | Put the vividness in scenes and verbs, never in labels. No coined labels at all, which also keeps her phrases out. |
| Villains or mechanisms | Name the wrongdoers | Show how the thing works | Mechanism first (loans cost more, people spend less, businesses hire less), then who pays. Clear mechanism is the moral clarity. |
| Questions | Provocative, sometimes left hanging | Every question answered soon | Ask, then answer within a breath or two. Only the closing pair is left with the viewer. |
| "We" | "We" as the people fighting back | A calm guide talking to an equal | "We" only for shared New Zealand facts. No calls to act. Neutral before the 7 November election. |
| Irony | Dry asides | Words that mean what they say | Skip irony: on the ear, and in a synthetic voice, it may be heard literally. Use plain contrast instead. |
| Personal witness | "I was there" | A narrator with no life story | Malina shows; she never remembers. Real witnesses only from the ledger. |
| Ending | A call to action | A conclusion within what's known | End on a question the evidence earns ("Spent on what?"), or on people the ledger shows winning something (Glasgow). |

**The same line, three ways (too campaigning, too much like a lecture, both kept)**
- Intent. Too campaigning: "They threw people out of work on purpose." Both kept: "Within a year, unemployment jumps from about two in every hundred workers to eleven. /məˈteɪi/ argues that was part of the point." (C2-H08, C2-H17)
- Who gains. Too campaigning: "The new money went straight into the pockets of the rich." Both kept: "The Bank's own study says the gains went to the people who owned the most." (C2-XB05)
- House prices. Too campaigning: "All that new money sent house prices through the roof." Both kept: "House prices rose about thirty percent in twelve months. A Royal Commission found the pandemic response was part of the reason... World events played a big part too." (C2-XC03, C2-M10, C2-N14; house prices are not in the inflation figure, so keep them apart.)
- Then and now. Too campaigning: "It's nineteen twenty all over again." Both kept: "/məˈteɪi/ said in twenty twenty-three that the same pattern was back, in America and Europe." (C2-H19: she was talking about America and Europe, not New Zealand.)
- The cause of inflation. Too much like a lecture: "Demand-pull inflation occurs when aggregate demand exceeds productive capacity." Both kept: "When people try to buy more than the economy can make... prices climb." (C2-M05, C2-XA03)
- Fake urgency. Too campaigning and empty: "Make no mistake, this is a crisis." Both kept: "That's about a hundred and seventy thousand people, looking for work and not finding it." (C2-XD05)

**Order of priority when they clash**
1. The ledger: facts, numbers, qualifications, and who said what (Mattei's views as hers; the review's and the Bank's words as theirs).
2. Newcomer understanding: every term explained, one name per thing, one idea per sentence, pace.
3. Urgency in her spirit: stakes, who gains and who pays, concrete scenes, direct questions.
4. Rhythm and flourish.

If a line can't be both urgent and accurate, keep it accurate and find the urgency somewhere else: a number of people, a place, an institution's own words, all from the ledger.

## 2. How each case was rated

The ratings use three measures:
- **Ease for a newcomer** (5 = easiest): how many new places, years, kinds of money and terms the viewer has to take in.
- **Vivid** (5 = most vivid): people, objects and pictures you can see.
- **Explaining:** how much has to be said before the case makes sense.

The argument has eight steps:
- **S0** The question, and what inflation is.
- **S1** Creating money isn't the same as spending it.
- **S2** The rule: idle means more gets made; flat out means prices rise.
- **S3** Things we can't make more of in a hurry (homes).
- **S4** The far edge: new money while less and less is made.
- **S5** A different cause: costs, not spending.
- **S6** The honest edge here, in New Zealand.

## 3. The lean set, step by step, with the wording the ledger allows

The order follows the argument's logic, and the teaching plan may change it. The one fixed point: 3.2% must come before the ending reuses it.

**Step 1. The question and what inflation is (about 70 words)**
- "Inflation means prices in general going up." [XA04, M01. "In general" matters: one price, like petrol, isn't inflation on its own, though it feeds the average.]
- "The statistics office fills an imaginary shopping basket with what households usually buy... food, rent, petrol, power... and checks the price of that same basket every three months." [M02. No "2,800 shops".]
- Keep the opening dateless: "costs more than it did a year ago". "About four percent more" [XA05] goes out of date on 22 October.

**Step 2. Chapter 1's idea, for viewers who missed it (about 35 words)**
- "Economists who study how modern money works say a country that issues its own money can't run out of it. What it can run out of is workers, materials and machines." [M12. Said as theirs.]

**Step 3. Japan (about 75 words)**
- "In twenty thirteen, Japan's central bank, the bank that can create the country's money, set out to double the amount of money it had created... in two years." [XB03]
- "For about eight years, prices barely moved: about half a percent a year, on average." [XB04, C06]
  - The eight years are 2013 to 2021.
  - On screen it must read "ABOUT ½%". The plain yearly average is nearer 0.6 to 0.7%, and the Bank of Japan's own figure is 0.5%.
  - Safer wording that fits every figure: "less than one percent a year".
- "Partly because most of that new money just sat in the banks." [C06, XB03. Keep "partly": the ledger names other reasons too.]

**Step 4. Two factories (about 90 words)**
- "With idle workers and machines, extra spending mostly raises output. When everything is already running flat out, it mostly raises prices." [XA03]
- Keep "mostly", "barely move" or "tends to". Never "prices don't move". [M09: prices can start rising before everything is flat out.]
- Optional: "it's textbook economics, not a fringe idea" [XA03, M05].

**Step 5. Britain at war (about 75 words)**
- "Britain paid for the war partly with new money." [XA01, H03. Keep "partly".]
- "During the war years, fewer than one worker in a hundred was out of work." [H02. 1915 alone was only just under one percent, so say "the war years".]
- "Factories were turned over to war work, and German submarines sank ships carrying food and materials." [H03, XA02, second fact check. The worst sinkings were in 1917. Never the "40%" figure.]
- "More money, chasing fewer things to buy." [H03. This is a simplification.]
- "By the end of the war, everyday prices had roughly doubled." [H01]

**Step 6. Germany 1923 (about 75 words; first to cut)**
- "Prices were already rising fast." [XC02. The occupation didn't start it.]
- "French and Belgian soldiers marched into Germany's coal and steel heartland." [C02]
  - Dropping the name Ruhr removes a place and the sound-symbol word.
  - If page 6 is reused, its THE RUHR label must go too.
  - No 80% or 71% figures.
- "The government told workers to stop work in protest... and kept paying them, with new money." [C02, second fact check: a fair plain summary.]
- "Less and less was being made." [XC02. Don't say how much.]
- Use one number only:
  - My pick: "By November, one American dollar cost more than four trillion German marks." [C03. Checked twice, and it matches page 6's counter.]
  - Or: "A loaf of bread cost billions of marks." [C03's note. Easier for a newcomer, but never re-checked.]

**Step 7. New Zealand 2021 (about 95 words)**
- "By the end of twenty twenty-one, unemployment here was three point two percent... the lowest since records began in nineteen eighty-six." [N09. "Unsustainable", if used, is the review's word.]
- "House prices rose about thirty percent in the twelve months to the middle of twenty twenty-one." [XC03. This is the house price index. Over calendar 2021 the rise was nearer a quarter.]
- House prices aren't in the basket. Add one clause to keep them apart from inflation: "House prices aren't even in the basket... but they rose about thirty percent."
- If houses are tied to the pandemic response, it must be "in part": "A Royal Commission, an official inquiry, found the pandemic response was part of the reason, along with big forces from overseas." [M10, N14]
- "An independent review of the Reserve Bank said the support from the Reserve Bank and the government together was right at first... but, looking back, too strong for too long." [XC04, N15]
  - "Looking back" must stay.
  - The review doesn't discuss house prices, so its verdict must not sound like a verdict on houses.
  - No quotation marks on screen.
- Explain "the support" briefly: the Reserve Bank cut its interest rate to a quarter of a percent [XD03] and created new money [N06], and the government spent more [N14: a Covid fund of about $60 billion].

**Step 8. Costs, not spending (about 65 words)**
- "When fuel gets dearer, almost everything that's made or moved gets dearer too... whatever the government is spending. So sometimes the cause isn't spending at all. It's costs." [M06. Keep "sometimes".]
- "In the year to June twenty twenty-six, prices here rose four point one percent. If petrol and diesel prices hadn't changed, they'd have risen two point nine." [N05, M04, XA05, XC05, and Stats NZ's own wording]
  - For the ear: "about four percent... just under three".
- **Never "mostly fuel".** The sum is wrong: fuel adds about 1.2 of the 4.1 points.
- Don't say why fuel rose; it wasn't researched.
- Optional: petrol "up more than a quarter" (27.5%).

**Step 9. The cure and who pays: Britain 1920 (about 115 words)**
- "After the war, prices kept climbing." [H06, H01]
- How the cure works: "When the interest rate goes up, many mortgages and loans cost more. People spend more on those, and less on other things. When people spend less, businesses find it harder to raise prices... and hire fewer people." [XD01, M17. It works with a delay.]
- "In nineteen twenty, the Bank of England, Britain's central bank, pushed its interest rate up to seven percent, and held it there for about a year." [H07. It can also be said without the number: "held it high for about a year".]
- "Within a year, unemployment jumped from about two percent to about eleven." [H08]
  - Say what followed, not what caused it.
  - Never "then came the crash".
- "Prices did fall." [H09]
- The other side: "Central banks call the lost jobs a cost, not the goal." [M17]
- "Mattei argues that was part of the point. With fewer jobs to go round, she says, workers lose the power to ask for more." [XD02, H18: high confidence, her own words. H17 is medium, so backup only. Her reading is disputed by Glasner and Tomlinson.]
- Optional who-gains line: "Britain's central bank found its new money pushed up the prices of shares... and the richest five percent of households held about forty percent of those." [XB05]
  - "Shares" would need explaining.
  - "The biggest gains went to the richest" is an inference, not the study's finding.

**Step 10. Here and now (about 85 words)**
- "Here, the Reserve Bank cut its interest rate to a quarter of a percent in twenty twenty, then raised it to five and a half percent by twenty twenty-three." [XD03]
- "Since the end of twenty twenty-one, unemployment has climbed from three point two percent to five point six, the highest in about ten years." [N11, XD05]
- "That's about a hundred and seventy-one thousand people out of work." [XD05. That is the number out of work now, not how many more than in 2021; "tens of thousands more" has no source.]
- No "because" between the interest rate and unemployment. Unemployment kept rising while the interest rate was being cut [XD05], and cyclones also played a part in 2023 [N11].
- "In July and September this year, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again." [XD04. Past tense with months stays true after 28 October.]
- If the script questions raising the interest rate against a fuel price rise, it can only say that is debated [N12, M17]. Don't suggest fuel is the Bank's only reason [M18].
- End on: "Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?"

Terms to explain in a few plain words at first use:
- **New money:** one name all the way through. Never "printing money", "money creation" or "stimulus". A central bank "can create" money, since most money is created by ordinary banks lending [M15].
- **Central bank:** the bank that can create a country's money and sets its main interest rate.
- **Interest rate:** "the price of borrowing". Never bare "rates", because New Zealanders also pay council rates.
- **Unemployment:** "the share of people who want a job but don't have one".
- **Flat out / idle:** keep these two words; no synonyms.
- **The support:** as explained in step 7.

## 7. Facts in the lean set that are argued over or simplified

- **Britain at war** (H03, XA01): how much was shortage and how much was money is argued. Say "partly".
- **The two-factory rule** (XA03, M09): prices can rise before everything is flat out. Say "mostly".
- **Why Japan's prices stayed flat** (C06): there are several reasons. Say "partly".
- **Germany** (C02, C03, XC02):
  - It began before the occupation.
  - The protest wasn't strictly a general strike.
  - Nobody knows how much less was made.
  - Ending it also took spending cuts.
- **Costs** (M06, N05): costs and spending were tangled together in 2021-22. The 2.9% holds only petrol and diesel unchanged.
- **The review** (N15, XC04):
  - "Unsustainable" is the review's own judgement.
  - The government ordered it in an election year.
  - It was read through news quotes only.
- **House prices** (N08, XC03, M10): the causes are argued, and they aren't in the basket.
- **Britain 1920** (H08, XD02): whether the interest rate caused the slump is argued. "About two percent" depends on which series is used.
- **Mattei's reading** (H18, XD02): hers, and disputed.
- **Raising the interest rate against a fuel rise** (N12, M17, M18): debated.
- **Unemployment now** (XD05, N11): it also rose while the interest rate was being cut.
