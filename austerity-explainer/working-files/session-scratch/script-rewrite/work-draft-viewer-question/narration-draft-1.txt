## Page 1 - The checkout

You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. And you've heard the warning that comes with it. If the government creates new money... prices will go up. It sounds like common sense. But the answer matters. Because the usual cure for rising prices can put people out of work.

## Page 2 - The question

So here's the question. Does new money always push prices up? First, who creates it? In New Zealand, the Reserve Bank can. Economists who study how modern money works say a country like ours can't run out of its own money. What it can run out of... is workers, materials and machines. Hold on to that. Now... three tests.

## Page 3 - Test one: Britain at war

Test one. Britain, in the First World War. Almost everyone who could work had a job. Fewer than one worker in a hundred was out of work. Factories switched to making things for the war. German submarines sank ships bringing in food and materials. And the war was paid for partly with new money. So... more money, and fewer things to buy. What happened to prices? Before the answer... have a guess.

## Page 4 - Test one: the answer

By the end of the war, prices had roughly doubled. So far, the warning looks right. But look at what else was going on. Britain was short of workers, materials and machines.

## Page 5 - Test two: Japan

Test two. Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's version of our Reserve Bank set out to double the money it had created... in just two years. If the warning is right, prices should rise fast. What happened? Have a guess.

## Page 6 - Test two: the answer

Prices barely moved. For about eight years, they rose about half a percent a year. On a hundred-dollar basket, that's less than a dollar a year. So no... new money doesn't always push prices up. Why not? Economists give several reasons. A big one... most of that new money just sat in the banks. It wasn't spent. And money that isn't spent doesn't push up prices at the shops.

## Page 7 - The quiet factory

So when does new money push prices up... and when doesn't it? Picture two factories. In the first, the machines stand still. Workers wait at the gate for a job. Call it the quiet factory. New money gets spent there... as new orders. The workers go in. The machines start up. More gets made... and prices hardly move.

## Page 8 - The flat-out factory

Now the second factory. Every worker and every machine is already busy. Flat out. Call it the flat-out factory. The same new money arrives. But there are no spare workers, materials or machines. So there's more money... but no more things. And prices go up. Britain at war was the flat-out factory.

## Page 9 - The answer

So here's the answer. When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more made. When there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up. The same goes for things we can't make more of in a hurry... like homes. Mainstream economists agree on this much. It's not how much new money there is. It's what it's spent on.

## Page 10 - Test three: here

Test three... and this one's ours. Any country can be either factory, at different times. In twenty twenty, Covid hit. To hold the economy up, the Reserve Bank created new money... and the government spent more. By the end of twenty twenty-one, which factory was New Zealand? Have a guess.

## Page 11 - Test three: the answer

The flat-out one. By the end of twenty twenty-one, almost everyone who wanted a job had one. Only about three in every hundred couldn't find work. That number is called unemployment... three point two percent. The lowest since records began. This September, an independent review of the Reserve Bank came out. It says the support from the Reserve Bank and the government together was right at first. But looking back... too strong, for too long.

## Page 12 - Homes

And homes... something we can't make more of in a hurry? In the year to the middle of twenty twenty-one, house prices rose about thirty percent. House prices aren't in the shopping basket. But the Covid Royal Commission, an official inquiry, says the Covid response was part of the reason... along with big forces from overseas.

## Page 13 - Costs

And the prices you've felt at the checkout lately? In the year to June twenty twenty-six, they rose four point one percent. If petrol and diesel prices hadn't changed, the rise would have been two point nine. Fuel is one price tag. But when fuel costs more, almost everything that's made or moved costs more too... whatever the government is spending. So part of this rise isn't spending at all. It's a cost.

## Page 14 - The usual cure

So what's the usual cure? When prices rise too fast, the Reserve Bank raises its interest rate. Interest is the extra you pay back when you borrow. So many mortgages and loans cost more. People spend less on other things. Businesses sell less... and hire fewer people. And prices rise more slowly. Not fall... rise more slowly.

## Page 15 - The cure, here

After Covid, the Reserve Bank did just that. From late twenty twenty-one, it raised its interest rate again and again... from a quarter of a percent to five and a half. Later, it cut it back down. But this year, in July and September, it raised it again.

## Page 16 - Who pays

So who pays for the cure? People with mortgages and loans... and people who lose their jobs. Since the end of twenty twenty-one, unemployment has climbed from three point two percent to five point six. That's a hundred and seventy-one thousand people, looking for work and not finding it. Not all of that is down to the interest rate. But central banks expect the cure to put some people out of work. In their view, that's an unwanted side effect... not the goal.

## Page 17 - Mattei's view

The economist Clara E. /məˈteɪi/, whose book this series follows, sees it differently. She argues the cure works by putting people out of work. When jobs are hard to find, she says, workers find it harder to push for higher pay.

## Page 18 - Spent on what?

So... does new money push prices up? When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more made. When there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up. So next time someone tells you new money causes inflation... ask them two things. Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?
