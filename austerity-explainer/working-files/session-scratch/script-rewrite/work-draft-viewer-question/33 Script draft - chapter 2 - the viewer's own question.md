# 33 Script draft - chapter 2 - the viewer's own question

Source for the series: Clara E. Mattei, *The Capital Order* (2022).

**What this is:** a new narration for chapter 2 ("what really causes inflation"), written for people who
have never seen chapter 1 and have no economics background. It turns the claim viewers have heard
("new money causes inflation") into one question and answers it like a short investigation: three
tests, each with a "have a guess" moment and a plain answer. Every fact comes from
`chapter-2-inflation/18 Research - chapter 2 - what really causes inflation.md`, with its id. Nothing
outside this work folder was changed.

**Status: a draft.** Nobody has heard it yet: no listener, no fact checker, no rules reader, and
Malina hasn't recorded it. The narration alone, ready for the voice checker, is in
`narration-final.txt` beside this file.

**Length:** 862 spoken words in 16 pages (the recorded chapter: 689 words in 10 pages).
- By the brief's measure (about 137 words a minute, counting pauses): about **6 minutes 16 seconds**.
- At Malina's measured speed on chapter 2 (164 a minute), plus about 36 seconds of page turns,
  guess pauses and the closing fade: about **5 minutes 51 seconds**.
- At a strict 140 a minute plus the same gaps: about 6 minutes 45 seconds. That's the worst case.
  It's over the 6½-minute limit, and it's why the optional lines at the end are left out.

**The one question:** Does new money always push prices up?

**The sentence a viewer should be able to say back:** "New money mostly pushes prices up when
there's nothing spare to make more with. When there are spare workers, materials and machines, it
mostly gets more made. So the question is: spent on what?"

Written for Malina: "..." marks a pause, numbers are written as said, there are no capitals for
emphasis and no bracketed cues (the accent line goes before each page when recording), and Mattei is
written /məˈteɪi/. The heading of each page is not spoken.

## Page 1 - The checkout (about 26 s)

You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. And you've heard the warning. If the government creates new money... prices will go up. Getting this right matters... because the usual cure for rising prices can put people out of work.

- **Research:** C2-XA04 and C2-M01 (inflation is prices in general going up, measured on a basket);
  C2-XA05 (the basket costs more than a year earlier; kept without a date so it doesn't go stale);
  C2-M17 (the usual cure, a higher interest rate, softens the job market).
- **Explained here:** inflation ("prices in general, going up"; "not one price tag, the whole basket").
- **Pictures:** page-1, with its two parts swapped (code only). First the painted checkout: the
  basket, the tag A YEAR AGO $100.00 flipping to NOW $104.10 (labelled as example prices),
  INFLATION / PRICES IN GENERAL, GOING UP, the lone tag swinging, then THE WHOLE BASKET. Then a dip to
  the painted chamber for "the warning". The invented politician mouths it: NEW MONEY → PRICES UP?
  Then THE USUAL CURE: PEOPLE OUT OF WORK. Remove the brass shell that bridged to the old page 2.

## Page 2 - The question (about 22 s)

So here's the question. Does new money always push prices up? First, who creates it? In New Zealand, the Reserve Bank can. Economists who study how modern money works say a country like ours can't run out of its own money. What it can run out of... is workers, materials and machines. Hold on to that.

- **Research:** C2-N06 (the Reserve Bank created new money from 2020); C2-M15 (most money is created by
  ordinary banks lending, so "can", not "does"); C2-M12 (the view of economists who study how modern
  money works, said as theirs; it applies to countries that borrow in their own money, as New Zealand
  does, hence "a country like ours").
- **Explained here:** who creates new money. The chapter 1 idea is said fresh, so a newcomer doesn't
  need chapter 1, and a returning viewer hears it as a reminder.
- **Pictures:** the chamber holds, with the card DOES NEW MONEY ALWAYS PUSH PRICES UP?. Page-9's card
  bank, relabelled RESERVE BANK, pops up beside NEW MONEY. Then page-3's three painted cut-outs (a
  worker, materials, a lathe) rise under a card CAN'T RUN OUT OF ITS OWN MONEY / CAN RUN OUT OF:,
  labelled WORKERS / MATERIALS / MACHINES. Code only.

## Page 3 - Test one: Britain at war (about 25 s, then a 3-second hold)

Test one. Britain, in the First World War. Fewer than one worker in a hundred was out of work. Factories were busy with war work. German submarines sank ships bringing in food and materials. And the war was paid for partly with new money. So... more money, and fewer things to buy. What happened to prices? Before the answer... have a guess.

- **Research:** C2-H02 (unemployment under one percent through the war years; only just under in
  1915, so no single year is named); the second fact check (factories turned over to war work);
  C2-H03 and C2-XA02 (submarines sank ships carrying food and materials; the worst year was 1917,
  another reason not to say 1915); C2-XA01 ("partly" with new money); C2-H03 ("more money, fewer
  things to buy" is a simplification, so it stays plain).
- **Pictures:** page-2's munitions hall as built. Change the title from BRITAIN 1915 to TEST 1 ·
  BRITAIN, FIRST WORLD WAR. Keep the hundred dots (UNDER 1 IN 100), the camera on the benches for
  "busy with war work", the paper ships (SUPPLY SHIPS SUNK), the bundles of notes (NEW MONEY) and
  MORE MONEY / FEWER THINGS. End on a card, WHAT HAPPENED TO PRICES?, held about 3 seconds with the
  music up, and keep the ×2 bars hidden until page 4. A "..." is too short for thinking time, so the
  hold must be a real gap between recorded pages.

## Page 4 - Test one: the answer (about 11 s)

By the end of the war, prices had roughly doubled. So far, the warning seems right. But look closer. Britain was short of workers, materials and machines.

- **Research:** C2-H01 (prices roughly doubled by 1918); the second fact check confirms "short of
  workers, materials and machines" as a fair plain reading.
- **Pictures:** page-2's bars, 1914 ×1 and 1918 ABOUT ×2. Chapter 1's rubber stamp lands SO FAR: THE
  WARNING SEEMS RIGHT. Then the three cut-outs return under SHORT OF: WORKERS / MATERIALS / MACHINES.

## Page 5 - Test two: Japan (about 15 s, then a 3-second hold)

Test two. Japan, twenty thirteen. Its version of our Reserve Bank set out to double the money it had created... in two years. If the warning is right, prices should rise fast. What happened? Have a guess.

- **Research:** C2-XB03 (in 2013 the Bank of Japan set out to double the money it had created, within
  two years).
- **Explained here:** a central bank, as "its version of our Reserve Bank". The words "central bank"
  are never used in this script.
- **Pictures:** page-5 part A, the noodle counter, with the title TEST 2 · JAPAN 2013. The stack of
  invented notes doubles (×2 MONEY / IN TWO YEARS). The dashed line is relabelled IF THE WARNING IS
  RIGHT and climbs during the 3-second hold under WHAT HAPPENED?.

## Page 6 - Test two: the answer (about 22 s)

Prices barely moved. For roughly eight years, they rose about half a percent a year. So no... new money doesn't always push prices up. Why not? Mainstream economists give several reasons. A big one... most of that new money just sat in the banks. It wasn't spent... so it didn't push up prices at the shops.

- **Research:** C2-XB04 and C2-C06 (from 2013 to 2021, about half a percent a year; the second fact
  check calls this fair as narration); C2-C06 and C2-XB03 (the mainstream reasons: the money mostly
  sat as bank reserves, along with others. Hence "several reasons" and "a big one", said as the
  economists' view).
- **Pictures:** page-5 part A. The real line is drawn almost flat, ABOUT ½% A YEAR (show ½%, not
  0.5%). The ramen price tag never changes. New words, code only: NOT ALWAYS, then MOST SAT IN THE
  BANKS / NOT SPENT, credited MAINSTREAM ECONOMISTS. Page-5 part B (the Bank of England) is not used.

## Page 7 - The quiet factory (about 20 s)

So what made the difference? Picture two factories. In the first, the machines stand still. Workers wait at the gate for a job. Call it the quiet factory. New money gets spent there... as new orders. The workers go in. The machines start up. More gets made... and prices hardly move.

- **Research:** C2-XA03 (with idle workers and machines, extra spending mostly raises output);
  C2-M09 (prices can start rising before everything is busy, hence "hardly move", never "don't
  move"). The factories are announced as imagined ("Picture").
- **Explained here:** the quiet factory. The money arrives "as new orders", so the picture also
  covers Japan, where most of the money never became orders.
- **Pictures:** page-3's two-factory set (the Blender works), works A only. The machines are still
  and the workers wait at the gate, under the label THE QUIET FACTORY. Coins arrive labelled NEW
  ORDERS. The workers go in, the windows light, smoke rises and goods come out, while the price
  counter stays put.

## Page 8 - The flat-out factory (about 22 s)

Now the second factory. Every worker and every machine is already busy. Flat out. Call it the flat-out factory. The same new money arrives. But there are no spare workers, materials or machines. So there's more money... but no more things. And prices go up. Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory.

- **Research:** C2-XA03 and C2-M05 (when everything is running flat out, extra spending mostly raises
  prices); the second fact check ("close to the second factory", which is why it says "close to").
- **Explained here:** flat out ("every worker and every machine is already busy").
- **Pictures:** page-3's set, works B, labelled THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY. The same coins arrive, the row
  of goods doesn't grow and the price counter flips up: MORE MONEY / NO MORE THINGS. The three
  cut-outs rise under NO SPARE: WORKERS / MATERIALS / MACHINES, with a small tag BRITAIN AT WAR: CLOSE
  TO THIS ONE. Drop the old "buyers bid against each other" (in the war, the government was the only
  buyer of shells).

## Page 9 - The answer (about 26 s)

Here's the answer. When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more made. When there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up. The same goes for things we can't make more of in a hurry... like homes. Their prices mostly go up. Mainstream economists agree with this part. So it's not just how much new money there is. It's what it's spent on.

- **Research:** C2-XA03, C2-M05 and C2-M09 (the rule, with "mostly"; it's textbook economics); C2-M10
  (things we can't quickly make more of, as the film's general point); the second fact check (homes
  can't be built in a hurry).
- **Explained here:** things we can't make more of in a hurry. This is the first time the rule is
  said in full; page 16 repeats its first two sentences word for word.
- **Pictures:** page-8 part A: works A and B side by side, and the card house with its climbing tag
  (no figures). Three short lines build one at a time: SPARE → MORE MADE / NONE SPARE → PRICES UP /
  HOMES → PRICES UP, with the credit MAINSTREAM ECONOMISTS AGREE. Then IT'S WHAT IT'S SPENT ON. Remove
  YES, IT CAN.

## Page 10 - Test three: here (about 16 s, then a 3-second hold)

Test three. This one's ours. A country can be either factory. In twenty twenty, Covid hit. The Reserve Bank created new money... and the government spent more. By the end of twenty twenty-one, which factory was New Zealand? Have a guess.

- **Research:** C2-N06 (the Reserve Bank created new money from March 2020); C2-N14 and C2-XC04
  (government spending, and the combined Reserve Bank and government support).
- **What it checks:** whether the viewer can use the picture on a new case, rather than repeat it.
- **Pictures:** page-8 part B, the New Zealand street, with the title TEST 3 · NEW ZEALAND 2020. The
  card bank (RESERVE BANK) appears with NEW MONEY, and a card GOVERNMENT SPENDING. Then a wide shot of
  the two factory models side by side (page-3's set) with WHICH ONE?, held 3 seconds.

## Page 11 - Test three: the answer (about 22 s)

The flat-out one. Only about three in every hundred people who wanted a job couldn't find one. That's called unemployment... three point two percent. The lowest since records began. This September, an independent review said the support from the Reserve Bank and the government was right at first. But looking back... too strong, for too long.

- **Research:** C2-N09 (3.2% in the December 2021 quarter, the lowest since the survey began in
  1986); C2-N15 (the review says the economy "overheated to an historically extreme degree", which
  backs "the flat-out one"); C2-XC04 ("with the benefit of hindsight", the combined support was
  "appropriate in the early stages" but "too strong for too long". "Looking back" keeps the
  hindsight. The review doesn't discuss house prices, so none are placed near it).
- **Explained here:** unemployment, by the example first and then the name.
- **Pictures:** page-8 part B, with page-10's hundred-dot panel showing three empty dots, then 3.2%
  UNEMPLOYMENT / LOWEST SINCE RECORDS BEGAN (the small line "Records began in 1986", credited STATS
  NZ). Dip to page-8 part C, the reviewer's desk: the report INDEPENDENT REVIEW / RESERVE BANK, and
  the stamps RIGHT AT FIRST and TOO STRONG, FOR TOO LONG, credited INDEPENDENT REVIEW, SEPTEMBER
  2026, with no quotation marks. Leave out the card house and +30% (house prices aren't in this
  draft).

## Page 12 - Costs (about 22 s)

And prices at the checkout lately? In the year to June twenty twenty-six, they rose four point one percent. If petrol and diesel prices hadn't changed... they'd have risen two point nine. When fuel costs more, almost everything that's made or moved costs more. So the fuel part isn't about spending. It's a cost.

- **Research:** C2-N05 and C2-XA05 (4.1% in the year to the June 2026 quarter); C2-XC05 and the
  second fact check (Stats NZ's own wording: if petrol and diesel prices had not changed, 2.9%);
  C2-M06 (a cost like fuel pushes prices up whatever is being spent); the ledger's Traps ("the fuel
  part", never "mostly fuel": fuel adds about 1.2 of the 4.1 points).
- **Pictures:** a brief return to page-1's checkout basket (the callback), then page-7 part B, the New
  Zealand street. The bars read 4.1% PRICES and 2.9% IF PETROL AND DIESEL HADN'T CHANGED (relabelled
  from WITHOUT FUEL), credited STATS NZ, YEAR TO JUNE 2026. Page-7 part A's row of domino price tags
  is moved onto this street (code only) and flips up on "almost everything". Then NOT SPENDING (struck
  through) → A COST. The 1973 forecourt, oil barrel and $2.90 → $11.65 counter are not used.

## Page 13 - The usual cure (about 22 s)

So what's the usual cure? When prices rise too fast, the Reserve Bank raises its interest rate. Interest is the extra you pay back when you borrow. Then many mortgages and loans cost more. People spend less. Businesses sell less... and hire fewer people. And prices rise more slowly. Not fall... rise more slowly.

- **Research:** C2-XD01 (higher charges on many mortgages and loans; people spend less; prices rise
  more slowly); C2-M17 (businesses hire less).
- **Explained here:** interest ("the extra you pay back when you borrow") and the usual cure. It
  isn't called "the price of borrowing", because "price" in this film means shop prices. It's never
  bare "rates", which New Zealanders also pay to the council.
- **Pictures:** page-10 part A's New Zealand street at dusk. The card dial is relabelled INTEREST RATE
  and its needle rises with no figures. A card reads INTEREST = THE EXTRA YOU PAY BACK. From page-9
  part A (code only): the coin stack is pulled back to the card bank (LOANS COST MORE) and the shop
  light goes out (PEOPLE SPEND LESS). Then PRICES RISE MORE SLOWLY / NOT FALL. Remove BRITAIN 1920
  and the 6% → 7% meter.

## Page 14 - Who pays (about 27 s)

So who pays for the cure? People with mortgages and loans... and people who lose their jobs. From late twenty twenty-one, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again and again. Since then, unemployment has climbed from three point two percent to five point six. That's a hundred and seventy-one thousand people, looking for work and not finding it. Not all of that comes from the interest rate.

- **Research:** C2-XD01 and C2-M17 (who carries the cure); C2-N10 and C2-XD03 (rises from October 2021
  to May 2023); C2-N09, C2-N11 and C2-XD05 (3.2% to 5.6%; 171,000 people unemployed in the June 2026
  quarter, which is the total, not the increase). C2-XD05 also says unemployment kept rising while
  the interest rate was being cut, hence "not all of that".
- **Pictures:** page-10 part A. The dial rises in steps, captioned AGAIN AND AGAIN, with no figures
  because Malina no longer says them. The hundred-dot panel goes from 3.2% to 5.6%. Dip to page-9
  part B, the factory gate: the dole queue grows, with 171,000 PEOPLE LOOKING FOR WORK (credited STATS
  NZ, JUNE 2026 QUARTER) and a small line NOT ALL FROM THE INTEREST RATE. Remove the 1920 and 1921
  bars.

## Page 15 - Two views (about 24 s)

The people who set interest rates say lost jobs are an unwanted side effect... not the goal. The economist Clara E. /məˈteɪi/, whose book this series follows, sees it differently. She argues putting people out of work is how the cure works. When jobs are hard to find, she says, workers have less power to push for higher pay.

- **Research:** C2-M17 (central banks treat job losses as an "unfortunate cost", not a goal; the
  Fed's chair expected "some pain"); C2-H18 (Mattei's own words: higher interest rates "cool down the
  labor market"; fewer jobs "diminish the bargaining power of workers"). Her view is marked twice
  ("she argues", "she says"). It's her general argument, not something she said about New Zealand
  (C2-H19). C2-XD02 is a backup only.
- **Explained here:** who Mattei is (an economist, whose book this series follows).
- **Pictures:** page-9 part B. First a typed card: THE PEOPLE WHO SET INTEREST RATES: AN UNWANTED SIDE
  EFFECT. Then her book with its credit (CLARA E. MATTEI, THE CAPITAL ORDER, 2022) and MATTEI ARGUES.
  On "less power to push for higher pay", the iron weight drops onto the pay packet, captioned LESS
  POWER TO PUSH FOR HIGHER PAY. No portrait.

## Page 16 - Spent on what? (about 22 s, then the 4-second fade)

Back to the question. Does new money always push prices up? No. When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more made. When there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up. So next time you hear that new money causes inflation... ask two questions. Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?

- **Research:** C2-XA03 and C2-M09 (the rule, repeated).
- **Pictures:** page-10 part B, the chamber: the invented politician, the rule as two short lines
  (SPARE → MORE MADE / NONE SPARE → PRICES UP), then SPENT ON WHAT? and WHO PAYS TO STOP IT?, then
  the fade. No party colours.

## How the draft teaches

- **The order:**
  1. Something the viewer has lived (the checkout) and a claim they've heard (the warning).
  2. Why it matters to them (the cure can put people out of work).
  3. One question.
  4. Two real tests that disagree: Britain (prices doubled) and Japan (prices barely moved).
  5. The two factories, which explain both tests.
  6. The rule.
  7. A third test at home, where the viewer uses the picture themselves.
  8. A second cause (costs).
  9. The cure and who pays.
  10. The question to carry away.
- **The two factories come after the tests,** so they answer a puzzle the viewer is already holding
  ("so what made the difference?") instead of arriving as a lecture.
- **Three guesses,** one at the end of pages 3, 5 and 10. Each is followed by a plain answer: "So far,
  the warning seems right." / "So no... new money doesn't always push prices up." / "The flat-out
  one." The brief asked for "before I tell you" moments; they're written "Before the answer... have a
  guess" and "Have a guess", because the voice brief says Malina never says "I".
- **Repeated in the same words each time:**
  - "workers, materials and machines" (pages 2, 4, 8, 9 and 16);
  - the rule (page 9, then page 16 word for word);
  - "It's what it's spent on" (page 9), which becomes "Spent on what?" (page 16).
- **One plain name for each thing:** new money (always created, never printed); the Reserve Bank
  (Japan's is "its version of our Reserve Bank"); the warning; the quiet factory and the flat-out
  factory; workers, materials and machines; things we can't make more of in a hurry; unemployment;
  interest rate; the usual cure.
- **Load:** three places, with time running one way (the First World War, 2013, 2020 to 2021, now;
  the cure covers late 2021 to now). There are 18 spoken figures, against about 37 in the recording.
  Sentences average 7.7 words and the longest is 19. Each page has one idea and 11 to 27 seconds of
  speech.

## Read-aloud and anti-banality changes (between drafts 1 and 4)

- **Lines that sounded empty, now specific or cut:**
  - "But the answer matters" became "Getting this right matters... because..." with the stake named.
  - "It sounds like common sense" was cut (a stock phrase, and already used in chapter 1).
  - "To keep the economy going" was cut, because the facts carry it.
  - "Fuel is one price tag" was cut, because it only repeated page 1.
- **"Cost" and "pay" are used only literally,** so the chapter's own word for a cause like fuel ("a
  cost") stays clear:
  - "can cost people their jobs" became "can put people out of work";
  - "an unfortunate cost" became "an unwanted side effect... not the goal";
  - "workers lose the power to ask for more" became "workers have less power to push for higher pay"
    (higher pay is what she means, C2-H18).
- **Claims softened to match the ledger:**
  - "It's not how much new money there is" became "not just how much";
  - "Britain at war was the flat-out factory" became "close to";
  - Japan's reason is now "Mainstream economists give several reasons. A big one...";
  - "Leave fuel out" became Stats NZ's "if petrol and diesel prices hadn't changed".
- **Lines that tripped when read aloud:**
  - "So many mortgages..." could be heard as "such a lot of mortgages", so it's now "Then many
    mortgages...".
  - "Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's version..." became "Its version...".
  - "The same goes for... homes" now says "Their prices mostly go up", so "the same" can't be taken
    to mean both earlier sentences.
- Length came down from 1,049 words to 862.

## A listener test (run once, before recording)

Read the script aloud to one or two of the people who got lost, with no replay, then ask:
1. Does new money always push prices up? When does it, and when doesn't it?
2. Britain and Japan both created new money. Why did prices double in one and barely move in the
   other?
3. *(The most useful one.)* The government creates new money to pay builders to build houses. Will it
   push prices up? What would you want to know first?
4. Prices rose four point one percent, and two point nine without the change in petrol and diesel.
   Was that new money?
5. Who pays when the interest rate goes up? Is "it works by putting people out of work" a fact, or
   someone's argument? Whose?

If question 1 passes but question 3 fails, the rule was memorised, not understood: strengthen pages
7 to 10. If question 5 comes back as plain fact, make "she argues" louder on page 15.

## Traps

- **Re-check before recording, and tie each figure to its date:**
  - 4.1% and 2.9% (new prices out on 22 October 2026);
  - 5.6% and 171,000 (new unemployment figures on 4 November 2026).
  - The Reserve Bank decides on 28 October. Nothing in this draft depends on that, because the
    "raised again" line is left out.
- "This September" (the review) and "In the year to June twenty twenty-six" go stale in 2027. Say
  "In September twenty twenty-six" if the film will be watched next year.
- **Say:**
  - "not all of that comes from the interest rate", and never "because" between the interest rate and
    today's unemployment;
  - "the fuel part", never "mostly fuel";
  - "can", never that the Reserve Bank creates all money.
- 171,000 is everyone looking for work now, not the increase since 2021.
- The general election is on 7 November 2026: name no party, minister or "this government", and say
  nothing about who ordered the review. The politician in the chamber is invented and wears no party
  colours.
- Words on screen are short forms of what Malina says, never more. Where she no longer says a figure
  (the interest rate's 0.25% to 5.5%, Britain's 1915), it comes off the page too.
- Malina never says "I", and Mattei's view is always "she argues" or "she says".
