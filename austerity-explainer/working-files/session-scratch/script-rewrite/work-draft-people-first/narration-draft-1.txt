## 1 - The question
You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. And you've heard the warning. Create new money, and prices will rise. Is that true? It matters... because the usual cure for rising prices can cost people their jobs.

## 2 - Where new money comes from
So where does new money come from? In New Zealand, the Reserve Bank can create it. Economists who study how modern money works say this. A country that makes its own money can't run out of it. What it can run out of is workers, materials and machines. Remember those three.

## 3 - Glasgow, nineteen fifteen
To see a country run short of workers, go to Glasgow, in Scotland... in nineteen fifteen. Britain is fighting the First World War. Crowds of workers come to the city to make shells for the war. Through the war years, almost everyone who wants a job has one. Fewer than one in every hundred is out of work.

## 4 - More money, no more things
The war is paid for partly with new money. But there's almost no one spare to hire. So there's more money... but no more things to buy. And prices go up. By the end of the war, prices in Britain have roughly doubled.

## 5 - Homes
And the workers need somewhere to live. In Glasgow, homes are already short. You can't build a house in a hurry... and the builders and materials have gone to the war. So landlords raise the rent. In some parts of the city, by up to a quarter. More people... the same number of homes.

## 6 - The families say no
The families renting those homes say no. Led by women like Mary Barbour, they keep paying the old rent... and refuse the rise. By November, about twenty thousand families are refusing. Within weeks, Britain's Parliament passes a law. Rents on small homes go back to what they were before the war.

## 7 - The quiet factory
Here's a simple way to picture it. Imagine two factories. The first is the quiet factory. Its machines stand still, and workers wait at the gate. Then new money is spent on what it makes. The workers go in. The machines start. More gets made... and prices barely move.

## 8 - The flat-out factory
The second is the flat-out factory. Every worker and every machine is already busy. The same new money is spent there. So what happens now? Have a guess.

## 9 - The rule
Nothing more can be made. There's more money... but no more things to buy. So prices go up. Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory. So here's the rule. When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more made. When there aren't, or when it's spent on things we can't make more of in a hurry, like homes... it mostly pushes prices up.

## 10 - Japan, the test
So does new money always push prices up? Test it. Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's version of our Reserve Bank sets out to double the money it has created... in just two years. If new money always pushed prices up... what would you expect? Have a guess.

## 11 - Japan, the answer
For about eight years, prices barely move. About half a percent a year. Economists give a few reasons. One is that most of the new money just sits in the banks. It isn't spent in the shops. So it's not how much new money there is. It's whether it's spent... and on what.

## 12 - Here, twenty twenty
Now, here. In twenty twenty, Covid arrived. The Reserve Bank created new money, and the government spent billions, including the wage subsidy. This year, two outside experts reviewed what the Reserve Bank did. Their review says that support, from the bank and the government together, was right at first.

## 13 - The flat-out factory, at home
But by the end of twenty twenty-one, just three point two percent of people who wanted work were out of work. The lowest since records began, in nineteen eighty-six. New Zealand was close to the flat-out factory. And looking back, the review says the support was too strong, for too long.

## 14 - Homes, here
And homes? House prices aren't in the shopping basket. But in the twelve months to the middle of twenty twenty-one, they rose about thirty percent. A Royal Commission, an official inquiry, found the Covid response was part of the reason. It also found the gap between people with wealth and people without grew wider.

## 15 - The usual cure
So how are rising prices brought down? The usual cure is for the Reserve Bank to raise its interest rate. An interest rate is the extra you pay to borrow money. When it goes up, many mortgages and loans cost more. People spend more on those, and less on other things. So businesses sell less. They find it harder to raise prices... and they hire fewer people.

## 16 - Britain, nineteen twenty
To see who pays for that cure, go back to Britain, in nineteen twenty. The war is over, but prices are still climbing. So the Bank of England, Britain's version of our Reserve Bank, raises its interest rate to seven percent... and holds it there for about a year. Prices do fall. But within a year, the number out of work jumps from about two in every hundred... to more than one in ten. Historians still argue over how much of that the interest rate caused.

## 17 - Who pays
Banks like our Reserve Bank say lost jobs are a cost of the cure... not the goal. The economist Clara E. /məˈteɪi/, whose book this series follows, sees it differently. She argues that in nineteen twenty, the lost jobs were part of the point. When jobs are hard to find, she says, workers lose the power to ask for more pay.

## 18 - And here, now
And here? After Covid, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate from a quarter of a percent... to five and a half percent by twenty twenty-three. Since the end of twenty twenty-one, the percentage out of work has climbed from three point two... to five point six. That's about a hundred and seventy-one thousand people, looking for work and not finding it. Not all of that is because of the interest rate. And this year, the Reserve Bank has raised its interest rate again.

## 19 - Spent on what?
So next time you hear that new money makes prices rise... remember the two factories. When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more made. When there aren't, or when it's spent on things we can't make more of in a hurry, like homes... it mostly pushes prices up. So ask two questions. Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?
