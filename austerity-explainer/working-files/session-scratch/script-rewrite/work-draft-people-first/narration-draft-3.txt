## 1 - The question
You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. And you've heard the warning. Create new money, and prices will rise. Is that true? It matters... because the usual cure for rising prices is to make borrowing cost more. And that can put people out of work.

## 2 - Where new money comes from
So where does new money come from? In New Zealand, the Reserve Bank can create it. Economists who study how modern money works say this. A country that makes its own money can't run out of it. But it can run out of workers, materials and machines. Remember those three.

## 3 - Glasgow, nineteen fifteen
To see a country run short of workers, go to Glasgow, in Scotland... in nineteen fifteen. Britain is fighting the First World War. Crowds of workers come to the city to make shells for the army's guns. Across Britain, fewer than one in every hundred people is out of work. The war is paid for partly with new money. But with almost no one spare to hire, no more can be made. By the end of the war, prices in Britain have roughly doubled.

## 4 - The families say no
The workers need homes too. But Glasgow's homes were already short. And you can't build a house in a hurry... the builders and materials have gone to the war. So landlords raise the rent, in some parts of the city by up to a quarter. The families renting those homes say no. Led by women like Mary Barbour, they keep paying the old rent... and refuse the rise. By November, about twenty thousand families are refusing. Within weeks, Britain's Parliament brings rents on small homes back to what they were before the war.

## 5 - Two factories
Picture two factories. The first is the quiet factory. Its machines stand still, and workers wait at the gate. Then new money is spent on what it makes. The workers go in, and more gets made. Prices barely move. The second is the flat-out factory. Every worker and every machine is already busy. The same new money is spent there. What happens now? Have a guess.

## 6 - The rule
Nothing more can be made. There's more money... but no more things to buy. So prices go up. Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory. So were Glasgow's homes. So here's the rule. If there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more made. If there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up.

## 7 - Japan, the guess
Now test the warning. Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's version of our Reserve Bank sets out to double the money it has created... in just two years. If new money always pushed prices up, what would you expect? Have a guess.

## 8 - Japan, the answer
For about eight years, prices barely move. About half a percent a year. Economists give a few reasons. One is that most of the new money just sits in the banks. It isn't spent. So it's not how much new money there is. It's whether it's spent... and on what.

## 9 - Here, twenty twenty
Now, here. In twenty twenty, Covid arrived. The Reserve Bank created new money, and the government spent billions, including the wage subsidy. This year, two outside experts reviewed what the Reserve Bank did. They say all that support was right at first. But by the end of twenty twenty-one, just three point two percent of people wanting work were out of work. The lowest since records began, in nineteen eighty-six. New Zealand was close to the flat-out factory. Looking back, the review says, the support was too strong, for too long.

## 10 - The usual cure
So how are rising prices brought down? To see the usual cure, and who pays for it, go back to Britain, in nineteen twenty. The war is over, but prices are still climbing. So the Bank of England, Britain's version of our Reserve Bank, raises its interest rate. That's the extra you pay to borrow money. It goes up to seven percent.

## 11 - Who pays, then
Loans cost more. People spend less. Businesses sell less, so they hire fewer people. Prices do fall. But within a year, many more people are out of work. Not about two in every hundred... but more than one in ten. Historians still argue over how much of that the interest rate caused.

## 12 - Mattei's argument
Banks like our Reserve Bank call lost jobs a cost, not the goal. The economist Clara E. /məˈteɪi/, whose book this series follows, sees it differently. She argues that in nineteen twenty, the lost jobs were part of the point. When jobs are hard to find, she says, workers lose the power to ask for more pay.

## 13 - Who pays, now
And here? To bring prices down, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate. From a quarter of a percent... to five and a half percent by twenty twenty-three. Since late twenty twenty-one, the percentage out of work has climbed from three point two to five point six. That's about a hundred and seventy thousand people, looking for work and not finding it. Not all of that is because of the interest rate. And this year, the Reserve Bank has raised its interest rate again.

## 14 - Spent on what?
So next time you hear that new money makes prices rise... remember the two factories. If there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more made. If there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up. So ask two questions. Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?
