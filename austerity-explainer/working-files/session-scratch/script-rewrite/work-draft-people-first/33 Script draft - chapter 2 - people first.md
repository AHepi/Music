# 33 Script draft - chapter 2 - people first

Source for the series: Clara E. Mattei, *The Capital Order* (2022). Every fact below is a row in
`chapter-2-inflation/18 Research - chapter 2 - what really causes inflation.md`, named by its id. No
new facts were added.

**What this is:** a complete new narration for chapter 2 ("what really causes inflation"), written
for newcomers who never saw chapter 1. It is built around people: the Glasgow families facing a
rent rise in 1915, the workers who lost their jobs in Britain after 1920, and New Zealanders from
Covid to now. The mechanism is taught through their stories. It is a draft. Nobody has heard it,
Malina hasn't read it, and no second fact checker has read it.

The narration alone, exactly as it would be sent to the voice (the accent line is added later), is
in `narration-final.txt` in this folder.

## The one sentence a viewer should be able to say back

Whether new money pushes prices up depends on what it's spent on. If there are spare workers,
materials and machines, it mostly gets more made. If there aren't, it mostly pushes prices up. And
the usual cure, making borrowing cost more, is paid for by people who lose their jobs.

## Length and pace

- **866 spoken words, 14 pages.** The recorded chapter had 689 words on 10 pages.
- **At 140 words a minute** (the project's planning speed), the speech is about 6 minutes 11
  seconds. With the two 3-second "have a guess" holds, it's about 6 minutes 17 seconds.
- **At Malina's measured speed** (164 words a minute in the recording), with 13 page turns of about
  1.5 seconds, the two holds and the fade, it's about 5 minutes 47 seconds.
- **If Malina is slowed to 140 and the page turns are added on top,** it's about 6 minutes 41
  seconds. That's just over the 6.5-minute ceiling. The ready cuts near the end take off about 35
  words if needed.
- **One new idea every 25 to 30 seconds.** Pages run 19 to 39 seconds at 140 words a minute.
- **Sentences are short.** None is over 20 words, and most are 6 to 12.
- **About 24 spoken numbers,** against about 37 in the recording. Each is said as people or with a
  plain anchor ("one worker in every hundred", "about a hundred and seventy thousand people").
- **Checked with** `work-voice-brief/voice_check.py`: no never-use phrases, digits, capitals,
  brackets or exclamation marks. Mattei is written in sound symbols, with "she argues" and "she says"
  beside her name.

## How it teaches

- **Three groups of people carry the chapter.** The film tells, in order:
  1. what makes prices rise: Glasgow, 1915;
  2. a test: Japan;
  3. the same thing at home: New Zealand, 2020–21;
  4. what the cure does, and who pays: Britain, 1920, then New Zealand now.
- **Time runs one way except once.** The jump back to 1920 is announced with its reason: "To see
  the usual cure, and who pays for it, go back to Britain... nineteen twenty."
- **The toolkit's order** (something the viewer has seen, then a case, then the idea, then a test,
  then the stakes):
  1. The checkout: inflation in plain words, and the stakes. The cure can put people out of work.
  2. Chapter 1's idea, in two sentences, for anyone who missed it.
  3. A real case: Glasgow at war. There's new money, no one spare to hire, and prices double. Homes
     can't be built in a hurry.
  4. The idea as a picture: the quiet factory and the flat-out factory. The viewer guesses the
     answer before it's given.
  5. The rule, said in plain words: "If there are spare workers, materials and machines... new
     money mostly gets more made. If there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up."
  6. A test: Japan doubled its new money. The viewer guesses first. Prices barely moved, and one
     reason economists give is that the money sat in the banks. Short recap: "It's whether it's
     spent... and on what."
  7. The rule used at home, in New Zealand 2020–21: the review's "right at first... too strong, for
     too long".
  8. The stakes: the usual cure, how it works (loans, then spending, then hiring), and who paid in
     1920. Mattei's argument is said as hers, after the central banks' own view.
  9. New Zealand now: about a hundred and seventy thousand people out of work, said next to the
     interest rate, but not blamed on it. The ending repeats the rule word for word, then the two
     questions.
- **One plain name for each thing:**
  - new money;
  - the Reserve Bank (other countries get "their version of our Reserve Bank", so the term "central
    bank" is never needed);
  - out of work (the word "unemployment" is never used);
  - the usual cure;
  - interest rate (never bare "rates");
  - the quiet factory and the flat-out factory;
  - spare;
  - workers, materials and machines (always in that order).
- **Klein in spirit only:**
  - the stakes come in the first half-minute;
  - the viewer's own questions are asked, then answered;
  - who gained and who paid sit side by side: landlords and families, the cure and the people who
    lost jobs;
  - people win something: the Glasgow families get rents brought back down;
  - urgency comes from facts and people counts, not loud words.
  - None of her titles, phrases or near-copies appear, and the word "shock" doesn't either.

## Page by page

### 1. The question (68 words, about 29 s)

> You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago.
> That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. And you've
> heard the warning. Create new money, and prices will rise. Is that true? It matters... because
> the usual cure is to make borrowing cost more. And that can put people out of work.

- Research: C2-XA04, C2-M01 (inflation is prices in general going up, measured on a basket);
  C2-XA05 (the basket costs more than a year earlier; the wording carries no number, so it doesn't go
  out of date); C2-M17, C2-XD01 (the usual cure is higher interest rates: loans cost more, and
  businesses hire less).
- Terms: inflation; the usual cure (said here as "make borrowing cost more", named on page 10).
- Pictures: page-1's checkout (basket, example tag A YEAR AGO $100.00 to NOW $104.10, PRICES IN
  GENERAL, GOING UP, THE WHOLE BASKET). Then a dip to page-1's debating chamber: the invented
  politician mouths the warning, NEW MONEY, then PRICES UP? The order is swapped from the recording.
  Remove the brass-shell bridge, since "go back to the shell factory" is gone. New words only:
  BORROWING COSTS MORE, then OUT OF WORK.

### 2. Where new money comes from (53 words, about 23 s)

> Where does new money come from? In New Zealand, the Reserve Bank can create it. It's the bank in
> charge of the country's money. Economists who study how modern money works say a country like
> ours can't run out of its own money. But it can run out of workers, materials and machines.

- Research: C2-N06 (the Reserve Bank created new money from 2020); C2-M15 ("can": most money is
  created by ordinary banks lending); C2-M12 (said as the view of economists who study how modern
  money works; it applies to countries that borrow in their own money, as New Zealand does).
- Terms: new money; the Reserve Bank; workers, materials and machines.
- Pictures: stay in page-1's chamber. A typed card reads THE RESERVE BANK CAN CREATE IT. Then a card
  in the style of chapter 1's page 8: ECONOMISTS WHO STUDY MODERN MONEY: CAN'T RUN OUT OF ITS OWN
  MONEY / CAN RUN OUT OF... Then page-3's three painted cut-outs pop up (a worker, materials, a
  lathe) with WORKERS, MATERIALS, MACHINES. These are existing pieces placed in code.

### 3. Glasgow, nineteen fifteen (75 words, about 32 s)

> To see that happen, go to Glasgow, in Scotland... nineteen fifteen. Britain is fighting the First
> World War. Crowds of workers come to make shells for the war. Across Britain, fewer than one
> worker in every hundred is out of work. The war is paid for partly with new money. But with almost
> no one spare to hire, factories can't make much more. By the end of the war, prices in Britain
> have roughly doubled.

- Research:
  - C2-XB01: crowds of workers came to Glasgow's munitions industry.
  - C2-H02: under one percent out of work in every war year, 1915 to 1918.
  - C2-XA01, C2-H03: the war was paid for "partly" with new money.
  - Second fact check, 12 October: "Britain at war was close to the second factory... short of
    workers, materials and machines" was confirmed.
  - C2-H01: prices roughly doubled by the end of the war.
- Terms: the First World War (named); out of work, with a percentage taught as "in every hundred";
  spare.
- Pictures: page-2's munitions hall. Change the title to GLASGOW 1915 / BRITAIN AT WAR. The munition
  women rise in the foreground. The 100-dot panel stays, with the last dot nearly empty, relabelled
  FEWER THAN 1 IN 100 OUT OF WORK. Bundles of notes land (PARTLY NEW MONEY), then NO ONE SPARE TO
  HIRE, then the bars ×1 (1914) and ×2 (1918). Take out the sinking supply ships and MORE MONEY /
  FEWER THINGS, because the narration no longer says them. The changes are all in code.

### 4. The families say no (91 words, about 39 s)

> The workers need homes. But Glasgow already has too few. You can't build homes in a hurry... not
> with the builders and materials gone to war. So landlords raise the rent, in some parts of the
> city by up to a quarter. The families who rent say no. Led by women like Mary Barbour, they keep
> paying the old rent... and refuse to pay any more. By November, about twenty thousand families are
> refusing. Within weeks, Britain's Parliament brings rents on small homes back to what they were
> before the war.

- Research:
  - C2-XB01: homes were already short; rises of "up to" a quarter "in some districts"; strikers paid
    the old rent and refused the increase; about twenty thousand by November.
  - C2-H04: tenants were led by the Glasgow Women's Housing Association and Mary Barbour; about
    twenty thousand families.
  - Second fact check: homes can't be built in a hurry, because labour and materials went to the
    war.
  - C2-XB02 and the second fact check: the 1915 law held the rents of small homes at their pre-war
    level, and "within weeks" counts from November.
- Terms: landlords; rent; Britain's Parliament (so nobody pictures Wellington).
- Pictures: page-4's Glasgow tenement street at dusk.
  - Files of workers stream in.
  - On "You can't build homes in a hurry", the existing building-site shot (timber frame, brick
    courses rising) moves to here from the end of the page, with HOMES CAN'T BE BUILT IN A HURRY.
  - The notice RENT RAISED / UP TO +25% slaps on. The strikers with placards pop up.
  - The counter reaches 20,000, relabelled FAMILIES REFUSING.
  - New: a typed name card, MARY BARBOUR / LED THE RENT STRIKE. It's a name card only, with no
    portrait.
  - The stamp's words change from RENTS FROZEN to RENTS BACK TO BEFORE THE WAR, to match the
    narration.

### 5. Two factories (65 words, about 28 s, then a 3-second hold)

> Picture two factories. The first is the quiet factory. Its machines stand still, and workers wait
> at the gate. Then new money is spent on what it makes. The workers go in, and more gets made.
> Prices barely move. The second is the flat-out factory. Every worker and machine is already busy.
> The same new money is spent there. What happens now? Have a guess.

- Research: C2-XA03 (spare room means extra spending mostly raises output); C2-M09 (prices can start
  rising before everything is busy, hence "barely" and, on page 6, "mostly"); C2-M05.
- Terms: the quiet factory and the flat-out factory, each defined by what you see. "Spent" makes the
  money arrive as spending, so Japan fits the same picture later.
- Pictures: page-3's two-factories set.
  - Works A: the queue, coins raining in, the workers going in, windows lighting, shells coming out,
    the price counter steady.
  - Then works B: coins rain in. Freeze before its counter moves, and hold about 3 seconds on WHAT
    HAPPENS NOW? This page ends there, so the hold is a real silence. A "..." is not long enough.
  - Labels: THE QUIET FACTORY and THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY.

### 6. The rule (57 words, about 24 s)

> Nothing more can be made. There's more money... but no more things to buy. And prices go up.
> Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory. And so were Glasgow's homes. Here's the rule. If
> there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more made. If there
> aren't... it mostly pushes prices up.

- Research: C2-XA03, C2-M09 ("mostly"); the second fact check (Britain close to the flat-out
  factory); C2-XB01 and C2-M10 (the film's general point about things we can't make more of in a
  hurry).
- Terms: the rule, in the chapter's fixed words. The same words come back on page 14.
- Pictures:
  - Page-3's works B: the counter flips up. MORE MONEY, NO MORE THINGS.
  - Page-8 part A's card house pops up beside works B, its tag climbing, with no figures: "And so
    were Glasgow's homes".
  - The rule on two cards: SPARE → MORE MADE / NONE SPARE → PRICES UP.

### 7. Japan, the guess (39 words, about 17 s, then a 3-second hold)

> Now test the warning. Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's version of our Reserve Bank sets out to
> double the money it has created... in two years. If new money always pushed prices up, what would
> you expect? Have a guess.

- Research: C2-XB03 (double the money the central bank had created, within two years); C2-C06.
- Terms: Japan's version of our Reserve Bank.
- Pictures: page-5 part A, the noodle counter at night. JAPAN 2013. The stack of notes doubles, with
  ×2 MONEY / IN TWO YEARS. During the hold, the dashed IF MONEY ALONE line climbs, with WHAT WOULD YOU
  EXPECT?

### 8. Japan, the answer (44 words, about 19 s)

> For about eight years, prices barely move. About half a percent a year. One reason economists
> give: most of the new money just sits in the banks, unspent. So it's not how much new money there
> is. It's whether it's spent... and on what.

- Research: C2-XB04, C2-C06 (about half a percent a year, 2013 to 2021, confirmed with nuance);
  C2-C06, C2-XB03 (most of the money sat in the banks; "one reason", because the ledger lists
  others).
- Terms: unspent. The short recap: "whether it's spent... and on what".
- Pictures: page-5 part A. The real line is drawn almost flat, ABOUT ½% A YEAR. Keep ½, not 0.5. The
  ramen tag never moves. New words only: MOSTLY UNSPENT, IN THE BANKS beside the stack, then the recap
  card NOT HOW MUCH... WHETHER IT'S SPENT, AND ON WHAT.

### 9. Here, twenty twenty (82 words, about 35 s)

> Now, here. In twenty twenty, Covid arrived. The Reserve Bank created new money, and the government
> spent billions, including the wage subsidy. By late twenty twenty-one, just three point two
> percent of people who wanted a job were out of work. A record low. New Zealand was close to the
> flat-out factory. This year, two outside experts reviewed what the Reserve Bank did. They say all
> that support was right at first... but, looking back, too strong, for too long.

- Research:
  - C2-N06: the Reserve Bank created new money.
  - C2-N14: a Covid fund of about sixty billion dollars, including wage subsidies.
  - C2-N09: three point two percent at the end of 2021, the lowest since records began in 1986.
  - C2-N15, C2-XC04: the review by two outside experts, Orphanides and Archer, September 2026. It
    found the combined support "appropriate in the early stages" but, "with the benefit of
    hindsight", "too strong for too long", and called the economy "overheated". That supports
    "close to the flat-out factory".
- Terms: the support (defined by the sentence before it); a record low; the review (said as "two
  outside experts reviewed", so "independent review" isn't needed).
- Pictures: page-8 part B's New Zealand street. NEW ZEALAND 2020, then 2021. New words: NEW MONEY +
  WAGE SUBSIDY. Then 3.2% OUT OF WORK / RECORD LOW, with a small source line: Stats NZ, records since
  1986. Then page-8 part C's reviewer's desk: the report, and the stamps RIGHT AT FIRST and TOO
  STRONG, FOR TOO LONG, with LOOKING BACK. Drop the card house and +30%, because house prices are no
  longer in the narration.

### 10. The usual cure (61 words, about 26 s)

> So how do you bring prices down? To see the usual cure, and who pays for it, go back to Britain...
> nineteen twenty. The war is over, but prices are still climbing. So the Bank of England, Britain's
> version of our Reserve Bank, raises its interest rate. That's the extra you pay to borrow money.
> It goes up to seven percent.

- Research: C2-H06 (after the war, prices kept climbing); C2-H07 (seven percent from April 1920, for
  about a year); C2-M17 (raising the interest rate is how central banks fight rising prices).
- Terms: interest rate, explained in its own sentence; the Bank of England.
- Pictures: page-9 part A, the wet town street at dusk. THE USUAL CURE? hook, then BRITAIN 1920. The
  card bank pops up, and the 3D meter slams from 6 to 7 on "seven". New words: INTEREST RATE: THE
  EXTRA YOU PAY TO BORROW.

### 11. Who pays, then (50 words, about 21 s)

> Loans cost more. People spend less. Businesses sell less, so they hire fewer people. Prices do
> fall. But within a year, many more people are out of work. Not about two in every hundred... but
> more than one in ten. Historians still argue over how much the interest rate caused.

- Research: C2-XD01 (loans cost more, people spend less); C2-M17 (businesses hire less); C2-H09
  (prices fell); C2-H08 (about two percent in 1920 to eleven in 1921, said as "more than one in
  ten"). Whether the interest rate caused the slump is debated (C2-H08, C2-XD02 notes). So the page
  says what followed, plus one line saying it's argued.
- Pictures:
  - Page-9 part A: coins pulled back into the bank, the shop light going out. LOANS COST MORE /
    PEOPLE SPEND LESS / FEWER HIRED.
  - Dip to part B's factory gate: the dole queue grows. PRICES DO FALL. The bars are relabelled
    ABOUT 2 IN 100 (1920) and MORE THAN 1 IN 10 (1921).
  - Remove PAID WITH THEIR JOBS, which the narration no longer says.
  - Small line: HISTORIANS STILL ARGUE HOW MUCH THE INTEREST RATE CAUSED.

### 12. Mattei's argument (57 words, about 24 s)

> Banks like our Reserve Bank call lost jobs a cost, not the goal. The economist Clara E.
> /məˈteɪi/, whose book this series follows, sees it differently. She argues that in nineteen
> twenty, the lost jobs were part of the point. When jobs are hard to find, she says, workers lose
> the power to ask for more pay.

- Research: C2-M17 (central banks call job losses a cost, not a goal); C2-XD02 (Mattei on 1920, in
  her own words: unemployment "deliberately engineered"); C2-H18 (fewer jobs mean workers can't
  ask for more). This is her interpretation, and it's disputed (Glasner, Tomlinson), so it is
  marked "she argues" and "she says". C2-H17 is backup only.
- Terms: Mattei, introduced by her full name and why she matters to the series.
- Pictures: page-9 part B continued. New card: BANKS LIKE OUR RESERVE BANK: A COST, NOT THE GOAL.
  Then her book with its credit as a typed card (CLARA E. MATTEI, THE CAPITAL ORDER, 2022; no
  portrait), MATTEI ARGUES: PART OF THE POINT. The iron weight drops on the pay packet. LESS POWER
  TO ASK FOR MORE PAY.

### 13. Who pays, now (76 words, about 33 s)

> And here? The Reserve Bank raised its interest rate. From a quarter of a percent... to five and a
> half percent by twenty twenty-three. Since late twenty twenty-one, the percentage out of work has
> climbed from three point two to five point six. That's about a hundred and seventy thousand
> people, looking for work. Not all of that is because of the interest rate. And this year, the
> Reserve Bank has raised its interest rate again.

- Research:
  - C2-XD03: a quarter of a percent in March 2020, five and a half by May 2023.
  - C2-N09, C2-N11, C2-XD05: 3.2% to 5.6% in the June 2026 quarter, with 171,000 people out of
    work (the total, not the rise). Unemployment also rose while the rate was being cut, so the
    script says "not all of that".
  - C2-XD04: raised in July and September 2026, said as "has raised", not "is raising".
- Pictures: page-10 part A, the New Zealand suburb at dusk.
  - The dial goes 0.25% to 5.5% (2023).
  - The counter goes 3.2% to 5.6% OUT OF WORK, over the 100 dots.
  - New words: ABOUT 170,000 PEOPLE and NOT ALL FROM THE INTEREST RATE.
  - The dial ticks 2.25% to 2.75% (2026). Relabel RISING AGAIN as RAISED AGAIN.

### 14. Spent on what? (50 words, about 21 s, then the fade)

> So next time you hear that new money makes prices rise... remember the two factories. If there
> are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more made. If there aren't...
> it mostly pushes prices up. So ask two questions. Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?

- Research: C2-XA03, C2-M09 (the rule, in the same words as page 6).
- Pictures: a two-second return to page-3's two factories with the rule cards. Then page-10 part B's
  chamber: the politician, SPENT ON WHAT?, WHO PAYS TO STOP IT?, and the fade.

## A listener test (after one watch, no replay)

1. When does new money push prices up, and when doesn't it?
2. Japan doubled its new money. What happened to prices, and why?
3. The key question: the government pays builders with new money to build state houses. Will that
   push prices up? What would you want to know first? (A strong answer asks whether builders are
   spare or already busy.)
4. Who paid in Britain after nineteen twenty? Is "the lost jobs were part of the point" a fact, or
   someone's argument? Whose?
5. Was there any word you didn't know?

The wrong idea to listen for: "Yes, printing money causes inflation." If question 1 passes but
question 3 fails, the rule was memorised but not understood: make page 6 plainer.

## Traps

- **Figures that change soon:**
  - New unemployment figures come out on 4 November and may replace 5.6% and 171,000.
  - The Reserve Bank decides again on 28 October, so "has raised its interest rate again" needs
    checking.
  - "This year" (pages 9 and 13) becomes wrong in 2027. Say "in twenty twenty-six" then.
- **The election is on 7 November.** Name no party or minister. Don't mention who ordered the review.
- **"Mostly" must stay** in the rule, and "partly" with Britain's new money. "One reason" must stay
