HOW IT TEACHES. It starts at the checkout (inflation is "prices in general, going up... the whole basket") and the warning viewers have heard ("if the government creates new money, prices will go up"). The stake comes in the first 25 seconds: the usual cure for rising prices can put people out of work. Then there's one question: "Does new money always push prices up?" A newcomer needs no chapter 1. Page 2 says who creates new money (the Reserve Bank can) and gives the "can run out of workers, materials and machines" premise, said as the view of economists who study how modern money works. Mattei is introduced by name and role before her view.

The investigation runs as three tests. Each ends a page with a guess, held for about 3 seconds as a real gap between recorded pages (a "..." is too short), and each gets a plain answer:
1. Britain at war: prices roughly doubled. "So far, the warning seems right. But look closer."
2. Japan 2013: prices barely moved. "So no... new money doesn't always push prices up." The reason is the mainstream economists' view: most of the money sat in the banks.
3. Here: "which factory was New Zealand?" The answer is "the flat-out one", with 3.2% unemployment and the review's "right at first... but looking back, too strong, for too long".

The two factories come after the first two tests disagree, so they answer a puzzle the viewer already holds. Test three makes the viewer use the picture on a new case. The rule is said in full on page 9 and repeated word for word on page 16. "It's what it's spent on" becomes the closing "Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?"

Then come the scope and the stakes:
- A second cause: the fuel part of today's 4.1% is a cost, not spending.
- The usual cure, step by step: loans cost more, people spend less, businesses hire fewer people, and prices rise more slowly ("not fall").
- Who pays: people with mortgages and loans, and 171,000 people looking for work. "Not all of that comes from the interest rate."
- The official view in its own terms ("an unwanted side effect... not the goal"), then Mattei's, marked twice as hers.

The takeaway a viewer should be able to say back: "New money mostly pushes prices up when there's nothing spare to make more with. When there are spare workers, materials and machines, it mostly gets more made. So ask: spent on what?"

FOR NEWCOMERS:
- Every term is explained at first use: inflation, who creates new money, the quiet and flat-out factories, things we can't make more of in a hurry, unemployment (example first, then the name), and interest ("the extra you pay back when you borrow").
- "Central bank" is avoided ("its version of our Reserve Bank"), and so are shares, marks, the Ruhr, "the price of borrowing" and bare "rates".
- One name for each thing.
- Three places, with time running one way.
- 18 spoken figures, against about 37 in the recording.
- 862 words in 113 sentences, averaging 7.7 words; the longest is 19.
- 16 pages with 11 to 27 seconds of speech each, about one new idea every 25 seconds.

NAOMI KLEIN IN SPIRIT: a scene the viewer has lived; stakes first; the viewer's own questions, answered within a breath; concrete people and places; who pays, named with numbers of people; the other side stated fairly. None of her wording, no "shock", no claims about intent except Mattei's, said as hers. The Pinker toolkit's principles are used without his voice:
- an observable goal;
- a concrete case before each principle;
- prediction checks;
- saying what each example shows;
- the analogy mapped where it breaks (money must be spent, which is why Japan is covered);
- qualifications kept ("partly", "roughly", "about", "mostly", "close to", "looking back", "not all of that").

## 9. The answer (26 s)

Here's the answer. When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more made. When there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up. It's what it's spent on.

# Draft: one-model - Chapter 2 - What really causes inflation: two factories (newcomer rewrite, one model)

How it teaches. One picture is built before any real case, then carried to the end.

The picture comes first, over three pages:
- Page 3, the quiet factory: machines still, workers waiting at the gate.
- Page 4, the flat-out factory: every worker and machine busy. A guess comes at the page end, with a 3-second hold.
- Page 5, the rule, stated in plain words.

The mapping is said aloud: "Think of each one as a whole country... all its workers, materials and machines". New money arrives as "orders for more things", and the counter shows prices.

Each case is then placed on the picture out loud:
- Britain in the First World War is "close to the flat-out factory".
- Japan 2013 is a guess, then the answer: the money "never reached the factory", because most of it sat in the banks.
- New Zealand 2020-21 is "close to the flat-out factory".
- Homes are "like the flat-out factory", because you can't build a house in a hurry.
- The usual cure means "the factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate".
- Fuel means "prices rise in both factories... with or without new money".
- Mattei argues "those workers at the gate aren't a side effect".
- Page 15 repeats page 5's rule word for word, then asks the two questions.

The picture's limits are handled where they arise: unspent money (Japan), slow-to-build homes, costs (fuel), and "mostly" and "close to" (no country is purely one factory).

Takeaway a viewer can say back: whether new money pushes prices up depends on what it's spent on. With workers waiting at the gate it mostly gets more things made; in a flat-out factory it mostly pushes prices up.

Toolkit principles used (not Pinker's voice):
- The opening starts from something the viewer has seen (the checkout).
- Expert shortcuts are unpacked: central bank is shown by its example, the Reserve Bank. Unemployment, interest, inflation and Royal Commission are each defined at first use.
- Every abstraction gets a concrete case, and the picture is mapped, with the points where it breaks.
- There are two prediction checks and three recaps in the same words.
- How sure each claim is can be heard in its wording: "most economists agree", "one reason economists give", "a Royal Commission found", "Mattei argues".

Naomi Klein in spirit only:
- Stakes come within about 25 seconds (the cure can cost jobs).
- Direct questions, and real places.
- Numbers are said as people: "about a hundred and seventy thousand people".
- Who pays is the refrain: people with loans and people who lose jobs.
- The central banks' view is given in one fair line ("an unwanted side effect"), then Mattei's, marked as hers twice.
- None of her wording; the checker's never-use list came back clean.
