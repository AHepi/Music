# 33 Script - chapter 2 - for newcomers - one model draft

Source for the series: Clara E. Mattei, *The Capital Order* (2022). Facts: only the ids in
`chapter-2-inflation/18 Research - chapter 2 - what really causes inflation.md`. No new facts.

**What this is:** a complete new narration for chapter 2, "what really causes inflation", written
for people who have never seen chapter 1 and have no economics background. It is built on one
picture carried from start to finish: two factories, one quiet and one flat out. Every real case is
placed onto that picture out loud. The narration is exactly as it would be sent to Malina (the accent
line is added later).

**Status:** a draft. It has not been heard by a real newcomer and not read by Malina. No independent
fact checker has checked it yet; I checked every line against the ledger myself. Nothing outside this
work folder was changed.

**Length:** 886 spoken words in 15 pages, and 101 sentences averaging 8.8 words, the longest 19.
- At Malina's likely pace with sentences this short (about 152 words a minute, the speed of the
  short-sentence pages in the recording) plus page turns and two 3-second guess holds: about **6
  minutes 22 seconds**.
- At the strict planning pace of 137.5 words a minute counting pauses, plus the two holds: about
  **6 minutes 33 seconds**.
- If it has to be shorter, the first two lines to cut are on pages 12 and 13 (see "What to cut first").

**How it compares with the recording:** the recording packed 689 words into about 4 minutes 25 seconds,
at about 164 words a minute. This draft has:
- 3 places instead of 8 cases;
- about 22 spoken numbers instead of about 37;
- one new idea about every 26 seconds instead of every 18.

## The one picture

**Two factories.** "Think of each one as a whole country... all its workers, materials and machines."

| In the picture | Stands for |
|---|---|
| a factory | a whole country's workers, materials and machines |
| workers waiting at the gate | people who want a job but can't find one |
| coins spent in the yard, as orders for more things | new money, once it is spent |
| things coming out | things made |
| the price counter | prices in general |
| **the quiet factory** (machines still, workers at the gate) | spare workers, materials and machines |
| **the flat-out factory** (every worker and machine already busy) | none spare |

**Where the picture breaks, and how the script handles each:**
1. Money only counts once it is spent. Japan's new money mostly sat in the banks, so "it never reached
   the factory" (page 8).
2. Some things can't be made faster, whatever the factory is doing. Homes: "money spent on homes mostly
   pushes prices up... like the flat-out factory" (page 10).
3. A cost like fuel pushes prices up in both factories (page 13).
4. Real countries are never exactly one or the other. Hence "mostly" in the rule, and "close to the
   flat-out factory" for both Britain and New Zealand.

**The takeaway a viewer should be able to say back:** whether new money pushes prices up depends on
what it's spent on. With workers waiting at the gate, it mostly gets more things made. In a flat-out
factory, it mostly pushes prices up.

**The recaps (said in the same words each time):**
- Pages 5 and 15, word for word: "In the quiet factory, new money mostly gets more things made. In the
  flat-out factory, it mostly pushes prices up."
- Page 8: "It isn't just how much new money is created. It's whether it's spent... and what it's spent
  on."
- Page 15: "Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?"

**Two guesses, each at the end of a page.** A "..." lasts under a second, so each guess needs a
held pause of about 3 seconds in the mix, with the question on screen:
- Page 4: "What do you think happens?"
- Page 7: "What would you expect?"

**One plain name for each thing:**
- inflation (defined once on page 1, then "rising prices"; "inflation" comes back only when quoting
  the claim on page 15)
- new money
- the Reserve Bank
- central bank
- workers, materials and machines (always in that order)
- the quiet factory / the flat-out factory
- workers at the gate
- the usual cure
- interest rate (never bare "rates")
- unemployment
- loans (home loans as the example)
- the shopping basket
- one price tag

## Page 1 - The question (about 28 seconds)

You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. You've heard the warning too. Create new money... and prices will rise. So is the warning right? The answer matters... because the usual cure for rising prices can cost people their jobs.

- **Research:** C2-XA04, C2-M01, C2-XA05 (the basket costs more than a year ago; dateless on purpose),
  C2-M17, C2-XD01 (the cure can cost jobs; "can" kept).
- **Explained here:** inflation ("prices in general, going up"); "not one price tag, the whole basket"
  (paid off on page 13).
- **Pictures:** page-1, with its two places swapped and code changes only.
  - First the painted checkout: the basket on the belt and its tag, A YEAR AGO $100.00 then NOW
    $104.10 (example prices, labelled as such). INFLATION / PRICES IN GENERAL, GOING UP. One tag
    swings alone, then all the tags flip together: THE WHOLE BASKET.
  - Then the debating chamber: the invented politician at the lectern (no party colours) with
    CREATE NEW MONEY... PRICES WILL RISE?, then THE USUAL CURE CAN COST JOBS.
  - The brass shell that dropped onto the belt (the bridge to chapter 1's shell factory) is removed.

## Page 2 - Where new money comes from (about 21 seconds)

First, where does new money come from? A country's central bank can create it. Here, that's the Reserve Bank. Economists who study how modern money works say a country with its own money can't run out of it. What it can run out of... is workers, materials and machines.

- **Research:** C2-N06 (the Reserve Bank created new money), C2-XB03, C2-M15 (a central bank *can*
  create money; never "all money"), C2-M12 (said as these economists' view).
- **Explained here:**
  - central bank, by its local example (the Reserve Bank);
  - where new money comes from;
  - chapter 1's idea, told fresh: a country can't run out of its own money, only of workers,
    materials and machines.
- **Pictures:** code changes only.
  - Page-10's New Zealand street at dusk, with a typed name card THE RESERVE BANK (name only, no logo)
    and page-2's banknote bundles landing: NEW MONEY.
  - Then page-3's three painted cut-outs (a worker, materials, a lathe) pop up as WORKERS /
    MATERIALS / MACHINES, under a card RUN OUT OF MONEY? struck through in red pen. The struck-through
    card is the same device chapter 1's page 8 uses.

## Page 3 - The quiet factory (about 28 seconds)

Picture two factories. Think of each one as a whole country... all its workers, materials and machines. In the first, the machines stand still. Workers wait at the gate, hoping for a job. Call it the quiet factory. Now new money is spent there, on orders for more things. The workers go in. The machines start. More things get made... and prices barely move.

- **Research:** C2-XA03, C2-M09 (an imagined picture, announced as one; "barely move", never "don't
  move").
- **Explained here:** the picture and what it stands for; the quiet factory; "orders for more things"
  (so "orders" can't be heard as commands).
- **Pictures:** page-3's two-factories set, works A (left). Code changes only.
  - The camera cranes down onto both works and settles on A, with a card: A WHOLE COUNTRY: WORKERS,
    MATERIALS, MACHINES, then THE QUIET FACTORY.
  - The queue waits at the gate. Coins rain into the yard (NEW ORDERS). The workers go in, the windows
    light, smoke rises and shells come out. The price counter stays put.
  - The counter's £ sign is replaced by a plain PRICES label, since the factory now stands for any
    country.

## Page 4 - The flat-out factory (about 15 seconds, then a 3-second hold)

In the second factory, every worker and every machine is already busy, working as hard as they can. Call it the flat-out factory. The same new money is spent there. What do you think happens?

- **Research:** C2-XA03, C2-M05.
- **Explained here:** flat out ("working as hard as they can"); the flat-out factory.
- **Pictures:** page-3's works B (right), already running. The same coins rain in, and the counter
  freezes before it flips. WHAT HAPPENS? is held about 3 seconds with the music up (guess 1).

## Page 5 - The rule (about 21 seconds)

No spare workers, materials or machines. So no more things get made. Just more money, offered for the same things... so prices rise. Most economists agree on this much. In the quiet factory, new money mostly gets more things made. In the flat-out factory, it mostly pushes prices up.

- **Research:** C2-XA03, C2-M05 (textbook, accepted across schools: "most economists agree"), C2-M09
  ("mostly": prices can start rising before everything is busy).
- **Explained here:** the rule (recap 1).
- **Pictures:** code changes only.
  - Works B's counter flips up: the answer.
  - The three cut-outs return, each stamped NO SPARE.
  - A wide shot of both works with two typed cards, worded as she says them: QUIET: MOSTLY MORE THINGS
    MADE and FLAT OUT: MOSTLY PRICES UP. A small line: MOST ECONOMISTS AGREE.
  - This reuses the end of page-8 part A on works B, with its YES, IT CAN replaced.

## Page 6 - Britain at war (about 28 seconds)

This really happened. Britain, during the First World War. Fewer than one worker in a hundred was out of work. German submarines sank ships carrying food and materials. And the war was paid for partly with new money. More money... and fewer things to buy. By the end of the war, prices had roughly doubled. Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory.

- **Research:** C2-H02 (under one percent in 1915 to 1918, so "during the war", not a single year),
  C2-H03, C2-XA02, C2-XA01 ("partly"), C2-H01 ("roughly doubled", 1914 to 1918). The second fact check
  confirmed "close to the second factory".
- **Explained here:** the First World War is named; nothing else new.
- **Pictures:** page-2's munitions hall as built, with its words matched to the new lines. Code
  changes only.
  - Title BRITAIN, FIRST WORLD WAR (not BRITAIN 1915).
  - The 100 dots with one nearly empty: FEWER THAN 1 IN 100 OUT OF WORK.
  - The paper ships go under: SHIPS SUNK: FOOD, MATERIALS.
  - The bundles of notes: PARTLY NEW MONEY.
  - MORE MONEY / FEWER THINGS.
  - The bars 1914 and 1918: ABOUT ×2.
  - Last line: a quick cut to works B with CLOSE TO THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY.

## Page 7 - Japan, the guess (about 15 seconds, then a 3-second hold)

## Page 8 - Japan, the answer (about 24 seconds)

For about eight years, prices barely moved. About half a percent a year, on average. Why? One reason economists give... most of the new money just sat in the banks. In our picture, it never reached the factory. So it isn't just how much new money is created. It's whether it's spent... and what it's spent on.

- **Research:** C2-XB04, C2-C06 ("about half a percent" for 2013 to 2021, confirmed fair as
  narration), C2-XB03 and C2-C06 (most of it sat as bank reserves; "one reason", because the ledger
  names others).
- **Explained here:** money that is created but not spent; recap 2.
- **Pictures:** code changes only.
  - Page-5 part A's real line, drawn almost flat: ABOUT ½% A YEAR (keep ½, not 0.5).
  - A quick cut to the two-factories set. Page-9's card bank stands beside works A. Coins pile up in
    front of the bank and never reach the yard: MOST OF IT SAT IN THE BANKS.
  - Card: NOT JUST HOW MUCH / WHETHER IT'S SPENT, AND ON WHAT.

## Page 9 - New Zealand, close to flat out (about 26 seconds)

New Zealand, twenty twenty. When Covid hit, the Reserve Bank created new money, and the government spent more. Unemployment is the share of people who want a job but can't find one. By the end of twenty twenty-one, it was three point two percent... the lowest since records began. Very few workers were spare. That's close to the flat-out factory.

- **Research:**
  - C2-N06 and C2-N14 (new money; the government spent more);
  - C2-N09 (3.2%, the lowest since records began; "unsustainable" is the review's word and is not
    used);
  - C2-N15 (not spoken, but it backs the mapping: the review says the economy "overheated" and the
    support "later contributed to excess demand").
- **Explained here:** unemployment (defined first, then given as a number).
- **Pictures:** page-8 part B's New Zealand street. Code changes only.
  - Title NEW ZEALAND 2020 (was 2021).
  - Bundles of notes land (NEW MONEY), and a card reads GOVERNMENT SPENT MORE.
  - The 100-dot panel, with 3 empty: 3.2% UNEMPLOYMENT / LOWEST SINCE RECORDS BEGAN, credited to
    Stats NZ. The "1986" line is dropped because the narration doesn't say it.
  - A quick cut to works B: CLOSE TO THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY.

## Page 10 - Homes (about 29 seconds)

And homes? You can't build a house in a hurry. So money spent on homes mostly pushes prices up... like the flat-out factory. House prices aren't in the shopping basket. But in the twelve months to the middle of twenty twenty-one, they rose about thirty percent. A Royal Commission, an official inquiry, found the economic response to Covid was part of the reason. So were world events.

- **Research:**
  - "You can't build a house in a hurry" was confirmed in the second fact check.
  - C2-M10 (the film's general point about things we can't make more of quickly; the Royal Commission

  - C2-N08 (house prices aren't in the inflation figure);
  - C2-XC03 ("about thirty percent" fits the house price index for the year to mid-2021 only);
  - C2-N14.
- **Explained here:** Royal Commission ("an official inquiry"); why house prices are kept apart from
  the basket.
- **Pictures:** all reused, with code changes only.

## Page 11 - The usual cure (about 31 seconds)

So what's the usual cure for rising prices? The Reserve Bank raises its interest rate. Interest is the extra you pay when you borrow. That makes many loans cost more, home loans included... so people have less to spend. Businesses sell less, and hire fewer people. The cure is meant to make prices rise more slowly... not fall. In our picture, the factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate.

- **Research:** C2-XD01 (the Bank of England's own account: loans cost more, people spend less,
  prices rise less quickly), C2-M17 (businesses hire less; it works with a delay).
- **Explained here:**
  - interest and interest rate;
  - the usual cure (the promise made on page 1);
  - falling inflation means prices rising more slowly, not falling.
- **Pictures:** code changes only.
  - Page-10 part A's New Zealand street at dusk. The card interest-rate dial swings up, with no
    figures. A card reads INTEREST: THE EXTRA YOU PAY TO BORROW.
  - Page-9 part A's coins pulled back into the card bank are moved onto this street, and a shop light
    goes out: LESS TO SPEND.
  - Then PRICES RISE MORE SLOWLY / NOT FALL.
  - Works B: the smoke thins, the windows dim and the queue cut-out comes back to the gate: MORE
    WORKERS AT THE GATE.

## Page 12 - The cure, here (about 29 seconds)

Here, from late twenty twenty-one, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again and again. Since then, unemployment has climbed from three point two percent to five point six. Five point six percent is about a hundred and seventy thousand people, looking for work. The interest rate isn't the only reason for that rise. And this year, the Reserve Bank has raised its interest rate again.

- **Research:**
  - C2-N10, C2-XD03 (the run of rises from October 2021 to May 2023; never say how many);
  - C2-N11, C2-XD05 (5.6% in the June 2026 quarter; 171,000 people out of work, the total, not the
    increase; unemployment also rose while the interest rate was being cut, hence "isn't the only
    reason");
  - C2-XD04, C2-N12 (raised in July and September 2026; past tense).
- **Explained here:** nothing new; the 5.6% is said as people.
- **Pictures:** page-10 part A. Code changes only.
  - The dial climbs in steps: AGAIN AND AGAIN. Its 0.25% and 5.5% figures are removed, because the
    narration doesn't say them.
  - The 100-dot panel counts from 3.2% to 5.6% (3 empty, then 6): ABOUT 170,000 PEOPLE, with a small

## Page 13 - Prices now (about 37 seconds)

And prices, now? In the twelve months to June, the shopping basket cost four point one percent more. Without the rise in petrol and diesel... it's two point nine. The Reserve Bank aims for one to three percent a year. So one price tag, fuel, pushed the whole basket past that. Almost everything is made or moved with fuel. When fuel costs more, prices rise in both factories... with or without new money. Can the usual cure fix a rise in costs? Economists argue about that.

- **Research:**
  - C2-N05, C2-M04, C2-XA05, C2-XC05. Stats NZ's own wording: "if petrol and diesel prices had not
    changed... 2.9 percent". Never say "mostly fuel": that's the C2-M18 arithmetic trap.
  - C2-N04 (the 1 to 3% target);
  - C2-M06 (a rise in costs, "whatever the government is spending");
  - C2-N12, C2-M17 (whether to answer a fuel rise with a higher interest rate is debated). The
    question is general, so it doesn't claim fuel is the Reserve Bank's reason (C2-M18).
- **Explained here:** what the Reserve Bank aims for (so "two point nine" means something); a cost as
  a second cause of rising prices.
- **Pictures:** code changes only.
  - Page-7 part B's sunny New Zealand street, with the bars 4.1% and 2.9%. Labels: WITHOUT THE PETROL
    AND DIESEL RISE, STATS NZ, YEAR TO JUNE 2026. Add a band, 1–3% RESERVE BANK AIMS, and the card ONE
    PRICE TAG: FUEL.
  - Then the two-factories wide shot. Page-7 part A's oil barrel, without its dollar counter, lands by
    each works, and both price counters flip up together: BOTH FACTORIES / WITH OR WITHOUT NEW MONEY.
  - Last, a small ECONOMISTS ARGUE.

## Page 14 - Who pays (about 31 seconds)

So who pays for the cure? People paying off loans. And people who lose their jobs. Central banks call those lost jobs an unwanted side effect. The economist Clara E. /məˈteɪi/, whose book this series follows, sees it differently. She argues those workers at the gate aren't a side effect. They're how the cure works. When jobs are hard to find, she says, workers have less power to ask for more pay.

- **Research:**
  - C2-XD01, C2-M17 (who pays; central banks treat lost jobs as a cost, not a goal);
  - C2-H18 (her own words: the main aim of interest rate rises is to "cool down" the labour market;
    fewer jobs "diminish the bargaining power of workers");
  - C2-XD02. Her reading is disputed (Glasner, Tomlinson), so it is said as hers twice: "she argues",
    "she says".
- **Explained here:** who Mattei is, in one clause, at first use.
- **Pictures:** page-9 part B's factory gate, without the 1920 and 1921 bars or PRICES DO FALL. Code
  changes only.
  - The queue grows from the gate. A card reads PEOPLE PAYING OFF LOANS, and another reads CENTRAL
    BANKS: AN UNWANTED SIDE EFFECT.
  - Her book pops up with its credit, CLARA E. MATTEI, THE CAPITAL ORDER (2022), and MATTEI ARGUES:
    NOT A SIDE EFFECT.
  - The iron weight drops on the pay packet: LESS POWER TO ASK FOR MORE PAY. The credit to her stays
    on screen.

## A listener test (once, on real newcomers, after one watch, no replay)

1. When does new money push prices up, and when doesn't it?
2. Japan doubled the money its central bank had created. What happened to prices, and why?
3. **The most useful one.** The government creates new money to pay builders for state houses. Will it
   push prices up? What would you want to know first?
   - A strong answer asks whether builders are already busy.
   - If question 1 passes but this one fails, the rule was memorised, not understood: make pages 3 to 5
     slower.
4. Prices rose about four percent, and under three without petrol and diesel. Was that new money?
5. Who pays when the interest rate goes up? Is "the lost jobs are how the cure works" a fact, or
   someone's argument? Whose?
6. Was there any word you didn't know?

The wrong idea to listen for: "Yes, printing money causes inflation, look at Germany." If it comes
back, add the one Germany line above.

## Read-aloud and anti-banality fixes made

**Read-aloud** (lines read as Malina would say them):
- "So many loans cost more" can be heard as "so many loans": it became "That makes many loans cost
  more".
- "So few workers were spare" can be heard two ways: it became "Very few workers were spare".
- "orders" alone can mean commands: it became "orders for more things".
- "forces from overseas" can sound military: it became the ledger's "world events".
- "That's about 170,000 people" could be heard as the rise: it became "Five point six percent is
  about...".
- "Leave out petrol and diesel" became "Without the rise in petrol and diesel", closer to Stats NZ's
  own wording.
- A colon became "...", so the voice pauses.

**Anti-banality** (stock phrases and vague lines replaced):
- "It sounds like common sense" was cut.
- "Good news for anyone looking for work" was cut: it is a judgement, not a sourced fact.
- "a cost, not a goal" became "an unwanted side effect", so Mattei's answer ("aren't a side effect")
  meets the central banks' view in the same words.
- "This has happened for real" became "This really happened".

## Traps

- **Figures that change soon:**
  - 4.1% and 2.9% change on 22 October;
  - the Reserve Bank decides on 28 October; "has raised... again" stays true;
  - 5.6% and 170,000 change on 4 November;
  - "this year" means 2026; say "in twenty twenty-six" if the film is watched in 2027.
- **The election is on 7 November:**
  - no parties or ministers;
  - nothing on who ordered the review;
  - the invented politician has no party colours.
- **Qualifiers that must stay:**
  - "mostly" in the rule;
  - "partly" for Britain;
