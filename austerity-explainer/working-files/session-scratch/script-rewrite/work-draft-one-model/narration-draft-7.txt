## Page 1 - The question
You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. You've probably heard the warning, too. Create new money... and prices will rise. So is the warning right? The answer matters... because the usual cure for rising prices can cost people their jobs.

## Page 2 - Where new money comes from
First, where does new money come from? A country's central bank can create it. Here, that's the Reserve Bank. Economists who study how modern money works say a country with its own money can't run out of it. What it can run out of... is workers, materials and machines.

## Page 3 - The quiet factory
So picture two factories. Think of each one as a whole country... all its workers, materials and machines. In the first, the machines stand still. Workers wait at the gate, hoping for a job. Call it the quiet factory. Now new money is spent there, on orders for more things. The workers go in. The machines start. More things get made... and prices barely move.

## Page 4 - The flat-out factory
In the second factory, every worker and every machine is already busy, working as hard as they can. Call it the flat-out factory. The same new money is spent there. What do you think happens?

## Page 5 - The rule
No spare workers, materials or machines. So no more things get made. Just more money, offered for the same things... so prices rise. Most economists agree on this much. In the quiet factory, new money mostly gets more things made. In the flat-out factory, it mostly pushes prices up.

## Page 6 - Britain at war
Britain, during the First World War. Fewer than one worker in a hundred was out of work. German submarines sank ships carrying food and materials. And the war was paid for partly with new money. More money... and fewer things to buy. By the end of the war, prices had roughly doubled. Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory.

## Page 7 - Japan, the guess
Now test the warning, nearly a century later. Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's central bank sets out to double the money it has created... in just two years. If new money on its own pushed prices up... what would you expect?

## Page 8 - Japan, the answer
For about eight years, prices barely moved. About half a percent a year, on average. Why? One reason economists give: most of the new money just sat in the banks. In our picture, it never reached the factory. So what matters isn't just how much new money is created. It's whether it's spent... and what it's spent on.

## Page 9 - New Zealand, close to flat out
New Zealand, twenty twenty. When Covid hit, the Reserve Bank created new money, and the government spent more. Unemployment counts the people who want a job but can't find one. By the end of twenty twenty-one, it was three point two percent... the lowest since records began. Very few workers were spare. That's close to the flat-out factory.

## Page 10 - Homes
And homes? You can't build a house in a hurry. So money spent on homes works like the flat-out factory... it mostly pushes prices up. House prices aren't counted in the shopping basket. But in the twelve months to the middle of twenty twenty-one, they rose about thirty percent. A Royal Commission, an official inquiry, found the economic response to Covid was part of the reason... along with world events.

## Page 11 - The usual cure
So what's the usual cure for rising prices? The Reserve Bank raises its interest rate. Interest is the extra you pay when you borrow. So many loans cost more, home loans included. People have less to spend on other things. Businesses sell less, and hire fewer people. The cure is meant to make prices rise more slowly... not fall. In our picture, the factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate.

## Page 12 - The cure, here
Here, from late twenty twenty-one, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again and again. Since then, unemployment has climbed from three point two percent to five point six. Five point six percent is about a hundred and seventy thousand people, looking for work. The interest rate isn't the only reason for that rise. And this year, the Reserve Bank has raised its interest rate again.

## Page 13 - Prices now
And prices? In the twelve months to June this year, the shopping basket cost four point one percent more. Leave out petrol and diesel... and it's two point nine. The Reserve Bank aims for one to three percent a year. So one price tag, fuel, pushed the whole basket past that. Almost everything is made or moved with fuel. When fuel costs more, prices rise in both factories... with or without new money. Can the usual cure fix a rise in costs? Economists argue about that.

## Page 14 - Who pays
So who pays for the cure? People paying off loans. And people who lose their jobs. Central banks call those lost jobs an unwanted side effect. The economist Clara E. /məˈteɪi/, whose book this series follows, sees it differently. She argues those workers at the gate aren't a side effect. They're how the cure works. When jobs are hard to find, she says, workers have less power to ask for more pay.

## Page 15 - Spent on what?
Remember the two factories. In the quiet factory, new money mostly gets more things made. In the flat-out factory, it mostly pushes prices up. Next time someone tells you new money causes inflation... ask two questions. Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?
