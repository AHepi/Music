## Page 1 - The question
You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. You've probably heard the warning, too. Create new money, and prices will rise. But the answer matters... because the usual cure for rising prices can cost people their jobs. So is the warning right?

## Page 2 - Where new money comes from
First, where new money comes from. A country's central bank can create it. Here, that's the Reserve Bank. Economists who study how modern money works say a country with its own money can't run out of it. What it can run out of... is workers, materials and machines.

## Page 3 - The quiet factory
So picture two factories. Think of each one as a whole country... all its workers, materials and machines. In the first, the machines stand still. Workers wait at the gate, hoping for a job. Call it the quiet factory. Now new money is spent there, on new orders. The workers go in. The machines start. More things get made... and prices barely move.

## Page 4 - The flat-out factory
In the second factory, every worker and every machine is already busy, working as hard as they can. Call it the flat-out factory. The same new money is spent there. What do you think happens?

## Page 5 - The rule
No spare workers, materials or machines. So no more things get made. Just more money, for the same things... so prices go up. Most economists agree on this much. In the quiet factory, new money mostly gets more things made. In the flat-out factory, it mostly pushes prices up.

## Page 6 - Britain at war
This has happened for real. Britain, during the First World War. Fewer than one in a hundred was out of work. German submarines sank ships carrying food and materials. And the war was paid for partly with new money. More money... and fewer things to buy. By the end of the war, prices had roughly doubled. Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory.

## Page 7 - Japan, the guess
Now test the warning, nearly a century later. Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's central bank sets out to double the money it has created... in just two years. If new money on its own pushed prices up... what would you expect? Have a guess.

## Page 8 - Japan, the answer
For about eight years, prices barely moved. About half a percent a year, on average. Why? Economists give several reasons. One is that most of the new money just sat in the banks. In our picture, it never reached the factory. So what matters isn't just how much new money is created. It's whether it's spent... and what it's spent on.

## Page 9 - Here, close to flat out
Here, when Covid hit, the Reserve Bank created new money, and the government spent more. By the end of twenty twenty-one, only about three in every hundred people who wanted a job couldn't find one. That's unemployment... three point two percent, the lowest since records began. So few workers were spare. That's close to the flat-out factory.

## Page 10 - Homes
And homes? You can't build a house in a hurry. So money spent on homes works like the flat-out factory... it mostly pushes prices up. House prices aren't in the shopping basket. But in the twelve months to mid twenty twenty-one, they rose about thirty percent. A Royal Commission, an official inquiry, found the economic response to Covid was part of the reason. So were forces from overseas.

## Page 11 - The usual cure
So what's the usual cure? The Reserve Bank raises its interest rate. Interest is the extra you pay when you borrow. So many loans cost more, home loans included. People have less to spend on other things. Businesses sell less, and hire fewer people. The cure is meant to make prices rise more slowly... not fall. In our picture, the factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate.

## Page 12 - The cure, here
Here, from late twenty twenty-one, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again and again. Since then, unemployment has climbed. By the middle of this year it was five point six percent... about a hundred and seventy thousand people, looking for work. Not all of that is the interest rate. And this year, the Reserve Bank has raised its interest rate again.

## Page 13 - Prices now
Yet prices are rising faster again. In the twelve months to June, the shopping basket cost four point one percent more. Leave out petrol and diesel... and it's two point nine. The Reserve Bank aims to keep that between one and three percent a year. So one price tag, fuel, pushed the whole basket past it. Almost everything is made or moved with fuel. When fuel costs more, prices climb in both factories... with or without new money. Is the usual cure the right answer to that? Economists argue about it.

## Page 14 - Who pays
So who pays for the cure? People with loans to pay. And people who lose their jobs. Central banks call those lost jobs a cost... not the goal. The economist Clara E. /məˈteɪi/, whose book this series follows, sees it differently. She argues those workers at the gate aren't a side effect. They're how the cure works. When jobs are hard to find, she says, workers have less power to ask for more pay.

## Page 15 - Spent on what?
So remember the two factories. In the quiet factory, new money mostly gets more things made. In the flat-out factory, it mostly pushes prices up. Next time you hear that new money causes inflation... ask two questions. Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?
