## Page 1 - The question
You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. And you've probably heard the warning that comes with it. Create new money, and prices will rise. It sounds like common sense. But the answer matters... because the usual cure for rising prices can cost people their jobs. So is the warning right?

## Page 2 - Where new money comes from
Start with where new money comes from. A country's central bank can create it. Ours is the Reserve Bank. Economists who study how modern money works say a country with its own money, like ours, can't run out of it. What it can run out of... is workers, materials and machines.

## Page 3 - The quiet factory
So picture two factories. Each one stands for a whole country... its workers, materials and machines. In the first, the machines stand still. Workers wait at the gate, hoping for a job. Call it the quiet factory. Now new money is spent there, on orders. The workers go in. The machines start. More things get made... and prices barely move.

## Page 4 - The flat-out factory
In the second factory, every worker and every machine is already busy, working as hard as they can. Call it the flat-out factory. The same new money is spent there. What do you think happens?

## Page 5 - The rule
No spare workers. No spare machines or materials. So no more things get made. Just more money, for the same things... so prices go up. Most economists agree on this part. In the quiet factory, new money mostly gets more things made. In the flat-out factory, it mostly pushes prices up.

## Page 6 - Britain at war
This has happened for real. Britain, during the First World War. Almost everyone who could work had a job. Fewer than one in a hundred was out of work. German submarines sank ships carrying food and materials. And the war was paid for partly with new money. More money... and fewer things to buy. By the end of the war, prices had roughly doubled. Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory.

## Page 7 - Japan, the guess
Now a test. Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's central bank sets out to double the money it has created... in just two years. If new money on its own pushed prices up... what would you expect? Have a guess.

## Page 8 - Japan, the answer
For about eight years, prices barely moved. About half a percent a year, on average. Why? Economists give several reasons. One is that most of the new money just sat in the banks. It never reached the shops. In our picture, it never reached the factory. So it's not how much new money is created. It's whether it's spent... and what it's spent on.

## Page 9 - Here, flat out
Now here. When Covid hit, the Reserve Bank created new money, and the government spent more, to support the economy. Unemployment means people who want a job but can't find one. By the end of twenty twenty-one, it was three point two percent... the lowest since records began in nineteen eighty-six. Almost everyone who wanted a job had one. But few workers were spare. That's the flat-out factory. An independent review of the Reserve Bank says the support was right at first. But looking back... it was too strong, for too long.

## Page 10 - Homes
And homes? You can't build a house in a hurry. So when more money goes into buying homes, it's like the flat-out factory. It mostly pushes prices up. House prices aren't counted in the shopping basket. But in the twelve months to the middle of twenty twenty-one, they rose about thirty percent. A Royal Commission, an official inquiry, found the economic response to Covid was part of the reason... along with big forces from overseas.

## Page 11 - Prices now
And now? In the twelve months to June, the shopping basket cost four point one percent more. Leave out petrol and diesel... and it's two point nine. The Reserve Bank aims to keep prices rising between one and three percent a year. So one price tag, fuel, pushed the whole basket past that. Almost everything is made or moved with fuel. When fuel costs more, prices climb in both factories... quiet or flat out. This kind of rise doesn't need new money at all. It comes from a cost.

## Page 12 - The usual cure
So when prices rise too fast, what's the usual cure? The Reserve Bank raises its interest rate. Interest is the extra you pay when you borrow. So many mortgages and loans cost more, and people have less to spend on other things. Businesses sell less, and hire fewer people. And prices rise more slowly. Not fall... rise more slowly. In our picture, the cure takes orders away. The factory slows... and some workers end up back at the gate.

## Page 13 - The cure, here
Here, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate from a quarter of a percent to five and a half percent, by twenty twenty-three. Since the end of twenty twenty-one, unemployment has climbed from three point two percent to five point six. That's about a hundred and seventy thousand people looking for work. Not all of that is the interest rate. And this year, the Reserve Bank has raised its interest rate again. Can that cure work on a rise in costs, like fuel? Economists argue about that.

## Page 14 - Who pays
So who pays for the cure? People with mortgages and loans. And people who lose their jobs. Central banks call those lost jobs a cost of the cure... not its goal. The economist Clara E. /məˈteɪi/, whose book this series follows, sees it differently. She argues the workers at the gate aren't a side effect. They're how the cure works. When jobs are hard to find, she says, workers have less power to ask for more pay.

## Page 15 - Spent on what?
So next time you hear that new money causes inflation... picture the two factories. And ask two questions. Spent on what? Workers waiting at the gate... or a factory already flat out? And who pays to stop it?
