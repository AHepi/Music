## Page 1 - The question
You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. And you've heard the warning. If the government creates new money... prices will go up. Is that always true? It matters... because when prices go up too fast, the usual cure is to make borrowing cost more. And that can put people out of work.

## Page 2 - Where new money comes from
First, where does new money come from? Here in New Zealand, the Reserve Bank can create it. Economists who study how modern money works say a country like ours can't run out of its own money. What it can run out of... is workers, materials and machines.

## Page 3 - The quiet factory
Picture two factories. Think of each one as a whole country... all its workers, materials and machines. In the first, the machines stand still. Workers wait at the gate, hoping for a job. Call it the quiet factory. Now new money is spent there, to buy more things. The workers go in. The machines start. More things get made... and prices barely move.

## Page 4 - The flat-out factory
In the second factory, every worker and every machine is already busy, working as hard as they can. No one is waiting at the gate. Call it the flat-out factory. The same new money is spent there, to buy more things. What do you think happens? Have a guess.

## Page 5 - The rule
With no spare workers, materials or machines, no more things get made. Just more money, offered for the same things... and prices go up. Most economists agree on this much. Here's the rule. When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more things made. When there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up.

## Page 6 - Britain at war
Now a real case. Britain, during the First World War. Fewer than one worker in a hundred was out of work. German submarines sank ships carrying food and materials. And the war was paid for partly with new money. More money... and fewer things to buy. By the end of the war, prices had roughly doubled. Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory.

## Page 7 - Glasgow's homes
Homes worked like the flat-out factory too. Early in the war, crowds of workers came to the British city of Glasgow, for war work. Homes were already short. With builders and materials gone to the war, hardly any new ones could be built. So landlords raised the rent... in some parts of the city, by up to a quarter. The families who rented said no. Led by women like Mary Barbour, they kept paying the old rent... but not the extra. Within the year, about twenty thousand families were refusing. Soon after, Britain's Parliament brought rents on small homes back to what they were before the war.

## Page 8 - Japan, the guess
Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's version of our Reserve Bank set out to double the money it had created... in two years. If the warning is right, prices should go up fast. What do you think happened? Have a guess.

## Page 9 - Japan, the answer
Prices barely moved. For about eight years, they went up about half a percent a year, on average. Why? One reason economists give... most of the new money just sat in the banks. In our picture, it never reached the factory. So it isn't just how much new money is created. It's whether it's spent... and what it's spent on.

## Page 10 - New Zealand, the guess
Now, here. In twenty twenty, Covid hit. The Reserve Bank created new money... and the government spent more. By the end of twenty twenty-one, only about three in every hundred people who wanted a job were out of work. Three point two percent... the lowest since records began. Which factory was New Zealand closer to? Have a guess.

## Page 11 - New Zealand, the answer
Close to the flat-out factory. Very few workers were spare. In twenty twenty-six, two outside experts reviewed what the Reserve Bank did. They said the new money and the extra government spending were right at first... but, looking back, too strong, for too long.

## Page 12 - The usual cure
So what's the usual cure, when prices go up too fast? The Reserve Bank raises its interest rate. Interest is the extra you pay back when you borrow. Then many loans cost more, home loans included... and people have less to spend. Businesses sell less, and hire fewer people. And prices go up more slowly. Not down... just more slowly. In our picture, the factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate.

## Page 13 - Who pays, here
So who pays for the cure? Here, from late twenty twenty-one, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again and again. Many people paying off loans had to pay more. And the number out of work grew. By the middle of twenty twenty-six, it was about a hundred and seventy thousand people. That's five point six percent... up from three point two. The interest rate isn't the only reason.

## Page 14 - Two views
Banks like our Reserve Bank say the cure works by slowing spending. In their view, putting people out of work is unfortunate... but not the goal. The economist Clara E. /məˈteɪi/, whose book this series follows, sees it differently. She argues that putting people out of work is how the cure works. With more people waiting at the gate, she says, the workers inside have less power to ask for more pay.

## Page 15 - Back at the checkout
And the checkout? In the year to June twenty twenty-six, prices here went up four point one percent. Without the rise in petrol and diesel... it's two point nine. When fuel like petrol and diesel costs more, almost everything that's made or moved costs more too. So prices go up in both factories... with or without new money. That's a rise in costs... not in spending.

## Page 16 - Spent on what?
So... does new money always push prices up? No. When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more things made. When there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up. Next time someone warns that new money will push prices up... ask two questions. Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?
