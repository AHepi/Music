## Chapter 1's narration (as recorded), page by page

1. The speech: "You've heard the speech. Times are tough. Tighten your belts. Cut spending, push up
   interest rates, hold wages down. Sounds like common sense... But austerity has a birthday. Economist
   Clara E. Mattei dug through the archives and found it, right after the First World War. And she
   argues it was never really about budgets. It was about stopping something."
2. The war: "Picture Britain in 1915. The war is burning through shells faster than factories can make
   them. And the government doesn't run out of money... It runs out of shells. So it takes charge: what
   factories make, what things cost, what workers are paid. Italy does the same. And people notice. If
   the economy can be organised for war... why not for what people need, instead of just for profit?"
3. Britain after the war: union membership doubles to eight million; Glasgow, 60,000 in a square for a
   shorter week; troops and tanks sent in; miners demand public ownership, a royal commission backs
   them, the government ignores it.
4. Italy: 1919 more than a million strike; 1920 half a million take over their factories.
5. The capital order: "a few people own the land, the factories and the mines... and everyone else must
   work for a wage to survive. If workers can run things themselves, that order could fall."
6. The experts: Brussels 1920 (39 countries, mostly bankers and treasury officials, not one trade
   unionist), Genoa 1922. Advice: balance the budget, cut public spending, stop creating new money, hand
   interest rates to central banks out of reach of voters, get back on gold.
7. Three parts (Mattei): spending (cut services, tax everyday purchases not wealth), money (make
   borrowing expensive so businesses slow and jobs disappear), work (cut wages, sell off public
   property, weaken unions).
8. The official story: "Prices are rising, war debts are huge, and a government, like a household, has
   to balance its books. Right? But economists who study how modern money works say a country that
   issues its own money isn't a household. It can't run out of its own money. It can run out of
   workers, materials and machines, as the war just showed. And gold? Gold was a limit they chose.
   Going back to it meant forcing wages and prices down."
9. Why: Mattei's answer is power. Take away alternatives (public housing, help for the unemployed) and
   people must take whatever job at whatever wage. "Work more, consume less."
10. Sound familiar: "That's the argument Mattei makes. Austerity was never really about the books. It
    was about power... Sound familiar? But wait. If a country can't run out of its own money... won't
    creating it just cause inflation? That's next."

Chapter 1's last scene: the politician from page 1 returns in the parliament, a stack of money and a
growing price tag appear on "creating it just cause inflation", the word INFLATION? stands on screen.

## Rules for the narration (from the user's rules file)

- Conversational and a little urgent (like Naomi Klein in spirit only: stakes up front, direct
  questions; none of her words). Talk to the viewer ("you").
- Steven Pinker toolkit: open with something the viewer already knows; one plain name for each thing,
  kept all the way through (never a synonym); a concrete case before each general idea; no unexplained
  terms (explain at first use); actors and actions visible; familiar first, new last in each sentence;
  clear links (because, so, but); read it aloud; keep truth and uncertainty; no stock phrases.
- Never say "MMT". Point to the view as "economists who study how modern money works".
- Mattei's interpretations are said as hers ("Mattei argues").
- Every fact must trace to a sourced research entry. Facts only a blocked page could confirm stay out.
- No drawn portraits of real people; real people appear only as typed name cards.
- Words on screen are short forms of what Malina says, never more.
- No words in capitals in the narration text, pauses written as "...".
- Length: about 3 to 4 minutes, 9 to 11 pages of 10 to 30 seconds, roughly 450-600 spoken words
  (Malina reads about 140 words a minute).
