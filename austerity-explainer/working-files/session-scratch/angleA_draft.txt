So... won't creating money just cause inflation? It's the first thing people say, and it's a fair question. Inflation just means prices, in general, going up over time... usually measured over a year. Not one price tag. The whole shopping basket. To answer it, let's go back to where we started... the shell factory.
Britain, 1915. Almost everyone who can work has a job... fewer than one in a hundred are out of work. The war is paid for partly with newly created money. And submarines are sinking ships full of food and materials. By the end of the war, prices have roughly doubled. More money... chasing fewer goods.
So here's the hinge. Picture two shell factories. In the first, machines stand still and workers wait at the gate. New money arrives... the machines start, the workers go in, and more shells come out. The price tag stays put. In the second, every machine is already running flat out. The same money arrives... but no more shells can be made. So buyers bid against each other, and the price tag climbs.
Some things can't be made more of in a hurry at all. In 1915, Glasgow landlords tried to push rents up by a quarter. About twenty thousand households refused to pay... and within weeks, Parliament froze rents. By 1918, sugar, meat and butter were rationed, so scarce food was shared, not bid up.
Let's test the rule. In 2013, Japan's central bank set out to double the money it had created. Prices barely moved... about half a percent a year. Britain's central bank created hundreds of billions of pounds. In 2015, prices weren't rising at all. The new money went into things people own... shares and bonds. And the gains went mostly to the wealthiest.
Now the opposite. Germany, 1923. Foreign soldiers march into its coal and steel heartland. Workers down tools, and the government keeps paying them with newly created money. Money keeps coming... coal and steel stop. By November, one US dollar costs more than four trillion marks. In Zimbabwe, years later, farms and factories collapsed first... then the new money came.
And sometimes it isn't money at all. In 1973, the price of oil nearly quadrupled in three months. Everything made or moved with fuel got dearer. Here, prices rose four point one percent in the year to June. Take fuel out... and it's two point nine.
So yes... creating money can push prices up. When the spending chases workers who are already busy, or things we can't make more of... like land, houses and oil. In 2021, New Zealand house prices jumped about thirty percent in a year. An independent review says the support was right at first... but too strong for too long.
So how do you fight inflation? In April 1920, the Bank of England pushed its interest rate up to seven percent. Within a year, unemployment jumped from about two in a hundred to more than one in ten. Prices did fall... but the price was paid in jobs. Mattei argues that was part of the point. Fewer jobs means workers can't ask for more. Remember the money part of austerity? This is it.
Here, the Reserve Bank pushed its rate from a quarter of a percent to five and a half. Unemployment climbed from three point two percent to five point six. Now it's raising rates again... against a rise that's mostly fuel. So when someone says creating money causes inflation... ask them. Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?
