so... won't creating money just cause inflation? it's a fair question. you've felt it at the checkout... the same basket of shopping costs more than last year. that's inflation... prices in general going up over time. not one price tag... the whole basket. so let's go back to the war... and the shell factory.
britain, 1915. fewer than one in a hundred people are out of work. the war is paid for partly with new money, created through the banks. submarines are sinking ships full of food and materials. so more money is chasing fewer things to buy. by the end of the war... prices have roughly doubled.
picture two factories. in the first, machines stand idle. new money arrives... the machines start, and more shells come out. the price tag mostly stays put. in the second, every machine is already running flat out. the same money arrives... but no more shells can be made. so buyers bid against each other... and the price tag climbs. britain in 1915 was the second factory.
and some things can't be made more of in a hurry... like homes. in 1915, glasgow landlords tried to push rents up by a quarter. about twenty thousand households refused to pay. that november, parliament froze rents.
now test it. in 2013, japan's central bank, which issues the yen, set out to double the money it had created... in two years. prices barely moved... about half a percent a year. britain's central bank created hundreds of billions of pounds. its own study found the new money pushed up the prices of things people own, like shares... and the richest households gained most.
germany, 1923. prices are already rising fast. then french and belgian soldiers march into the ruhr, germany's coal and steel heartland. workers down tools... and the government keeps paying them with new money. by november, one us dollar costs more than four trillion marks. here, new money really did push prices up... while less and less was being made.
and sometimes it's costs, not spending. in 1973, with prices already climbing, the price of oil nearly quadrupled in three months. everything made or moved with fuel got dearer. here, prices rose four point one percent in the year to june. take fuel out... and it's two point nine. inside the reserve bank's target.
so yes... creating money can push prices up, when it's spent where workers are already flat out, or on things we can't make more of in a hurry. in 2021, our unemployment fell to a record low... and house prices jumped about thirty percent in a year. an independent review says the pandemic support was right at first... but too strong for too long.
and the usual cure? in april 1920, the bank of england pushed its interest rate up to seven percent. dearer loans, less spending, lower prices. within a year, unemployment jumped from about two in a hundred to more than one in ten. prices did fall... but people paid with their jobs. mattei argues that was part of the point... the money part of austerity. fewer jobs, and workers can't ask for more.
in new zealand, the reserve bank pushed its interest rate from a quarter of a percent to five and a half. unemployment has climbed from three point two percent to five point six. now it's raising its interest rate again. so when someone says creating money causes inflation... ask: spent on what? and who pays to stop it?
