# Chapter 2, round 20: design review, and a direction for the series

What this is: a close look at the finished chapter 2 film ("20 Chapter 2 - what really causes
inflation.mp4", 4 minutes 30 seconds), page by page and as a whole; what would lift it most; how to
fill the tall frame with the story; and three possible directions for the rest of the series, with
the one I would choose. Times are minutes:seconds into the film. Positions are given as a share of
the frame's height measured from the top (0% is the top edge, 100% the bottom edge), or as a share
of its width.

## In short

**What works**

- **The paintings.** The 1915 munitions hall, the Glasgow tenements in rain, the Ruhr in snow, the
  noodle shop and the 1973 petrol station are atmospheric, right for their period, and share one
  palette (teal shadows, amber light). Each case gets its own place, season and time of day, which
  helps people remember which case is which.
- **The numbers.** Every figure arrives on the word that says it, and the colours mean the same thing
  all the way through (white for the statement, amber for the key part, red for harm). The flip
  counters are the best moments in the film: the marks counter climbing to 4,200,000,000,000 at
  2:25 to 2:29, and the price board flipping £1.00, £1.60, £2.00 at 1:05 to 1:08.
- **The rhymes.** The politician opens and closes the film. The shell factory comes back on page 8.
  The interest dial of Britain in 1920 (3:34) returns in today's New Zealand (3:58), so an object
  carries the chapter's argument: the same cure, then and now. The hundred dots of 1915 (0:24)
  return for New Zealand (4:11).
- **The camera.** It holds still (20 stretches of 2 to 5 seconds with no movement at all) and its
  moves mean something: it pushes in on the price board at "prices barely move" (0:55) and "prices
  climb" (1:05).
- **Light used as a fact, once.** At 0:52 to 0:53 the idle factory's windows light up as the workers
  go in. It is the best idea in the film, and it is used only once.

**The three biggest problems**

1. **It looks like a collage of five kinds of image**, held together only by the film's colour
   finish (teal shadows, warm highlights): painted rooms, cut-outs with white paper edges, smooth card
   models, detailed Blender models, and see-through chart boxes. The card models are the weak link.
   The banknote bundles read as pale green bricks or soap (0:30 to 0:42, 1:40 to 2:07, 2:23 to 2:35),
   the shell is a glossy gold bullet (0:18), the coins look like washers (0:50 to 1:07), and the house
   frame (1:32 to 1:34) is the same "sparse in detail" problem you named for the card house in round
   19. Every time one appears, the image drops in quality.
2. **The story sits in a middle band and the bottom of the frame is floor.** In most shots the words
   sit 10 to 25% down, the subject 35 to 80%, and the bottom 20 to 35% is floor, street, conveyor
   belt or snow with nothing in it that belongs to the line being spoken. Measured on one frame per
   second across the film: the bottom tenth of the frame is about half as bright as the middle, and
   in 72% of frames the bottom fifth is more than 30% darker than the middle. The cause is built in:
   the painting prompts ask for "standing eye level" and "the lower quarter of the picture is the
   empty floor/street", a rule made for chapter 1, where cut-outs stood in front of a flat page.
3. **People are props.** The chapter's point is who pays, but people appear as one strip of figures
   copied many times (1:17 to 1:20; about 25 copies at 3:42 to 3:57, the chapter's moral peak), as
   heads cut off by the bottom edge (0:23 to 0:29), or not at all: the New Zealand street, while
   Malina talks about unemployment, is empty (3:11 to 3:18 and 4:09 to 4:15).

**The direction I would choose: "the house"**

Stage each chapter in one tall building of society, cut open from roof to cellar like a doll's
house and seen from the front. The top floor is where austerity is decided (the Treasury, the
central bank's vault, the economists' desks), the middle floors are what public money pays for (a
classroom, a clinic, council homes being built), and the bottom floors are work and home (the
factory floor, a family's kitchen, the mine). Austerity is decided at the top and lands at the
bottom, so the tall frame is full by its very shape and every shot shows who decides and who pays.
Lights going out, rooms changing hands and weight pressing down through the floors carry the facts.
It suits your camera grammar exactly (pan up to the experts' promises, pan down to the pressure),
it keeps real 3D depth without the tearing of painted rooms, and it costs about as much to film as
today's painted pages. Two other directions, "the money machine" and "the pop-up book", are set
out in section 5 for comparison.

**A first step that costs nothing:** one test shot of the British house for chapter 3's 1922 cuts,
built from plain coloured room boxes and chapter 1's existing cut-outs, lit at night, with one move
down from the vault to the mine. If it reads well as a still and in motion, paint the room walls
(about ten small paintings, about 25 US cents, only with your yes).

## 1. The film as a whole

### 1.1 One world, or a collage?

A concrete example first. At 2:23 ("and keeps paying them, with new money"), banknotes are thrown
onto the Ruhr's snow. Some are painted cut-outs with white paper edges; others are plain olive
blocks with a yellow band; one falling note shows as a black slab (2:23.1 to 2:23.7).
Behind them is a painting with brushwork, and on top sits a hook in clean type. Four of the film's
five kinds of image meet in one second, and the eye notices the joins before the meaning.

Across the film there are five kinds of image:

1. **Painted rooms**: brushwork, soft painted light. Every page.
2. **Cut-outs**: painted like the rooms, but with a white paper edge (the politician, the basket, the
   women munition workers, the queues, the rent strikers, the soldiers, the ramen bowl, the bank,
   the book, the iron weight).
3. **Card models**: smooth, untextured, lit by the stage lamp (the shell, banknote bundles, paper
   boats, coins, price tags, notice board, house frame, oil barrel, dial, villa).
4. **Blender models**: detailed, realistic surfaces, a miniature look (the works and town on pages 3
   and 8, the desk on page 8).
5. **Flat graphics**: hooks, labels, counters, and charts in see-through dark panels.

Where they sit together well: painted rooms with painted cut-outs and plain words. The rent strikers
in the rain (1:25 to 1:31), the soldiers marching through the snow (2:14 to 2:18) and the ramen bowl
on the counter (1:35 to 1:45) look like one world. The Blender model town on pages 3 and 8 is also
one world, a lit miniature, just a different world from the painted pages.

Where they don't: every card model inside a painting.

| Time | Card model | What it reads as on a phone |
|---|---|---|
| 0:18 to 0:19 | the shell on the checkout belt | a glossy gold rifle bullet (the exact confusion chapter 1 fixed by repainting its shells as 18-pounders) |
| 0:30 to 0:42, 1:40 to 2:07, 2:23 to 2:35 | banknote bundles | pale green or olive bricks, a bar of soap; no printing visible |
| 0:31 to 0:33 | paper boats | flat grey triangles on a dark blue pool painted on the factory floor |
| 0:50 to 1:07, 2:05 to 2:07, 3:30 to 3:40 | coins | flat gold discs with a dark centre, like washers |
| 1:21 to 1:31 | the rent notice | a plain brown box on two posts standing in the road |
| 1:32 to 1:34 | the house frame | plain timber and bright red bricks with white mortar on a grey slab |
| 2:41 to 2:50 | the oil barrel | clean, glossy, no wear, beside a painted station |
| 3:15 to 3:18 | the villa in the New Zealand street | a toy house on a lawn base, at a different scale and finish from the painted houses behind it |

The white paper edges are a second, quieter split. On dark scenes they glow like stickers (the
queue at 3:42 to 3:57, the workers at 0:48 to 0:51). They say "paper on a page", while the painted
rooms and the Blender models say "film set". Chapter 1 earned the white edges because everything
there was paper on a page.

From page to page, the colour finish and the type never change, and places come back (parliament
on pages 1 and 10, the factory on pages 3 and 8, the New Zealand street on pages 7, 8 and 10), which
holds the chapter together. What changes from page to page is the material, and that is what the
eye trips on.

What this means: the three drops in quality you pointed out after round 19 (the sparse card house,
the plain factories, the plain report) all had the same source: plain card models among detailed
paintings. Round 20 replaced those three with Blender models, but the same kind of object is still
used elsewhere (the table above).

### 1.2 Composition in the tall frame

A concrete example first. The Ruhr page opens (2:08 to 2:14) with "GERMANY 1923" and "PRICES RACING"
in the top quarter and the counter at 33%, while the bottom 40% of the frame is snow and rail tracks
with nothing on them for six seconds, as Malina says "Prices are already racing."

That is the pattern of the whole film:

- **Words in the top quarter, the subject in a middle band, floor at the bottom.** Measured on grid
  overlays of 36 frames: hooks and titles sit 10 to 25% down; the subject sits between 35 and 80%;
  the bottom 20 to 35% is floor, street, belt or snow. The longest cases: 0:20 to 0:23 (factory
  floor, 60 to 100%), 0:46 to 1:07 (road and kerb, 65 to 100%, for 21 seconds), 1:14 to 1:17
  (cobbles, 50 to 100%), 2:08 to 2:14 (snow, 60 to 100%), 3:11 to 3:14 (footpath and road, 45 to
  100%), 4:09 to 4:15 (road, 75 to 100%, with the dial shrunk to a fifth of the width).
- **The bottom is dark as well as empty.** Average brightness by tenth of the frame, over one frame
  per second: between 74 and 82 (out of 255) in every band from 10 to 70%, then 69 at 70 to 80%, 53
  at 80 to 90%, and 37 at the bottom tenth. The film's darkened corners add to this.
- **The cause is in the recipe.** The painting prompts ask for "standing eye level" and "the lower
  quarter of the picture is the empty floor/street". At eye level in a tall frame the horizon lands
  near the middle, so half the frame is ground. The phone rule keeps words out of the bottom 380
  pixels (the bottom 20%), and nothing else was given that band to do.
- **Round 20's fill rule was applied as decoration, not story.** On the model-town pages the bottom
  corners are filled with blurred barrels, crates and a cart (0:43 to 0:45, 1:08 to 1:13). They fill
  the corners, but on a phone they read as a brown blur and say nothing. On the painted pages the
  subject is often far below the rule's "60 to 80% of the width": one banknote bundle at 13% of the
  width (0:30), the paper boats at 10 to 15% (0:31 to 0:33), the basket at 6% (0:36 to 0:41).
- **The same shape again and again.** Eight of the thirteen places are one-point views: parliament,
  the checkout, the munitions hall, the tenements, the Ruhr gate, the 1920 street, the factory gate
  and the New Zealand street. With the subject centred in most of them (the politician, the notice
  board, the house frame, the barrel, the dial), each frame is strong alone and the film feels
  same-shaped in a row. The only changes of angle are the raised view of the model town and the
  top-down desk (3:19 to 3:27), which is a welcome break.

Balance follows from this. A tall frame feels stable when it is heavy at the bottom (big, close
shapes), lighter in the middle (the subject) and lightest at the top (sky and words). Here the
bottom is dark but holds no shapes, so it reads as a hole rather than a base, and the frames float.

### 1.3 Depth, scale, light, colour and texture

- **Depth comes from focus blur and layers, not from the painted rooms.** Nothing at all moves in 60%
  of the film's 8,110 frames, and the camera holds still for most of the rest; a still camera cannot
  show the depth of a painted room. Depth reads through the soft foreground and background, through
  cut-outs overlapping the painting, and through light beams and haze. On the sideways move at the
  petrol station (2:39.5 to 2:40.9), the canopy edge bends and the queued cars smear: the known
  tearing.
- **Scale is used well when it means something.** The book at 3:50 is big because it is the
  authority (your round-20 note), and the shell on the belt is big as a symbol. It is used badly when
  the subject is the point but tiny: the basket at 0:36 to 0:41, the queue at 3:43.
- **The colours are consistent and readable**: teal and amber everywhere, red kept for harm ("PRICES
  UP", "7%", "TOO STRONG, FOR TOO LONG", the 11% bar). The one clash is the red title over the green
  desk (3:24 to 3:27).
- **The light sometimes argues against the line.** I measured the average brightness and warmth (red
  minus blue) of each place. The New Zealand street on page 10, where Malina says rates went up and
  unemployment climbed, is the second warmest place in the film (warmth +43, brightness 87), behind
  only the cosy noodle shop (+51). The 1920 street, where the cure begins, is the coldest place in
  the film (-5), and the factory gate where the queue forms is among the coldest (+1). The chapter
  argues that today rhymes with 1920, but its light says today is golden.
- **Texture splits the same way as the materials.** The paintings and cut-outs have brushwork; the
  card models have none; the Blender models have realistic brick and wood. The copy you were sent is
  compressed, which smooths the film grain away, so the smooth card models stand out even more.

### 1.4 Words on screen

- **Good:** the hooks (heavy white and amber type) are clear; the place titles with their thin amber
  rule have a documentary feel; the big figures ("3.2%" is about 170 pixels tall) read instantly; the
  counters and the hundred dots are excellent; the way Mattei's argument is marked ("MATTEI:" in
  amber above the hook, with "Clara E. Mattei, The Capital Order (2022)" beneath, 3:50 to 3:57) is
  exactly right and should be the pattern for chapter 3.
- **Too many words.** I counted about 110 separate word items (hooks, titles, labels, chart words) in
  4 minutes 30, one every 2.4 seconds. Chapter 1's approved test page had four hooks in 23 seconds
  (8 MILLION, 60,000, PUBLIC OWNERSHIP, IGNORED). Many chapter 2 hooks repeat the narration almost
  word for word ("NOT ONE PRICE TAG", "WHAT REALLY DRIVES IT?", "BACK TO THE SHELL FACTORY",
  "PRICES ALREADY CLIMBING", "WITHIN A YEAR", "SINCE THE END OF 2021"), which your own style rules
  warn turns hooks into subtitles. Phone apps then add their own captions on top.
- **Small words are too small for a phone.** "EXAMPLE PRICES" is about 19 pixels tall, "FACTORY 1 ·
  SPARE CAPACITY" about 29 and the place titles about 33: roughly 1.1 to 2 mm on a typical phone,
  and softer still after compression. Anything meant to be read needs about 36 to 40 pixels.
- **Panels look like television news.** The see-through dark boxes (1:46 to 1:52, 2:52 to 2:59, 3:44
  to 3:48, 4:11 to 4:15) are the most "digital" thing in the film. Your style rules already say that
  printed things belong on objects (cards, signs, boards).
- **Hooks over bright, busy paint** rely on their soft shadows: "BRITAIN AT WAR" in white on a pale
  sky (1:08 to 1:10), "PRICES ALREADY CLIMBING" over the lit canopy with a lamp glowing behind
  "CLIMBING" (2:38).

### 1.5 Motion, pacing, cuts and page turns

- **The rhythm of arrivals works.** Something new lands every second or two, on the word it belongs
  to; hooks pop with a small bounce and cut-outs fold up. That keeps attention.
- **But the image freezes between arrivals.** On pages 2, 3, 5, 7 and 10, between 73 and 82% of
  frames are identical to the frame before: the camera holds still, as you asked, but so does the
  world. Pages 4 and 6 hardly ever freeze (1% and 8% of frames), because rain and snow keep falling.
  That is the lesson: a still camera can look at a living world.
- **Nine dips to black** inside pages (0:05, 1:32, 1:53, 2:24, 2:51, 3:10, 3:18, 3:41, 4:19), as well
  as the nine page turns. A book doesn't go black between pages; a hard cut on a word (already
  used at 0:45, 0:56 and 3:19) keeps the pace and the book idea.
- **The page turns** (0:20, 0:42, 1:14, 1:34, 2:08, 2:35, 2:59, 3:27, 3:57) are quick and clean, a
  corner peel of 0.85 seconds showing a plain warm grey back. They are always the same and carry
  nothing; the back of the page is empty space you could use.

### 1.6 What to keep: the series' vocabulary

The flip counters; the hundred (dots that are workers); the stamps (RENTS FROZEN, TOO STRONG, FOR TOO
LONG); the interest dial that travels across a century; the red book as Mattei's authority, with the
"MATTEI:" credit; the politician as the voice of "common sense" at both ends; windows lighting up as
a fact; rain and snow; the place-and-year titles; the page turn as the thread back to chapter 1's
book.

## 2. Page by page

**Page 1, the question (0:00 to 0:20).** She asks whether new money just causes inflation, shows it
at the checkout, and sends us back to the shell factory.
- Right: the politician (0:00 to 0:05) is a strong, centred opening, filling 41 to 92% of the height

## 3. What would lift it most, ranked

Cost words: a **cheap tweak** is a change to a page file with no new paintings; a **moderate
rebuild** means new pieces or models and a page rebuilt; a **new approach** means a new way of
building sets for the series.

|---|---|---|---|---|

If chapter 2 is ever filmed again, ranks 4, 5, 7 and 9 could be done in a day of page edits at no
cost. Ranks 1 to 3 are the real lessons for chapter 3. The new approach, a kind of set built for the
tall frame from the start, is section 5.

## 4. Filling the tall frame with the story

### 4.1 Give every band a job

| Band (from the top) | What the phone does there | Its job | A chapter 2 moment that already does it |

### 4.2 Working rules

1. **Fill with consequences, not decoration.** The filler must be the cause or the effect of the line
   being spoken. At "workers wait at the gate", the bottom band is the waiting workers, not barrels.
2. **People in front.** The nearest band is usually people. It is where the viewer stands, and it
   sits under the captions, where words can't go but faces can.
3. **Make the subject big.** The thing she names fills 50 to 80% of the width when she names it. Keep
   a small subject in a big room only when smallness is the point ("fewer things").
4. **Let light and state change.** Windows lit or dark, smoke or none, lamps warm or cold: the image
   should change when the fact changes. The factory windows at 0:53 are the model.
5. **Put numbers on things.** Print figures on objects that belong to the place (a price board, the
   petrol station's sign, a ledger, a blackboard, a newspaper bill, a calendar). Keep panels for true
   comparisons only.
6. **No copies.** Every visible person is a different figure. Paint crowd sheets with many different
   people; reuse figures only in far, soft layers.
7. **Change the angle.** Alternate frontal views (façades, cut-open buildings), top-down (desks,
   maps, ledgers), low (looking up at power) and high (looking down on pressure). No more than two
   one-point views in a row.
8. **A still camera, a living world.** Every held shot has one slow movement that belongs to the
   place: smoke, steam, rain, snow, a flickering lamp, dust in a light beam.
9. **Keep the headline area calm.** Put hooks over sky, a wall or a soft dark band, never over the
   brightest, busiest paint. Keep words out of the phone's margins, but put story in them.
10. **Fewer, bigger words.** One hook per beat; numbers and names first; nothing that repeats the
    sentence she is saying.

## 5. Three directions for the series

### What any direction has to do

- Carry Mattei's argument, not just decorate it: who decides, who pays, and that austerity was a
  choice that experts presented as necessity (Mattei argues).
- Fill the tall frame by its own logic, so the filling is never decoration.
- Look like one world.
- Keep your rules: the camera still unless a move lands a point, at most three moves a shot; few
  words; real people only as typed name cards; no political party colours; no hands.
- Grow across the chapters still to come: Britain and Italy in the 1920s, Thatcher, why nations need
  social spending to develop, New Zealand and Australia, what austerity did to people, why you lose
  bargaining power, why it is oppressive.
- Be buildable on this machine (no graphics card, software drawing) with about US$3 left for paintings.

### Direction A: the house of the capital order (my choice)

**The idea.** Each chapter is staged in one tall building of society, cut open from roof to cellar
like a doll's house and seen from the front, a little to one side, so every room shows its depth,
with the street and its people in front and the city's lights behind. It is plainly a symbol (no
real building is shown or implied). Mattei argues that austerity was decided by a few and paid for
by working people, to protect what she calls "the capital order" (a world where a few own the land,
factories and mines); the house turns that argument into architecture. The top floor is where
decisions are made (the Treasury, the central bank's vault, the economists' desks with typed name
cards); the middle floors are what public money pays for (a classroom, a clinic, council homes being
built, a public company's office); the ground floor and cellar are work and home (the factory floor,
a family's kitchen with its pay packet, the mine). Austerity acts on the building physically: weight
(gold bars, chapter 1's iron weight) presses down through a central column; rooms go dark when they
are cut; when a public company is sold off, its sign is swapped and the whole room slides up into
the owners' floor; a pipe carries money upward. The lit windows are the data: a hundred windows on
the façade are a hundred households, the hundred dots made into homes. Each chapter can open on the
façade at night, its windows lit, before the front folds down like a page to show the rooms.

**Three example frames (chapter 3)**

*A1. Britain, 1922: the cuts (the whole building, a still shot).*
- 0 to 10%: night sky with chimney smoke; the place title "BRITAIN 1922" at about 9%, left.
- 10 to 32%, the top floor: on the left, a committee room with a long table and typed name cards
  (the names from the chapter 3 research); on the right, the Bank's vault, dim (Britain is not yet
  back on gold).
- 32 to 66%, two middle storeys of three rooms each: a classroom with children at desks, a clinic,
  a council-house building site with scaffolding and bricklayers, all warm-lit.
- 66 to 84%, the ground floor: a factory floor with a lathe and workers; a kitchen, a family at the
  table with the pay packet.
- 84 to 100%, the cellar: the coal seam, miners' lamps glinting. Faces and lamps sit under the
  phone's captions, where words can't go.
- On "cuts", an axe crashes down through the roof into the middle storeys (a short shake): the
  building site goes dark and its scaffold folds flat, the classroom dims to half, a typed card
  slides onto the site's door (wording from the research). Hook "CUT" in the sky.
- Why: one still frame shows who decided (top), what was lost (middle), and who is next (bottom).
  No band is empty, because every band is a level of society. The axe is the period's own image:
  the 1922 cuts were nicknamed "the Geddes Axe".

*A2. 1925 to 1926: back to gold, then the strike (same building; a pan down).*
- On "back to gold", gold bars land in the vault (top right, a glint and a small shake). The iron
  column running down the middle of the building takes the weight: dust falls in the rooms below,
  and the cellar's ceiling lowers, so the mine shrinks from about 16% to 12% of the frame's height.
  The miners' pay packets are swapped for smaller ones (chapter 1's way of showing a cut). Hook "BACK
  ON GOLD, 1925".
- Then 1926: the factory floor and the lower rooms empty as their people walk out into the street in
  front of the house, filling the bottom 20% with caps, faces and blank placards; the lower windows go
  dark because work has stopped. After nine days the crowd folds away, except the miners, who stay
  outside the dark mine while a calendar on the mine wall flips through the months.
- Why: the cause (gold at the top) and the cost (wages at the bottom) are joined by something you can
  see, and the strike is the bottom of the building stopping. The move from vault to mine is your
  "pan down to signal pressure".

*A3. Italy: the same medicine (two half-buildings, one above the other).*
- 0 to 47%: the British house, smaller. 53 to 100%: an Italian building cut open (arcades at street
  level, a ministry upstairs, a telephone exchange, a factory). Between them, at about 50%, the hook
  "SAME ADVICE".
- The same three things happen in both, one after another, on her words: wages (pay packets swapped
  for smaller ones on both ground floors); selling off (in the Italian building a public company's
  sign is swapped for SOLD and its room slides up a floor into the owners' rooms); taxes (on the
  Italian top floor a typed ledger line for a tax on the rich is struck through, while a stamp lands
  on the ground floor for taxes on what everyone buys; the exact taxes from the research).
- Typed name cards on the Italian top floor: "ALBERTO DE STEFANI, FINANCE MINISTER", and on a desk
  piled with books, the economists Mattei names (for example Maffeo Pantaleoni and Luigi Einaudi, to
  be confirmed in the chapter 3 research), under the credit "MATTEI:" with her book.
- Why: Mattei's comparison, that a democracy and a dictatorship applied the same austerity, becomes a
  single frame, and a stacked comparison is the natural way to compare on a tall screen.

**Light and colour for chapter 3.** Use the share of warm light in the building as the chapter's
graph. In 1919 to 1921 nearly every room glows; in 1922 the middle storeys dim; in 1925 a cold gold
glint appears at the top while the cellar darkens; in 1926 the street flares with lamp-lit people,
then goes dark. Italy starts in warm ochre stucco and arcades at street level, with a cool ministry
above; as wages fall and taxes move, the ground floor's light thins while the top floor's warms. Keep
strikes in amber lamplight, not red, and avoid black as a colour code in Italy, so no party colours
creep in; keep red for harm in the words, as now. Keep any symbol of the regime out of the paintings,
as your painting rules already require.

**How it grows through the series.**
- Thatcher: the same British house sixty years on, with the same mine in the cellar (1926 and 1984
  rhyme); council homes change hands; public companies' rooms slide up; a lock appears on the union
  room.
- Why nations need social spending to develop: the middle floors are load-bearing. Building schools,
  roads and power adds floors and steadies the house; cutting them makes it sag.
- New Zealand and Australia: two houses stacked in one frame, each with its own architecture (villas
  and state houses), the 1980s and 1990s changes as rooms sold and lights dimmed.
- What austerity did to people: the camera moves down into the rooms, the kitchens, clinics and the
  dole office (your "move down to expose the minor details").
- Why you lose bargaining power: the workers' floor; the door upstairs locks; the queue outside the
  factory is the weight on their wages.
- Why it is oppressive: the whole house, the top floor's controls, the ceilings lowering.

**What it demands from this machine.**
- Build: each building is 9 to 12 shallow rooms, real 3D boxes, so the camera sees true depth. Each
  back wall is a flat painting, and a flat painting on a flat wall never tears when the camera moves;
  only paintings pretending to be deep spaces tear. Props are cut-outs, card models dressed in
  painted surfaces, or Blender models (furniture and machines from the 3D tools being looked into in
  parallel would fit straight in). One shadow-casting lamp for the whole building, and a cheap warm
  glow per room instead of more shadows.
- Filming: about 3 to 5 seconds a frame, close to today's painted pages, so a 4.5-minute chapter in
  about 4 to 6 hours with two drawn at a time, as now. Heavy Blender props in many rooms would push it
  towards the 12 to 16 seconds of the model-town pages; keep them for a few hero rooms. Since the
  camera holds still for most of each shot, drawing a still shot's unchanging background once and only
  redrawing what moves could cut filming time a lot (a change to the engine).
- Paintings: per building about 10 room walls at 1k (about 25 US cents), a façade or sky at 2k
  (about 10 cents) and 15 or so new people and objects at 1k (about 40 cents): about 75 cents a
  building, so about US$1.50 for chapter 3's two, out of the roughly US$3 left, and only with your
  yes. Many of chapter 1's cut-outs already fit 1920s Britain and Italy (miners, crowds, pithead,
  council houses, pay packet, iron weight, gold bars, scissors, the bank, the map of Italy).
- Risks: the whole building in every shot would feel like a diagram. Use it for openings, turning
  points and endings (about a third of the shots) and go into single rooms for the rest; each room is
  a small stage, filmed like chapter 2's best shots. Rooms must share one scale and one light.

### Direction B: the money machine

**The idea.** A tall machine of glass tanks, pipes and brass valves in which money is a warm, glowing
amber liquid, inspired by the water machine of the economy that the New Zealand economist Bill
Phillips built in 1949 (one is said to be on show at the Reserve Bank of New Zealand's museum in
Wellington; to be confirmed). The tanks are what a country can make, its workers, materials and
machines. Pour money into a tank with room and it fills: more jobs, more things. Pour into a full
tank and it overflows into the prices gauge: inflation. That is chapter 2's whole argument in one
image. Austerity's levers are valves: close the spending tap, open the interest drain, lock the gold
reservoir, drain the wages tank into the owners' tank. Every valve handle carries a typed name card
of who turned it, so the machine never looks natural or neutral, which matters because Mattei argues
the experts dressed their choices up as laws of nature.

**Three example frames**

*B1. Chapter 2's two factories, told again.* 5 to 25%: a tap marked "NEW MONEY". 25 to 70%: two glass
tanks side by side; the left one a third full, with idle workers standing in its empty space; the
right one full to the brim, workers busy. 70 to 92%: a tall prices gauge. The same amber stream falls
into both: the left tank's level rises and its workers start (its lamps come on); the right one
overflows into the gauge, which climbs in red. Why: it shows in one frame what page 3 needed two
places and 31 seconds to say.

*B2. 1925: the gold reservoir.* At the top (5 to 30%), a sealed glass reservoir of gold behind chapter
1's padlock, its outlet valve fixed to the size of the gold; typed name cards hang on the valve (the
Chancellor and the Bank; names from the research). Below (30 to 75%), the outflow is throttled: the
wages tank (bottom left) sinks while the owners' tank (bottom right) holds. In the bottom band,
miners stand inside the wages tank as its level falls past their knees. Hook "BACK ON GOLD, 1925".
Why: the gold standard appears as a choice with names on it, and its cost is a level you can watch
falling around the miners.

*B3. Italy: the tax pipes.* The pipe that drained the top tank (the rich) is capped, with a typed tag
for the tax that was cut; a new pipe drains the bottom tanks (taxes on what everyone buys); a public
company's tank unbolts itself and slides across to the owners' side with a SOLD tag; De Stefani's
typed name card hangs by the valves, the economists' cards beside it under the "MATTEI:" credit.
Why: "taxes moved off the rich" becomes a re-plumbing you can follow with your eye, and the names
keep it a decision rather than a law of nature.

**How it grows.** Development: public investment builds bigger tanks, so more money can flow
without overflowing (chapter 2's warning turned into a promise). Thatcher: pipes rerouted from
public tanks into private ones. New Zealand and Australia: the machine is a New Zealander's idea, a
natural homecoming. People and bargaining power: the wages tank's level is bargaining power, and the
unemployment tank fills as the interest drain opens. Oppression: valves locked, names on them.

**What it demands.** One set, built once in Blender (cylinders, pipes, valves and gauges are simple
to make detailed by script) and reused in every chapter. The liquid is faked cheaply (glowing
surfaces whose height changes, a few rising bubbles), with no real fluid simulation. Glass needs
see-through drawing, which software drawing handles slowly: about 5 to 9 hours to film a chapter,
more with the works' realistic metal surfaces. Paintings: almost none (some people as cut-outs).
Risks: it is abstract, a machine rather than a story, close to the "lecture" feeling you didn't want
in chapter 1's first script; it can make austerity look like plumbing, natural and neutral, which is
the view Mattei argues against, so the names on the valves and the people in the tanks must always be
visible; and it cannot carry the human scenes on its own, so it would alternate with painted places.

### Direction C: the pop-up book

**The idea.** Go back to the paper world chapter 1 began, done the way you described for the
one-page picture ("an open book... the pop ups have a lot of intricate details so the shading sells
the drama"), but as a tall book: the upper page stands up as the backdrop and the lower page lies
flat towards us as the stage, printed with small columns of type and footnotes (the sources). Every
scene rises from the fold in several layers of cut paper with real thickness and shadows. Everything
is paper, even the charts: strips that slide out of slots, wheels turning in windows, flaps that lift.
Austerity's acts become paper acts: cutting (chapter 1's scissors, an axe), folding flat (homes and
schools fold down), locking (a gold clasp shuts the book); resistance pops up. One reading lamp, deep
shadows.

**Three example frames (chapter 3)**

*C1. 1922: cuts are cuts.* 0 to 50%: the upper page, a painted dusk sky with chimneys. 45 to 62%: at
the fold, a row of new council homes (chapter 1's council-houses cut-out) with warm windows, 90% of
the width. 62 to 100%: the lower page, printed type and a paper tab marked SPENDING in a slot. On
"cut", the steel scissors crash in from the top and snip the homes at their hinge; they fall flat
towards us one after another, showing their plain backs. Title "BRITAIN 1922" at 9%; hook "CUT" at
18%. Why: the cuts are literal, and the flattened homes stay lying on the page as a record of what
was lost.

*C2. 1925: the gold clasp.* The book's brass clasp swings over the spread and locks; gold bars pile on
the top edge of the upper page, which leans forward under their weight, so the space at the fold,
where the pithead and the miners stand (40 to 75%), narrows; on the lower page a paper wheel in a
window turns the miners' wage down (the figure from the research). Why: gold is both a lock and a
weight, chapter 1's "a limit they chose", and the shrinking gap at the fold is the miners' room to
live.

*C3. 1926: the strike rises and falls.* Every crowd cut-out rises across the spread at once (chapter
1's Glasgow crowds: the back row at the fold, the front row over the lower page with heads crossing
the bottom edge); a paper wheel counts the days. Then the crowd folds flat row by row, leaving only
the miners standing while the printed calendar on the lower page turns its months. Why: solidarity
rising and collapsing, told with paper alone, and the calendar shows how long the miners held out.

**How it grows.** Every chapter is a chapter of the same book. Thatcher: pages torn out and sold. Why
social spending develops nations: pop-ups that raise each other (a school pops up a workforce, which
pops up a factory). People and bargaining power: chapter 1's paper chain of people, and the scissors.
Oppression: the book shut under its clasp.

**What it demands.** The cheapest: flat paper layers with real shadows. Chapter 1's paper stage drew
about 0.85 seconds a frame; with focus blur and glow, perhaps 1 to 2 seconds, so a chapter in about 2
to 4 hours. No painted rooms, so no tearing, and sideways moves are free. Paintings: reuse many of
chapter 1's 47; perhaps 4 backdrops at 2k and 20 pieces at 1k, about 90 US cents. Risks: it could
feel like a step back from chapter 2's 3D, which you liked ("I love the fact theirs depth in these");
it only looks rich with intricate, many-layered pop-ups and strong light, which takes design time.

### Which I would choose, and why

**Direction A, the house.**

- It fills the tall frame by its shape, and the filling is always the argument: every band is a
  level of society, so nothing is ever "just floor".
- It carries Mattei's argument in every shot (who decides, who pays, where the cuts land) and grows
  naturally through all the chapters still to come, with the same grammar each time, so viewers learn
  to read it.
- It uses your camera grammar literally: pan up to the experts and their promises, pan down to the
  pressure, move up for the whole building, move down into one room.
- It keeps real 3D depth, which you asked for, and stops the tearing, because paintings become flat
  walls inside real rooms instead of pretend-deep spaces.
- It is affordable here: filming close to today's painted pages, about US$1.50 of paintings for
  chapter 3, and it can use whatever the 3D tool search finds for furniture and machines without
  depending on it.

What I would borrow from the others: from C, the paper actions for the big moments (the axe cut, rooms
folding flat, a crowd popping up), which keep the thread to chapter 1; from B, one pipe or gauge in
the house for the moments that explain how money moves. I would not mix all three equally; one
building grammar, used consistently, is what will make the series recognisable.
