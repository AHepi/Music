# 30 Why it works - checked reasons and rebuild plan

What this file is: the reasons independent critics gave for why "30 Test - composed by Nano Banana - own camera"
works, after a skeptic checked each one against the picture and the measurements (log 30, decision 36). Positions
are fractions of the frame (0 = left or top, 1 = right or bottom) or pixels of the 1536 x 2752 picture. The plain
rules drawn from them are in `film-kit/07 Composing a shot.md`.

## Reasons kept

1. **Every line of the street leads to one corner of the picture: the spot where the shut colliery gate meets the notice board. The notice is the bright thing that sits there.** (main)
   - Evidence: I traced the lines myself. Pixel positions are on the 1536x2752 original; fractions run 0 = left/top to 1 = right/bottom. The far part of the right kerb, from (1030,1300) to (890,1700), and the far left kerb, from (700,1340) to (800,1295), cross at about (1085,1167), which is 0.71 across and 0.42 down. The left house gutters cross a little further left, at about 0.62 to 0.68. The near kerb bends around the drum and points a little lower. The automatic finder put the point at 0.69 across, 0.49 down. So the lines meet in a small zone rather than at one exact point: 0.62 to 0.71 across, 0.42 to 0.49 down. That zone is exactly where the right end of the gate (x 795 to 1055) passes behind the board's left post (x of about 1060). The paper's left edge (0.745 across) and top edge (0.455 down) are a hand's width away. For comparison, in your clip's frame 4 the same lines meet at about 0.14 across, 0.59 down, inside the black colliery doorway at the far left, while the notice sits at 0.79 to 0.90 across. In the empty-street painting they meet at the gate, at about 0.27 across, 0.59 down.
   - For the picture: The big, quiet left two-thirds (road, kerbs, house fronts) is not empty space. It works as a set of arrows aimed at the small, busy right-hand group, so the large side helps the small side instead of fighting it. In your clip the arrows point at the door on the far left, so the props on the right have nothing pointing at them.
   - For the story: The road every miner walked to work ends at a shut gate, and the paper explaining why it is shut stands right next to it. The place and the reason meet at one spot.

2. **The model moved the camera off the road and onto the right-hand pavement, at a standing person's eye height, held level. That one decision causes most of what follows: where the lines meet, how big the notice is, and why we can see into the drum and onto the seat.** (main)
   - Evidence: The strip of pavement directly in front of the camera shows up in the picture as a straight vertical line under the point where the street's lines meet, at about 0.70 across. At the bottom edge, that line lands on flagstones. The cobbles stop at about x 300 and the kerb stones run from x 300 to 520 (0.2 to 0.34 across), so the camera is standing on the pavement, to the right of the kerb. Eye level is at 0.42 to 0.46 down. That is well above the drum's rim (0.66) and the top of the chair back (0.60), so we look down into the drum (the fire inside is visible), onto the chair seat and into the pail. The lamp post, board posts, drainpipes and chimney all stay upright, so the camera is not tilted. The empty-street painting and your clip both keep the camera in the road (lines meet at 0.27 and 0.14 across). The notice fills 3.3% of this picture against about 0.95% of clip frame 4.
   - For the picture: A straight-ahead corridor becomes a slanting road that runs from the bottom-left corner toward the group. The props get a floor of near pavement to stand on, and their tops show, so the fire, the seat and the lantern read clearly instead of as dark side-on shapes.
   - For the story: You stand where a villager would stand to read the notice, an arm's length from the fire. Nobody stands in the middle of a road, so the clip's viewpoint belongs to no one.

3. **The props near the notice touch and overlap, so they read as one group. In your clip each prop sits on its own patch of bare cobbles.** (main)
   - Evidence: The top-left corner of the chair back (about x 1350 to 1490, y 1660 to 1700) covers the paper's lower-right corner (the paper's right edge is at x of about 1395, its bottom at about 1780). The lantern stands on the seat, and its top ring sits against the board's bottom rail at about (1240,1790). The pail stands in front of the chair's back legs. The drum's top-right rim (1048,1810) almost touches the board's bottom-left corner (1060,1830). In clip frame 4 the sacks (bottom left, about 7% of the frame by their box), the drum (alone in the road), the coal heap (on the bottom edge) and the board (back against the wall) have bare cobbles between them. Only the drum's edge reaches the board's leg.
   - For the picture: One group gives the eye one place to go, so the rest of the frame can stay quiet. Props spread out one by one make the eye hop between them, and the cobbles in between look like left-over space.
   - For the story: Props that touch read like a sentence: someone sat here, by a fire, with a lamp, next to the notice. Props spaced apart read like a list of nouns.

## Claims rejected by the skeptic

- Key things sit on the one-third lines and their crossings (drum rim on the lower line, the lower-right crossing between the fire and the lantern), and the left one-third line is left empty. Rejected: The positions are roughly right, but this holds for most pictures and does not separate this one from your clip, which also puts the drum (about 0.7 across) and the notice (about 0.85) near those lines. The drum rim landing on the line looks like chance.
- 73% of the warm light is in the right third, and that explains it. Rejected: It depends on the cut-off. With looser cut-offs the picture gives 55 to 62% on the right and clip frame 4 gives 51 to 59%. The brightness spread (83 to 90% of the brightest areas in the right third, against 31 to 48% in the clip) holds at every cut-off, so that is the claim I kept.
- The cool blue that fills the frame is what makes the small orange strong, and it pushes the pit into the distance. Rejected: True, but the same in all three pictures: 60% of pixels are bluer than red here, 64% in the clip and 67% in the empty-street painting. It cannot explain why this one works better.
- The subject belongs on the right because we read from left to right. Rejected: The mirror image still looks good. The arrangement works either way round; only the text comes out backwards.
- The three flames make a triangle, ring or L shape around the notice. Rejected: The positions are right (lamp above at 0.79/0.17, lantern below at 0.81/0.69, fire to the left at 0.59/0.65), but any three points make a triangle. What actually does the work, the brightness ranking and the paper being lit from below, is kept as its own reasons.
- The edges close the picture in: house walls on both sides, plain sky above, empty pavement below. Rejected: The same walls and sky are in the empty-street painting and in your clip. It is true of almost any street picture.
- Top to bottom the frame is a sandwich of light, dark, light. Rejected: Clip frame 4 also has a dark middle row (0.19 to 0.27). The real difference is that this picture keeps the sky dim, which is covered by the brightness reason.
- The right-hand kerb is the picture's main guiding line, and the fire sits right on it. Rejected: Overstated. The far kerb, carried on in its own direction, does pass through the flames, but the near kerb bends (its slope changes from about -2.7 to -1.8) and runs into the drum's left side. 'The drum stands on the kerb' is true. 'The kerb is the main line' is not. Folded into the reasons about the lines and the camera.
- The lantern's glow lands on exactly the words that matter. Rejected: The warmth is a smooth fade from the bottom of the paper upward, not a patch on particular words. The key lines simply sit in the lower half. Kept in a weaker form under the reason about light from below.
- The street lamp lights up the sky behind the winding wheels so they stand out. Rejected: The squint view does show a lighter patch of sky behind the wheels, but nothing tells lamp glow apart from a gap in the clouds. The cause is unproven and minor.
- There are no people, and the emptiness is the subject. Rejected: Your clip has no people either, so this does not explain the difference. Its useful part (the empty chair stands in for a person) is merged into the chair reason.
- The drum was made into a brazier on purpose (air holes) and placed where a careful person would put it. Rejected: A story detail that says nothing about the layout, and your clip's drum is the same.
- Each prop does two or three jobs at once. Rejected: This is the conclusion, not a separate reason, so it went into the principle.
- The busiest tenth of the frame holds 23% of the weight here against 19% in the clip. Rejected: The difference is too small to carry an explanation. The brightness spread and the overlaps show the same idea far more clearly.

## Why it is hard to vary

Every strong signal in the picture points at the same corner: where the street's lines meet, the largest bright shape, the biggest and nearest props, the warm light, the overlapping objects, and the story's cause (the notice) and effect (the fire and the empty seat). Everything else is kept small, dim or far away so it does not compete. Because all these signals agree, almost any change either removes one of them or creates a second place to look, and that is why every change costs you something, both in looks and in story. The 'balance' you feel is not an even left-right weight (your clip measures almost the same, 46/54 against 43/57). It is that agreement. Your clip uses the same props, but its lines point at the colliery door, its brightest spot is a cloud and its biggest prop is the sacks: three different places. So nothing in it has to be where it is.

## Weak spots

- The notice text is garbled and has the wrong year: 'FRIDAY, 50H, APRIL, 1756' and 'For PATURDAY, 1o MAY, 1756', plus a nonsense company name and signature. The colliery sign is garbled too (the empty-street painting had ABBOTSCLOUGH COLLIERY). For a 1926 history film the paper must be replaced with your correct notice from clip frame 9. The paper is flat and nearly square to the camera, so pasting the correct one over it is practical, as long as the warm fade from the bottom is put back.
- Some edges almost touch (designers call these 'tangents': two edges at different distances that meet, which flattens the depth). The street lamp's glass is about 10 to 15 px from the right winding wheel, near (1110 to 1125, 590). The drum's top-right rim (1048,1810) meets the board's bottom-left corner (1060,1830). The lantern's top ring sits on the board's bottom rail at about (1240,1790). Small, but each makes two things look stuck together.
- The arrangement works only from this spot. The lines meet at the board only from exactly here, and moving the camera sideways by a metre or so would slide that point off the board and change which props overlap. For the film, keep any camera move small, or end it here (for example, a slow straight push toward the notice at the same height).
- The lines meet in a zone, not at a point: different lines cross between 0.62 and 0.71 across and 0.42 and 0.49 down, because AI-drawn perspective is slightly inconsistent and the near kerb bends around the drum. The zone falls on the gate and the board's wooden edge, not on the paper. The notice wins only because it is the brightest thing next to that zone.
- About 70% of the very brightest pixels sit on a spotless white chair, not on the paper. It competes a little with the notice and looks freshly painted for a sooty pit street. Dirty it slightly without darkening it much, because its pale tone helps the layout.
- The model redrew the street and the props: the chimney and winding gear have moved compared with the empty-street painting, and the board has gained a roof. Cutting between this view, the original street and your 3D props or close-up will break continuity.
- The right edge is tight. The chair back and the pail reach about 0.96 to 0.97 across, between 0.6 and 0.9 down, which is where TikTok, Reels and Shorts put their like and share buttons.
- A coal tub stands on a short loose length of rail in a village street (about 0.47 to 0.61 across, 0.55 to 0.58 down). The rails lead nowhere and make no sense there, so remove them.
- The street lamp is the brightest single point in the top half (the white dot in the squint view) and may catch the first glance on a phone. Put titles in the dim top-left sky rather than near the lamp.
- The colliery chimney is pouring smoke while the notice says the pits are closed. That is a mixed message, unless the narration explains it (boilers may have been kept going for pumps; low confidence, worth checking).

## Camera move idea (not yet tested)

A slow, straight push toward the notice with no turning. Move the camera 1.2 m over about 6 seconds, easing in and out, from (3.18, 15.40, 3.52) to the composed position (3.25, 16.60, 3.52), along the line that points at the paper's centre. Keep the height and the rotation (90, 0, 5.9 degrees) fixed, and keyframe the location only. Because the camera never turns, the street lines meet at the same spot (0.66 across, 0.44 down) in every frame. The group is 4.4 to 5.6 m away and only about 1.2 m deep, so the overlaps barely change: the drum's rim stays over the board's corner, the chair back stays on the paper's corner and the lantern stays against the board. The notice grows from about 2.0% to 2.9% of the frame and stays the brightest shape, and all props stay between 0.49 and 0.93 across. Stop exactly on the composed frame, because pushing 0.5 m further crowds the pail and chair to about 0.95 across and the bottom edge. Then cut to the notice close-up. In the first second, watch the bottom-right corner for the door step of right-row cottage 05.
