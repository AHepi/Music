# Chapter 2, round 20: design review, and a direction for the series

This review looks closely at the finished chapter 2 film ("20 Chapter 2 - what really causes inflation.mp4", 4 minutes 30 seconds), both page by page and as a whole. It covers what would lift the film most, how to fill the tall frame with the story, and three possible directions for the rest of the series, with the one I would choose. Times are minutes:seconds into the film. Positions are given as a share of the frame's height measured from the top (0% is the top edge, 100% the bottom edge), or as a share of its width.

## In short

**What works**

- **The paintings.** The 1915 munitions hall, the Glasgow tenements in rain, the Ruhr in snow, the noodle shop and the 1973 petrol station all have strong atmosphere and are right for their period. They share one palette: teal shadows and amber light. Each case gets its own place, season and time of day, which helps people remember which case is which.
- **The numbers.** Every figure arrives on the word that says it. The colours mean the same thing all the way through: white for the statement, amber for the key part, red for harm. The flip counters are the best moments in the film: the marks counter climbing to 4,200,000,000,000 at 2:25 to 2:29, and the price board flipping £1.00, £1.60, £2.00 at 1:05 to 1:08.
- **The rhymes.** The politician opens and closes the film, and the shell factory comes back on page 8. The interest dial of Britain in 1920 (3:34) returns in today's New Zealand (3:58), so an object carries the chapter's argument: the same cure, then and now. The hundred dots of 1915 (0:24) also return for New Zealand (4:11).
- **The camera.** It holds still, with 20 stretches of 2 to 5 seconds where nothing moves at all. Its moves mean something: it pushes in on the price board at "prices barely move" (0:55) and "prices climb" (1:05).
- **Light used as a fact, once.** At 0:52 to 0:53 the idle factory's windows light up as the workers go in. It is the best idea in the film, and it is used only once.

**The three biggest problems**

1. **The film looks like a collage of five kinds of image.** Only the film's colour finish (teal shadows, warm highlights) holds them together. The five are:
   - painted rooms
   - cut-outs with white paper edges
   - smooth card models
   - detailed Blender models
   - see-through chart boxes.

   The card models are the weak link:
   - the banknote bundles read as pale green bricks or soap (0:30 to 0:42, 1:40 to 2:07, 2:23 to 2:35)
   - the shell is a glossy gold bullet (0:18)
   - the coins look like washers (0:50 to 1:07)
   - the house frame (1:32 to 1:34) has the "sparse in detail" problem you named for the card house in round 19.

   Every time one of them appears, the image drops in quality.
2. **The story sits in a middle band and the bottom of the frame is floor.** In most shots the words sit 10 to 25% down and the subject sits 35 to 80% down. The bottom 20 to 35% is floor, street, conveyor belt or snow, with nothing in it that belongs to the line being spoken. I measured one frame per second across the film:
   - the bottom tenth of the frame is about half as bright as the middle
   - in 72% of frames the bottom fifth is more than 30% darker than the middle.

   The cause is built into the painting prompts, which ask for "standing eye level" and "the lower quarter of the picture is the empty floor/street". That rule was made for chapter 1, where cut-outs stood in front of a flat page.
3. **People are props.** The chapter's point is who pays, but people appear in three weak ways:
   - as one strip of figures copied many times (1:17 to 1:20, and about 25 copies at 3:42 to 3:57, the chapter's moral peak)
   - as heads cut off by the bottom edge (0:23 to 0:29)
   - or not at all: the New Zealand street is empty while Malina talks about unemployment (3:11 to 3:18 and 4:09 to 4:15).

**The direction I would choose: "the house"**

Stage each chapter in one tall building of society, cut open from roof to cellar like a doll's house and seen from the front.
- The top floor is where austerity is decided: the Treasury, the central bank's vault, the economists' desks.
- The middle floors are what public money pays for: a classroom, a clinic, council homes being built.
- The bottom floors are work and home: the factory floor, a family's kitchen, the mine.

Austerity is decided at the top and lands at the bottom. So the tall frame is full by its very shape, and every shot shows who decides and who pays. Lights going out, rooms changing hands and weight pressing down through the floors carry the facts.

It suits your camera grammar exactly: pan up to the experts' promises, pan down to the pressure. It keeps real 3D depth without the tearing of painted rooms, and it costs about as much to film as today's painted pages. Section 5 sets out two other directions, "the money machine" and "the pop-up book", for comparison.

**A first step that costs nothing:** make one test shot of the British house for chapter 3's 1922 cuts.
- Build it from plain coloured room boxes and chapter 1's existing cut-outs, lit at night.
- Give it one move down, from the vault to the mine.
- If it reads well as a still and in motion, paint the room walls. That would be about ten small paintings, about 25 US cents, and only with your yes.

## 1. The film as a whole

### 1.1 One world, or a collage?

Here is a concrete example. At 2:23 ("and keeps paying them, with new money"), banknotes are thrown onto the Ruhr's snow:
- some are painted cut-outs with white paper edges
- others are plain olive blocks with a yellow band
- one falling note shows as a black slab (2:23.1 to 2:23.7).

Behind them is a painting with brushwork, and on top sits a hook in clean type. Four of the film's five kinds of image meet in one second, and the eye notices the joins before the meaning.

Across the film there are five kinds of image:

1. **Painted rooms**: brushwork and soft painted light, on every page.
2. **Cut-outs**: painted like the rooms, but with a white paper edge. These are the politician, the basket, the women munition workers, the queues, the rent strikers, the soldiers, the ramen bowl, the bank, the book and the iron weight.
3. **Card models**: smooth, untextured and lit by the stage lamp. These are the shell, banknote bundles, paper boats, coins, price tags, notice board, house frame, oil barrel, dial and villa.
4. **Blender models**: detailed, realistic surfaces with a miniature look. These are the works and town on pages 3 and 8, and the desk on page 8.
5. **Flat graphics**: hooks, labels, counters, and charts in see-through dark panels.

Painted rooms sit well with painted cut-outs and plain words. These moments look like one world:
- the rent strikers in the rain (1:25 to 1:31)
- the soldiers marching through the snow (2:14 to 2:18)
- the ramen bowl on the counter (1:35 to 1:45).

The Blender model town on pages 3 and 8 is also one world, a lit miniature. It is just a different world from the painted pages.

What doesn't sit well is every card model inside a painting:

| Time | Card model | What it reads as on a phone |
|---|---|---|
| 0:18 to 0:19 | the shell on the checkout belt | a glossy gold rifle bullet (the exact confusion chapter 1 fixed by repainting its shells as 18-pounders) |
| 0:30 to 0:42, 1:40 to 2:07, 2:23 to 2:35 | banknote bundles | pale green or olive bricks, a bar of soap; no printing visible |
| 0:31 to 0:33 | paper boats | flat grey triangles on a dark blue pool painted on the factory floor |
| 0:50 to 1:07, 2:05 to 2:07, 3:30 to 3:40 | coins | flat gold discs with a dark centre, like washers |
| 1:21 to 1:31 | the rent notice | a plain brown box on two posts standing in the road |
| 1:32 to 1:34 | the house frame | plain timber and bright red bricks with white mortar on a grey slab |
| 2:41 to 2:50 | the oil barrel | clean, glossy, no wear, beside a painted station |
| 3:15 to 3:18 | the villa in the New Zealand street | a toy house on a lawn base, at a different scale and finish from the painted houses behind it |

The white paper edges are a second, quieter split. On dark scenes they glow like stickers, for example the queue at 3:42 to 3:57 and the workers at 0:48 to 0:51. They say "paper on a page", while the painted rooms and the Blender models say "film set". Chapter 1 earned the white edges because everything there was paper on a page.

From page to page, the colour finish and the type never change, and places come back:
- parliament on pages 1 and 10
- the factory on pages 3 and 8
- the New Zealand street on pages 7, 8 and 10.

That holds the chapter together. What changes from page to page is the material, and that is what the eye trips on.

What this means: you pointed out three drops in quality after round 19 (the sparse card house, the plain factories, the plain report). All three had the same source: plain card models among detailed paintings. Round 20 replaced those three with Blender models, but the same kind of object is still used elsewhere (the table above).

### 1.2 Composition in the tall frame

Here is a concrete example. The Ruhr page opens (2:08 to 2:14) with "GERMANY 1923" and "PRICES RACING" in the top quarter and the counter at 33%. Meanwhile the bottom 40% of the frame is snow and rail tracks with nothing on them, for six seconds, as Malina says "Prices are already racing."

That is the pattern of the whole film:

- **Words in the top quarter, the subject in a middle band, floor at the bottom.** I measured 36 frames on grid overlays. Hooks and titles sit 10 to 25% down, the subject sits between 35 and 80%, and the bottom 20 to 35% is floor, street, belt or snow. The longest cases are:
  - 0:20 to 0:23: factory floor, 60 to 100%
  - 0:46 to 1:07: road and kerb, 65 to 100%, for 21 seconds
  - 1:14 to 1:17: cobbles, 50 to 100%
  - 2:08 to 2:14: snow, 60 to 100%
  - 3:11 to 3:14: footpath and road, 45 to 100%
  - 4:09 to 4:15: road, 75 to 100%, with the dial shrunk to a fifth of the width.
- **The bottom is dark as well as empty.** Here is the average brightness by tenth of the frame (out of 255), measured on one frame per second:
  - between 74 and 82 in every band from 10 to 70%
  - 69 at 70 to 80%
  - 53 at 80 to 90%
  - 37 at the bottom tenth.

  The film's darkened corners add to this.
- **The cause is in the recipe.** The painting prompts ask for "standing eye level" and "the lower quarter of the picture is the empty floor/street". At eye level in a tall frame the horizon lands near the middle, so half the frame is ground. The phone rule keeps words out of the bottom 380 pixels (the bottom 20%), and nothing else was given that band to do.
- **Round 20's fill rule was used as decoration, not story.** On the model-town pages the bottom corners are filled with blurred barrels, crates and a cart (0:43 to 0:45, 1:08 to 1:13). They fill the corners, but on a phone they read as a brown blur and say nothing. On the painted pages the subject is often far below the rule's "60 to 80% of the width":
  - one banknote bundle at 13% of the width (0:30)
  - the paper boats at 10 to 15% (0:31 to 0:33)
  - the basket at 6% (0:36 to 0:41).
- **The same shape again and again.** Eight of the thirteen places are one-point views: parliament, the checkout, the munitions hall, the tenements, the Ruhr gate, the 1920 street, the factory gate and the New Zealand street. Most of them also centre the subject (the politician, the notice board, the house frame, the barrel, the dial). Each frame is strong alone, but in a row the film feels same-shaped. The only changes of angle are the raised view of the model town and the top-down desk (3:19 to 3:27), which is a welcome break.

Balance follows from this. A tall frame feels stable when it is heaviest at the bottom (big, close shapes), lighter in the middle (the subject) and lightest at the top (sky and words). Here the bottom is dark but holds no shapes. It reads as a hole rather than a base, and the frames float.

### 1.3 Depth, scale, light, colour and texture

- **Depth comes from focus blur and layers, not from the painted rooms.** Nothing at all moves in 60% of the film's 8,110 frames, and the camera holds still for most of the rest. A still camera cannot show the depth of a painted room. Instead, depth reads through:
  - the soft foreground and background
  - cut-outs overlapping the painting
  - light beams and haze.

  On the sideways move at the petrol station (2:39.5 to 2:40.9), the canopy edge bends and the queued cars smear: the known tearing.
- **Scale is used well when it means something.** The book at 3:50 is big because it is the authority (your round-20 note), and the shell on the belt is big as a symbol. Scale is used badly when the subject is the point but tiny: the basket at 0:36 to 0:41, and the queue at 3:43.
- **The colours are consistent and readable.** Teal and amber run everywhere, and red is kept for harm: "PRICES UP", "7%", "TOO STRONG, FOR TOO LONG" and the 11% bar. The one clash is the red title over the green desk (3:24 to 3:27).
- **The light sometimes argues against the line.** I measured the average brightness and warmth (red minus blue) of each place:
  - The New Zealand street on page 10 is the second warmest place in the film (warmth +43, brightness 87), behind only the cosy noodle shop (+51). Yet this is where Malina says rates went up and unemployment climbed.
  - The 1920 street, where the cure begins, is the coldest place in the film (-5).
  - The factory gate where the queue forms is among the coldest (+1).

  The chapter argues that today rhymes with 1920, but its light says today is golden.
- **Texture splits the same way as the materials.** The paintings and cut-outs have brushwork, the card models have none, and the Blender models have realistic brick and wood. The copy you were sent is compressed, which smooths the film grain away, so the smooth card models stand out even more.

### 1.4 Words on screen

- **What is good:**
  - the hooks, in heavy white and amber type, are clear
  - the place titles, with their thin amber rule, have a documentary feel
  - the big figures read instantly ("3.2%" is about 170 pixels tall)
  - the counters and the hundred dots are excellent
  - the way Mattei's argument is marked is exactly right: "MATTEI:" in amber above the hook, with "Clara E. Mattei, The Capital Order (2022)" beneath (3:50 to 3:57). It should be the pattern for chapter 3.
- **Too many words.** I counted about 110 separate word items (hooks, titles, labels, chart words) in 4 minutes 30 seconds, or one every 2.4 seconds. Chapter 1's approved test page had four hooks in 23 seconds: 8 MILLION, 60,000, PUBLIC OWNERSHIP and IGNORED. Many chapter 2 hooks repeat the narration almost word for word, such as:
  - "NOT ONE PRICE TAG"
  - "WHAT REALLY DRIVES IT?"
  - "BACK TO THE SHELL FACTORY"
  - "PRICES ALREADY CLIMBING"
  - "WITHIN A YEAR"
  - "SINCE THE END OF 2021".

  Your own style rules warn that this turns hooks into subtitles. Phone apps then add their own captions on top.
- **Small words are too small for a phone.** "EXAMPLE PRICES" is about 19 pixels tall, "FACTORY 1 · SPARE CAPACITY" about 29, and the place titles about 33. That is roughly 1.1 to 2 mm on a typical phone, and softer still after compression. Anything meant to be read needs about 36 to 40 pixels.
- **Panels look like television news.** The see-through dark boxes (1:46 to 1:52, 2:52 to 2:59, 3:44 to 3:48, 4:11 to 4:15) are the most "digital" thing in the film. Your style rules already say that printed things belong on objects: cards, signs and boards.
- **Hooks over bright, busy paint rely on their soft shadows.** Two examples are "BRITAIN AT WAR" in white on a pale sky (1:08 to 1:10), and "PRICES ALREADY CLIMBING" over the lit canopy, with a lamp glowing behind "CLIMBING" (2:38).

### 1.5 Motion, pacing, cuts and page turns

- **The rhythm of arrivals works.** Something new lands every second or two, on the word it belongs to. Hooks pop with a small bounce and cut-outs fold up, which keeps attention.
- **But the image freezes between arrivals.** On pages 2, 3, 5, 7 and 10, between 73 and 82% of frames are identical to the frame before. The camera holds still, as you asked, but so does the world. Pages 4 and 6 hardly ever freeze (1% and 8% of frames), because rain and snow keep falling. The lesson is that a still camera can look at a living world.
- **There are nine dips to black inside pages** (0:05, 1:32, 1:53, 2:24, 2:51, 3:10, 3:18, 3:41, 4:19), on top of the nine page turns. A book doesn't go black between pages. A hard cut on a word, already used at 0:45, 0:56 and 3:19, keeps both the pace and the book idea.
- **The page turns** (0:20, 0:42, 1:14, 1:34, 2:08, 2:35, 2:59, 3:27, 3:57) are quick and clean: a corner peel of 0.85 seconds showing a plain warm grey back. They are always the same and carry nothing. The back of the page is empty space you could use.

### 1.6 What to keep: the series' vocabulary

- the flip counters
- the hundred (dots that are workers)
- the stamps (RENTS FROZEN, TOO STRONG, FOR TOO LONG)
- the interest dial that travels across a century
- the red book as Mattei's authority, with the "MATTEI:" credit
- the politician as the voice of "common sense" at both ends
- windows lighting up as a fact
- rain and snow
- the place-and-year titles
- the page turn, as the thread back to chapter 1's book.

## 3. What would lift it most, ranked

Cost words:
- a **cheap tweak** is a change to a page file with no new paintings
- a **moderate rebuild** means new pieces or models and a page rebuilt
- a **new approach** means a new way of building sets for the series.

| Rank | Change | Where | Why it lifts the film | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **One world.** Give every card model the painting's surface (a painted texture and the painting's light, no gloss), or swap it for a painted cut-out; decide on the white edges (keep them if the world is paper, darken or drop them if it is film) | pages 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9 | removes the kind of quality drop you flagged three times after round 19 | moderate rebuild: about 10 object paintings at 1k, about 25 US cents (needs your yes), or reuse painted pieces that already exist (banknotes, the row of shells, padlock, money bag) |
| 2 | **Fill the bottom with story**: people, near objects and consequences instead of floor | all pages, most of all the stretches listed in 1.2 | the thing you asked for twice; balances every frame | moderate rebuild, mostly with existing pieces |
| 3 | **People as the subject**: different figures, big and close, never copies | pages 2, 3, 4, 9 and 10 | carries the chapter's point, who pays | moderate: one or two crowd paintings per era, about 10 US cents each |
| 4 | **Let light and state tell the change**: windows on and off, smoke on and off, the street darkening | the page 3 wide shots, pages 6, 10 and 4 | the image changes when the fact changes, not only a label | cheap tweak: masks over the paintings and the existing colour settings |
| 5 | **Life in still shots**: one slow movement per shot (smoke, steam, rain, dust in a beam, a flickering lamp) | pages 2, 3, 5, 7 and 10 | ends the frozen-slideshow feel without moving the camera | cheap tweak: the effects already exist |
| 6 | **Fewer words, numbers on things, bigger labels** | all pages | fewer words competing with the image; readable on a phone | cheap to moderate |
| 7 | **Clarity fixes**: the rate's path on the page 10 dial; ships not on a floor pool; a painted 18-pounder shell; remove the black slab; make the two factories differ in the wide shot | pages 10, 2, 1, 6 and 3 | removes confusions | cheap tweaks |
| 8 | **Vary the angle**: frontal, top-down, low (power) and high (pressure), not one-point views in a row | across the chapter | breaks the same-shaped rhythm | moderate: new paintings at about 10 US cents each |
| 9 | **Cuts and page turns**: hard cuts on words instead of dips; print something on the back of the turning page (the next place and year showing through, or a ledger) | 9 dips, 9 page turns | keeps the book idea and the pace | cheap tweaks |

If chapter 2 is ever filmed again, ranks 4, 5, 7 and 9 could be done in a day of page edits at no cost. Ranks 1 to 3 are the real lessons for chapter 3. The new approach, a kind of set built for the tall frame from the start, is in section 5.

## 4. Filling the tall frame with the story

### 4.1 Give every band a job

| Band (from the top) | What the phone does there | Its job | A chapter 2 moment that already does it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to 7% | the app's top bar | calm: sky or ceiling, nothing to read | the parliament window at 0:02 |

### 4.2 Working rules

1. **Fill with consequences, not decoration.** The filler must be the cause or the effect of the line being spoken. At "workers wait at the gate", the bottom band is the waiting workers, not barrels.
2. **People in front.** The nearest band is usually people. It is where the viewer stands, and it sits under the captions, where words can't go but faces can.
3. **Make the subject big.** The thing she names fills 50 to 80% of the width when she names it. Keep a small subject in a big room only when smallness is the point ("fewer things").
4. **Let light and state change.** Windows lit or dark, smoke or none, lamps warm or cold: the image should change when the fact changes. The factory windows at 0:53 are the model.
5. **Put numbers on things.** Print figures on objects that belong to the place: a price board, the petrol station's sign, a ledger, a blackboard, a newspaper bill, a calendar. Keep panels for true comparisons only.
6. **No copies.** Every visible person is a different figure. Paint crowd sheets with many different people, and reuse figures only in far, soft layers.
7. **Change the angle.** Alternate frontal views (façades, cut-open buildings), top-down views (desks, maps, ledgers), low views (looking up at power) and high views (looking down on pressure). Use no more than two one-point views in a row.
8. **A still camera, a living world.** Every held shot has one slow movement that belongs to the place: smoke, steam, rain, snow, a flickering lamp, dust in a light beam.
9. **Keep the headline area calm.** Put hooks over sky, a wall or a soft dark band, never over the brightest, busiest paint. Keep words out of the phone's margins, but put story in them.
10. **Fewer, bigger words.** Use one hook per beat, with numbers and names first, and nothing that repeats the sentence she is saying.

## 5. Three directions for the series

### What any direction has to do

- Carry Mattei's argument, not just decorate it: who decides, who pays, and the fact that austerity was a choice that experts presented as necessity (Mattei argues).
- Fill the tall frame by its own logic, so the filling is never decoration.
- Look like one world.
- Keep your rules:
  - the camera stays still unless a move lands a point, with at most three moves a shot
  - few words
  - real people only as typed name cards
  - no political party colours
  - no hands.
- Grow across the chapters still to come: Britain and Italy in the 1920s, Thatcher, why nations need social spending to develop, New Zealand and Australia, what austerity did to people, why you lose bargaining power, and why it is oppressive.
- Be buildable on this machine (no graphics card, software drawing) with about US$3 left for paintings.

### Direction A: the house of the capital order (my choice)

**The idea.** Each chapter is staged in one tall building of society, cut open from roof to cellar like a doll's house. It is seen from the front, a little to one side, so every room shows its depth, with the street and its people in front and the city's lights behind. It is plainly a symbol; no real building is shown or implied.

Mattei argues that austerity was decided by a few and paid for by working people, to protect what she calls "the capital order" (a world where a few own the land, factories and mines). The house turns that argument into architecture:
- The top floor is where decisions are made: the Treasury, the central bank's vault, and the economists' desks with typed name cards.
- The middle floors are what public money pays for: a classroom, a clinic, council homes being built, a public company's office.
- The ground floor and cellar are work and home: the factory floor, a family's kitchen with its pay packet, the mine.

Austerity acts on the building physically:
- weight (gold bars, chapter 1's iron weight) presses down through a central column
- rooms go dark when they are cut
- when a public company is sold off, its sign is swapped and the whole room slides up into the owners' floor
- a pipe carries money upward.

The lit windows are the data: a hundred windows on the façade are a hundred households, which turns the hundred dots into homes. Each chapter can open on the façade at night, its windows lit, before the front folds down like a page to show the rooms.

**Three example frames (chapter 3)**

*A1. Britain, 1922: the cuts (the whole building, a still shot).*
- 0 to 10%: night sky with chimney smoke; the place title "BRITAIN 1922" at about 9%, on the left.
- 10 to 32%, the top floor: on the left, a committee room with a long table and typed name cards (the names come from the chapter 3 research). On the right, the Bank's vault, dim, because Britain is not yet back on gold.
- 32 to 66%, two middle storeys of three rooms each: a classroom with children at desks, a clinic, and a council-house building site with scaffolding and bricklayers, all warm-lit.
- 66 to 84%, the ground floor: a factory floor with a lathe and workers; a kitchen, with a family at the table and the pay packet.
- 84 to 100%, the cellar: the coal seam, with miners' lamps glinting. Faces and lamps sit under the phone's captions, where words can't go.
- On "cuts", an axe crashes down through the roof into the middle storeys, with a short shake:
  - the building site goes dark and its scaffold folds flat
  - the classroom dims to half
  - a typed card slides onto the site's door (wording from the research)
  - the hook "CUT" appears in the sky.
- Why: one still frame shows who decided (top), what was lost (middle) and who is next (bottom). No band is empty, because every band is a level of society. The axe is the period's own image: the 1922 cuts were nicknamed "the Geddes Axe".

*A2. 1925 to 1926: back to gold, then the strike (same building; a pan down).*
- On "back to gold", gold bars land in the vault (top right), with a glint and a small shake. The iron column running down the middle of the building takes the weight:
  - dust falls in the rooms below
  - the cellar's ceiling lowers, so the mine shrinks from about 16% to 12% of the frame's height
  - the miners' pay packets are swapped for smaller ones (chapter 1's way of showing a cut)
  - the hook "BACK ON GOLD, 1925" appears.
- Then 1926: the factory floor and the lower rooms empty as their people walk out into the street in front of the house. They fill the bottom 20% with caps, faces and blank placards. The lower windows go dark because work has stopped. After nine days the crowd folds away, except the miners. They stay outside the dark mine while a calendar on the mine wall flips through the months.
- Why: the cause (gold at the top) and the cost (wages at the bottom) are joined by something you can see, and the strike is the bottom of the building stopping. The move from vault to mine is your "pan down to signal pressure".

*A3. Italy: the same medicine (two half-buildings, one above the other).*
- 0 to 47%: the British house, smaller. 53 to 100%: an Italian building cut open, with arcades at street level, a ministry upstairs, a telephone exchange and a factory. Between them, at about 50%, the hook "SAME ADVICE".
- The same three things happen in both, one after another, on her words:
  - wages: pay packets are swapped for smaller ones on both ground floors
  - selling off: in the Italian building, a public company's sign is swapped for SOLD and its room slides up a floor into the owners' rooms
  - taxes: on the Italian top floor, a typed ledger line for a tax on the rich is struck through, while a stamp lands on the ground floor for taxes on what everyone buys. The exact taxes come from the research.
- The Italian top floor carries typed name cards: "ALBERTO DE STEFANI, FINANCE MINISTER". On a desk piled with books sit the names of the economists Mattei names, under the credit "MATTEI:" with her book. Maffeo Pantaleoni and Luigi Einaudi are examples, to be confirmed in the chapter 3 research.
- Why: Mattei's comparison, that a democracy and a dictatorship applied the same austerity, becomes a single frame. A stacked comparison is the natural way to compare on a tall screen.

**Light and colour for chapter 3.** Use the share of warm light in the building as the chapter's graph:
- 1919 to 1921: nearly every room glows.
- 1922: the middle storeys dim.
- 1925: a cold gold glint appears at the top while the cellar darkens.
- 1926: the street flares with lamp-lit people, then goes dark.
- Italy starts with warm ochre stucco and arcades at street level and a cool ministry above. As wages fall and taxes move, the ground floor's light thins while the top floor's warms.

Keep strikes in amber lamplight, not red, and avoid black as a colour code in Italy, so no party colours creep in. Keep red for harm in the words, as now. Keep any symbol of the regime out of the paintings, as your painting rules already require.

**How it grows through the series.**
- Thatcher: the same British house sixty years on, with the same mine in the cellar, so 1926 and 1984 rhyme. Council homes change hands, public companies' rooms slide up, and a lock appears on the union room.
- Why nations need social spending to develop: the middle floors are load-bearing. Building schools, roads and power adds floors and steadies the house; cutting them makes it sag.
- New Zealand and Australia: two houses stacked in one frame, each with its own architecture (villas and state houses). The 1980s and 1990s changes show as rooms sold and lights dimmed.
- What austerity did to people: the camera moves down into the rooms (the kitchens, the clinics, the dole office), which is your "move down to expose the minor details".
- Why you lose bargaining power: the workers' floor, where the door upstairs locks and the queue outside the factory is the weight on their wages.
- Why it is oppressive: the whole house, the top floor's controls, the ceilings lowering.

**What it demands from this machine.**
- Build:
  - Each building is 9 to 12 shallow rooms, built as real 3D boxes, so the camera sees true depth.
  - Each back wall is a flat painting. A flat painting on a flat wall never tears when the camera moves; only paintings pretending to be deep spaces tear.
  - Props are cut-outs, card models dressed in painted surfaces, or Blender models. Furniture and machines from the 3D tools being looked into in parallel would fit straight in.
  - One shadow-casting lamp lights the whole building, with a cheap warm glow in each room instead of more shadows.
- Filming:
  - It should take about 3 to 5 seconds a frame, close to today's painted pages. With two frames drawn at a time, as now, a 4.5-minute chapter takes about 4 to 6 hours.
  - Heavy Blender props in many rooms would push it towards the 12 to 16 seconds a frame of the model-town pages, so keep them for a few hero rooms.
  - The camera holds still for most of each shot, so the unchanging background of a still shot could be drawn once, with only what moves redrawn. That would cut filming time a lot, but needs a change to the engine.
- Paintings:
  - Each building needs about 10 room walls at 1k (about 25 US cents), a façade or sky at 2k (about 10 cents), and 15 or so new people and objects at 1k (about 40 cents). That is about 75 cents a building.
  - Chapter 3's two buildings would cost about US$1.50, out of the roughly US$3 left, and only with your yes.
  - Many of chapter 1's cut-outs already fit 1920s Britain and Italy: miners, crowds, pithead, council houses, pay packet, iron weight, gold bars, scissors, the bank, the map of Italy.
- Risks: the whole building in every shot would feel like a diagram. Use it for openings, turning points and endings (about a third of the shots), and go into single rooms for the rest. Each room is a small stage, filmed like chapter 2's best shots. Rooms must share one scale and one light.

### Direction B: the money machine

**The idea.** A tall machine of glass tanks, pipes and brass valves, in which money is a warm, glowing amber liquid. It is inspired by the water machine of the economy that the New Zealand economist Bill Phillips built in 1949. One is said to be on show at the Reserve Bank of New Zealand's museum in Wellington, but that needs confirming.

The tanks are what a country can make: its workers, materials and machines.
- Pour money into a tank with room and it fills: more jobs, more things.
- Pour money into a full tank and it overflows into the prices gauge: inflation.

That is chapter 2's whole argument in one image. Austerity's levers are valves: close the spending tap, open the interest drain, lock the gold reservoir, drain the wages tank into the owners' tank. Every valve handle carries a typed name card of who turned it, so the machine never looks natural or neutral. That matters because Mattei argues the experts dressed their choices up as laws of nature.

**Three example frames**

*B1. Chapter 2's two factories, told again.*
- 5 to 25%: a tap marked "NEW MONEY".
- 25 to 70%: two glass tanks side by side. The left one is a third full, with idle workers standing in its empty space. The right one is full to the brim, with workers busy.
- 70 to 92%: a tall prices gauge.
- The same amber stream falls into both. The left tank's level rises and its workers start (its lamps come on). The right one overflows into the gauge, which climbs in red.
- Why: it shows in one frame what page 3 needed two places and 31 seconds to say.

*B2. 1925: the gold reservoir.*
- At the top (5 to 30%), a sealed glass reservoir of gold behind chapter 1's padlock. Its outlet valve is fixed to the size of the gold, and typed name cards hang on the valve: the Chancellor and the Bank, with names from the research.
- Below (30 to 75%), the outflow is throttled: the wages tank (bottom left) sinks while the owners' tank (bottom right) holds.
- In the bottom band, miners stand inside the wages tank as its level falls past their knees. The hook is "BACK ON GOLD, 1925".
- Why: the gold standard appears as a choice with names on it, and its cost is a level you can watch falling around the miners.

*B3. Italy: the tax pipes.*
- The pipe that drained the top tank (the rich) is capped, with a typed tag for the tax that was cut.
- A new pipe drains the bottom tanks (taxes on what everyone buys).
- A public company's tank unbolts itself and slides across to the owners' side with a SOLD tag.
- De Stefani's typed name card hangs by the valves, with the economists' cards beside it under the "MATTEI:" credit.
- Why: "taxes moved off the rich" becomes a re-plumbing you can follow with your eye, and the names keep it a decision rather than a law of nature.

**How it grows.**
- Development: public investment builds bigger tanks, so more money can flow without overflowing. Chapter 2's warning turns into a promise.
- Thatcher: pipes are rerouted from public tanks into private ones.
- New Zealand and Australia: the machine is a New Zealander's idea, a natural homecoming.
- People and bargaining power: the wages tank's level is bargaining power, and the unemployment tank fills as the interest drain opens.
- Oppression: valves are locked, with names on them.

**What it demands.**
- Build: one set, built once in Blender and reused in every chapter. Cylinders, pipes, valves and gauges are simple to make detailed by script.
- The liquid is faked cheaply, with glowing surfaces whose height changes and a few rising bubbles. There is no real fluid simulation.
- Filming: glass needs see-through drawing, which software drawing handles slowly. A chapter would take about 5 to 9 hours to film, more with the works' realistic metal surfaces.
- Paintings: almost none, just some people as cut-outs.
- Risks:
  - It is abstract, a machine rather than a story. That is close to the "lecture" feeling you didn't want in chapter 1's first script.
  - It can make austerity look like plumbing, natural and neutral, which is the view Mattei argues against. The names on the valves and the people in the tanks must always be visible.
  - It cannot carry the human scenes on its own, so it would alternate with painted places.

### Direction C: the pop-up book

**The idea.** Go back to the paper world chapter 1 began, done the way you described for the one-page picture ("an open book... the pop ups have a lot of intricate details so the shading sells the drama"), but as a tall book:
- the upper page stands up as the backdrop
- the lower page lies flat towards us as the stage, printed with small columns of type and footnotes (the sources)
- every scene rises from the fold in several layers of cut paper, with real thickness and shadows.

Everything is paper, even the charts: strips that slide out of slots, wheels turning in windows, flaps that lift. Austerity's acts become paper acts:
- cutting, with chapter 1's scissors or an axe
- folding flat, as homes and schools fold down
- locking, as a gold clasp shuts the book.

Resistance pops up. One reading lamp gives deep shadows.

**Three example frames (chapter 3)**

*C1. 1922: cuts are cuts.*
- 0 to 50%: the upper page, a painted dusk sky with chimneys.
- 45 to 62%: at the fold, a row of new council homes (chapter 1's council-houses cut-out) with warm windows, 90% of the width.
- 62 to 100%: the lower page, with printed type and a paper tab marked SPENDING in a slot.
- On "cut", the steel scissors crash in from the top and snip the homes at their hinge. The homes fall flat towards us one after another, showing their plain backs. The title "BRITAIN 1922" sits at 9% and the hook "CUT" at 18%.
- Why: the cuts are literal, and the flattened homes stay lying on the page as a record of what was lost.

*C2. 1925: the gold clasp.*
- The book's brass clasp swings over the spread and locks.
- Gold bars pile on the top edge of the upper page, which leans forward under their weight. The space at the fold, where the pithead and the miners stand (40 to 75%), narrows.
- On the lower page, a paper wheel in a window turns the miners' wage down (the figure comes from the research).
- Why: gold is both a lock and a weight, chapter 1's "a limit they chose", and the shrinking gap at the fold is the miners' room to live.

*C3. 1926: the strike rises and falls.*
- Every crowd cut-out rises across the spread at once, using chapter 1's Glasgow crowds: the back row at the fold, the front row over the lower page, with heads crossing the bottom edge.
- A paper wheel counts the days.
- Then the crowd folds flat row by row, leaving only the miners standing while the printed calendar on the lower page turns its months.
- Why: solidarity rises and collapses, told with paper alone, and the calendar shows how long the miners held out.

**How it grows.** Every chapter is a chapter of the same book.
- Thatcher: pages torn out and sold.
- Why social spending develops nations: pop-ups that raise each other (a school pops up a workforce, which pops up a factory).
- People and bargaining power: chapter 1's paper chain of people, and the scissors.
- Oppression: the book shut under its clasp.

**What it demands.**
- Build: the cheapest of the three, with flat paper layers and real shadows.
- Filming: chapter 1's paper stage drew about 0.85 seconds a frame. With focus blur and glow, perhaps 1 to 2 seconds, so a chapter takes about 2 to 4 hours.
- There are no painted rooms, so no tearing, and sideways moves are free.
- Paintings: reuse many of chapter 1's 47, plus perhaps 4 backdrops at 2k and 20 pieces at 1k, about 90 US cents.
- Risks: it could feel like a step back from chapter 2's 3D, which you liked ("I love the fact theirs depth in these"). It only looks rich with intricate, many-layered pop-ups and strong light, which takes design time.

### Which I would choose, and why

**Direction A, the house.**

- It fills the tall frame by its shape, and the filling is always the argument. Every band is a level of society, so nothing is ever "just floor".
- It carries Mattei's argument in every shot: who decides, who pays, where the cuts land. It grows naturally through all the chapters still to come, with the same grammar each time, so viewers learn to read it.
- It uses your camera grammar literally: pan up to the experts and their promises, pan down to the pressure, move up for the whole building, move down into one room.
- It keeps real 3D depth, which you asked for, and stops the tearing, because paintings become flat walls inside real rooms instead of pretend-deep spaces.
- It is affordable here: filming time is close to today's painted pages, and chapter 3 would need about US$1.50 of paintings. It can use whatever the 3D tool search finds for furniture and machines, without depending on it.

What I would borrow from the others:
- from C, the paper actions for the big moments (the axe cut, rooms folding flat, a crowd popping up), which keep the thread to chapter 1
- from B, one pipe or gauge in the house, for the moments that explain how money moves.

I would not mix all three equally. One building grammar, used consistently, is what will make the series recognisable.
