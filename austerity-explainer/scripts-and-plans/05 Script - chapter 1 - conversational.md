# 05 Script - chapter 1 - conversational

**What this is:** the test chapter's narration, rewritten to sound like someone talking to you,
with more urgency: short sentences, present tense, questions to the viewer, the stakes up front.
It replaces "03 Script", which read like a lecture. About 500 words, so about 3 minutes 35
seconds in Malina's voice (she read the sample at about 140 words a minute).

How it was rewritten, following your Pinker toolkit: open with something the viewer has heard
before; one plain name for each thing (workers, owners, the experts, the capital order); a
concrete case before each general idea; Mattei's interpretations still said as hers. The facts
are unchanged and still checked in "02 Research - chapter 1"; the codes in brackets point there.
The film never says "MMT".

---

## Page 1 - The speech

You've heard the speech. Times are tough. Tighten your belts. Cut spending, push up interest
rates, hold wages down. Sounds like common sense. But austerity has a birthday. Economist
Clara E. Mattei dug through the archives and found it, right after the First World War. And she
argues it was never really about budgets. It was about stopping something. [C1-01, C1-02]

**On the page:** AUSTERITY pasted up in cut-out letters. Typed slips: CUT SPENDING / PUSH UP
INTEREST RATES / HOLD WAGES DOWN. Red pen: "SOUNDS LIKE COMMON SENSE". Typed card: CLARA E. MATTEI,
THE CAPITAL ORDER, 2022.

## Page 2 - The war

Picture Britain in 1915. The war is burning through shells faster than factories can make them.
And the government doesn't run out of money. It runs out of shells. So it takes charge: what
factories make, what things cost, what workers are paid. Italy does the same. And people notice.
If the economy can be organised for war, why not for what people need, instead of just for
profit? [C1-03, C1-04, C1-07, C1-29]

**On the page:** engraved artillery shells, "1915". Typed list ticked off: WHAT FACTORIES MAKE /
WHAT THINGS COST / WHAT WORKERS ARE PAID. Red pen: "NOT SHORT OF MONEY. SHORT OF SHELLS."

## Page 3 - Britain after the war

When the war ends, workers don't want to go back. In Britain, union membership doubles, to
eight million. In Glasgow, sixty thousand people pack a square, demanding a shorter working week.
The government sends in troops. And tanks. Coal miners demand public ownership of the mines.
A royal commission backs them. The government ignores it. [C1-08, C1-10, C1-11]

**On the page:** bar chart drawn to scale, union members in millions: 1914 4.1 / 1918 6.5 /
1920 8.3, red pen "DOUBLED". Typed cards: GLASGOW, 31 JAN 1919, 60,000 PEOPLE, TROOPS AND TANKS /
COAL, 1919, ROYAL COMMISSION: PUBLIC OWNERSHIP, GOVERNMENT: IGNORED.

## Page 4 - Italy

In Italy, it goes further. In 1919, more than a million workers go on strike. In 1920, half a
million take over their factories. [C1-13]

**On the page:** an engraved factory. Typed: 1919: OVER 1,000,000 ON STRIKE / 1920: ABOUT 500,000
TAKE OVER THEIR FACTORIES, the number circled in red.

## Page 5 - The capital order

Now look at it from the owners' side. This isn't just about pay. It threatens what Mattei calls
the capital order: a few people own the land, the factories and the mines, and everyone else
must work for a wage to survive. If workers can run things themselves, that order could fall.
[C1-15]

**On the page (the test picture):** an engraved temple front. On its roof: THE CAPITAL ORDER.
One column: A FEW OWN THE LAND, FACTORIES AND MINES. The other: EVERYONE ELSE WORKS FOR A WAGE.

## Page 6 - The experts

So the experts get together. Brussels, 1920: thirty-nine countries send delegates, mostly
bankers and treasury officials. Guess who isn't in the room? Not one trade unionist. Genoa, 1922:
same advice. And it's simple. Balance the budget. Cut public spending. Stop creating new money.
Hand interest rates to central banks, out of reach of voters. And get back on gold.
[C1-16, C1-17, C1-18, C1-19]

**On the page:** typed notes: BRUSSELS 1920, 39 COUNTRIES, BANKERS AND TREASURY OFFICIALS. Red pen:
"TRADE UNIONISTS: 0". Then GENOA 1922 and the five pieces of advice typed one by one, each ticked.

## Page 7 - Three parts

Mattei says the plan has three parts. Spending: cut public services, and tax everyday purchases
instead of wealth. Money: make borrowing expensive, so businesses slow down and jobs disappear.
Work: cut wages, sell off what the public owns, weaken the unions. [C1-21]

**On the page:** three tags on a twine line, SPENDING / MONEY / WORK, each filling with typed lines.

## Page 8 - The official story

The official story sounds reasonable. Prices are rising, war debts are huge, and a government,
like a household, has to balance its books. Right? But economists who study how modern money
works say a country that issues its own money isn't a household. It can't run out of its own
money. It can run out of workers, materials and machines, as the war just showed. And gold? Gold
was a limit they chose. Going back to it meant forcing wages and prices down.
[C1-27, C1-28, C1-26, C1-24, C1-25]

**On the page:** two cards: A HOUSEHOLD CAN RUN OUT OF MONEY / A COUNTRY THAT ISSUES ITS OWN MONEY
CAN'T. IT CAN RUN OUT OF WORKERS, MATERIALS, MACHINES. An engraved gold bar; red pen draws a
padlock on it: "A LIMIT THEY CHOSE".

## Page 9 - Why

So why do it? Mattei's answer is blunt: power. Take away the alternatives, like public housing
and help for the unemployed, and people must take whatever job is going, at whatever wage. The
message to workers: work more, consume less. [C1-22, C1-12, C1-21, C1-23]

**On the page:** a brown wage envelope pinned up. Cards PUBLIC HOUSING and HELP FOR THE
UNEMPLOYED crossed out in red. Cut-out letters: WORK MORE, CONSUME LESS.

## Page 10 - Sound familiar?

That's Mattei's argument. Austerity was never really about the books. It was about power. Sound
familiar? Don't just take her word for it. Next: where austerity was tested first, and what it
did to people. [C1-22]

**On the page:** red pen "SOUND FAMILIAR?". A rubber stamp: CHAPTER 1. Typed: NEXT: WHERE IT WAS
TESTED FIRST.

## A quick listener test (from your toolkit)

Nobody has heard this yet, so whether it is easy to follow is untested. After a first listen,
ask someone (or yourself):

1. In one sentence, why does Mattei say austerity was invented?
2. What is "the capital order"?
3. Why does the film say a country isn't a household?

If any answer comes back wrong, that page needs rewording.

## Traps

- "Mattei says", "Mattei calls", "Mattei's answer", "That's Mattei's argument" mark her
  interpretation. Urgent wording must not turn these into plain facts.
- Genoa was a meeting of heads of government; only Brussels is "mostly bankers and treasury
  officials".
- "Not one trade unionist" is about Brussels (The Conversation's account); keep it there.
- One word for each thing in the narration: workers (not "working people"), owners (not "bosses").
