# 08 Test findings on the combined script

Source: Clara E. Mattei, *The Capital Order* (2022).

## fresh-newcomers

WHAT I DID: I created the work folder (SCRATCH/script-rewrite/work-test-fresh-newcomers, left empty; findings are returned here). I read the narration once, page by page, at about 140 words a minute, and answered the five comprehension probes from the toolkit's pinker-teach-for-understanding/SKILL.md (reconstruction, terms, hesitation, examples, application). I opened the ledger only to check that each fix I suggest is supported. I did not open the old script or the drafts. IMPORTANT: these three listeners are my simulation, not real people. The script is still untested with real newcomers.

OVERALL: the two-factories picture works. All three listeners can say the core rule back, and they answer the hospitals case correctly. The guesses land, and "prices go up more slowly. Not down" comes through. The failures are in the links around the rule:
- the closing "Spent on what?" is never taught;
- New Zealand's price outcome is never said;
- the Reserve Bank is never introduced;
- page 2 packs the hardest idea into 18 seconds;
- Mattei's view is missing the step that connects it to prices;
- "a rise in costs" uses a word the script has already used for shop prices.

STUDENT, 17

- Terms: inflation, the quiet and flat-out factories, spare and interest are all fine. The Reserve Bank is only a name; they assume it is the government. "Economists who study how modern money works" sounds like "the experts". "Spent on what" means "what they buy, like hospitals or a war". "A rise in costs" just means "prices went up".
- Hesitations:
  - page 2: "then why can't the government pay for everything?";
  - pages 10-11: "did prices go up here? it never says";
  - page 12: "is 170,000 a lot?";
  - page 13: "how does weaker pay slow prices?".
- Examples: Britain and Japan are clear. For Glasgow: "where's the new money?" New Zealand is clear, but they assume prices went up.
- New case (hospitals while builders are flat out): prices go up. Correct.
- Drought with no new money: unsure whether it is the flat-out factory (like the sunk ships) or a rise in costs.

WHERE THE LISTENERS NEED TO HEAR IT AGAIN: pages 2, 11 and 13 for everyone; also pages 3-4, 7 and 14 for the listener whose first language isn't English.

1. **must-fix, page 15:** "Spent on what?"
   - Problem: The closing question, the line viewers are meant to keep, is never taught. The "on what" half first appears on page 9 ("It's whether it's spent... and on what"), but no case compares two things the money could be spent on. Glasgow is never labelled as an "on what" example. The factory stands for a whole country, which has no "what" to choose between. In the simulation, the student heard it as "what they buy, like hospitals or a war". The listener whose first language isn't English heard "good things or bad things". Only the builder linked it to whether builders are free. In a series about austerity, the bare question sounds like a question about values, not about spare workers, materials and machines. So viewers can leave with the wrong rule.
   - Suggested fix: Make the question carry the rule, in the same words both times.
- Page 9: "It's whether it's spent... and on what. On things there are spare workers, materials and machines to make... or not."
- Page 15: "Spent on what... and are there spare workers, materials and machines to make it?"
- Give one real "on what" case. The best is New Zealand in 2022, using ledger C2-N02 (Stats NZ: the rise was "driven mainly by construction of new homes and rents"). It links Glasgow's homes to New Zealand's homes and on to the hospitals transfer question.
2. **should-fix, page 11:** "Close to the flat-out factory. So what's the usual cure, when prices go up too fast?"
   - Problem: New Zealand's outcome is never said. The listener hears that almost everyone had a job, and then "So what's the usual cure, when prices go up too fast?". The step "and prices went up fast" is left for the listener to fill in. The student asked "did prices go up here?". Without it, the home case only shows how to sort a country into a factory. It never shows the rule working. The answer also comes with no reason and no pause, and then the densest page begins. That page has seven steps in about 32 seconds, and the listener whose first language isn't English needed it again.
   - Suggested fix: After the answer, add the reason and the outcome:
"Close to the flat-out factory. Almost no one was waiting at the gate. By the middle of twenty twenty-two, prices here were going up seven point three percent a year... the fastest in thirty-two years." (C2-N01)
- Optionally add: "Mostly from building new homes, and rents." (C2-N02)
- Think about putting these lines on their own short page, so page 11 starts at the cure.
3. **should-fix, page 2:** "Where does new money come from? Here, the Reserve Bank can create it."
   - Problem: The Reserve Bank is the main actor in the chapter, but it is never introduced.
- Page 1's warning names "the government", page 2 names the Reserve Bank, and page 10 has both. The student assumed they were the same thing.
- "Japan's version of our Reserve Bank" (page 8) and "Banks like our Reserve Bank" (page 13) only work if the viewer already knows what ours does.
- The listener whose first language isn't English knows a central bank from home, but not this name.
- "Here" can be heard as "Here's the answer" rather than "in New Zealand".
   - Suggested fix: "In New Zealand, it's the Reserve Bank... the bank whose job is to stop prices going up too fast. It can create new money."
- This is supported by C2-M15 (inflation targeting began here around 1990).
- It also names the actor behind page 1's "usual cure".
4. **should-fix, page 2:** "Economists who study how modern money works say a country like ours can't run out of its own money. What it can run out of... is workers, materials and machines."
   - Problem: This is the hardest idea in the chapter, and it gets about 18 seconds and the longest sentence (19 words).
- "A country like ours" and "its own money" carry the key condition without explaining it. In C2-M12 the condition is a country that borrows in its own currency. The listener hears "rich" or "like New Zealand".
- The unnamed group sounds like the whole profession. The ledger marks this as one school's view, and it has critics.
- The student and the builder both stalled on "then why can't the government pay for everything?".
- The sentence that starts "What it can run out of" made the listener whose first language isn't English replay it.
   - Suggested fix: "Some economists, who study how modern money works, say a country that makes its own money, like ours, can't run out of it. But it can run out of workers, materials and machines."
- "Some" shows it is a view, not settled fact. The wording still never names the school.
5. **should-fix, page 10:** "When Covid hit in twenty twenty, the Reserve Bank created new money... and the government spent more."
   - Problem: Japan has just taught that most of its new money "never reached the factory". Nothing then says why New Zealand's would. The listener assumes the Reserve Bank's new money was spent this time. The student asked "why was ours spent and Japan's not?". The ledger is weaker than that assumption. C2-N06 says the money created here also "sits as bank reserves", and economists argue over how much reached spending. The builder also wanted to know what the money was spent on, and the script never says.
   - Suggested fix: Make the spending that was clearly spent the government's spending, and say what it was spent on:
"...and the government spent much more... including paying wages so people could keep their jobs." (C2-N14: wage subsidies supported over a million workers)
- Don't imply the Reserve Bank's own new money is what got spent.
- Have a fact checker confirm the contrast wording.
6. **should-fix, page 13:** "She argues that putting people out of work is how the cure works. With more people at the gate, she says, the workers inside have less power to ask for more pay."
   - Problem: The two views sound the same to a newcomer. Page 11 has already said the cure works by making businesses hire fewer people, so "how the cure works" sounds like agreement. Mattei's last sentence then stops before it gets back to prices. The student asked "so what if workers can't ask for more pay... how does that slow prices?".
   - Suggested fix: Make the two mechanisms visibly different, and finish her chain.
- Banks: "Banks like ours say the cure works by cutting spending... job losses are unfortunate, but not the goal." (C2-M17, C2-XD01)
- Mattei: "...less power to ask for more pay. Pay rises get the blame for prices going up, she says... so the cure works by holding pay down." (C2-H18; keep it said as hers)
7. **should-fix, page 12:** "By the middle of twenty twenty-six, about a hundred and seventy thousand people were out of work."
   - Problem: Page 10 counted people out of work as "three in a hundred". Page 12 switches to a total, so no listener could tell how much worse things got. The builder asked "out of how many?". A big number is also hard to catch by ear for the listener whose first language isn't English. "Raised its interest rate again and again" sounds as if it went on until 2026. That makes "isn't the only reason" hard to understand. In fact the rate was cut from August 2024 (C2-N10, C2-XD05).
   - Suggested fix: Use the same unit as page 10, and date the rises:
"From late twenty twenty-one to the middle of twenty twenty-three, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again and again... By the middle of twenty twenty-six, nearly six in a hundred were out of work... the most in about ten years." (C2-N10, C2-XD05: 5.6%, highest since 2015)
- Swap the total for this rather than adding to it.
- Relabel the on-screen card to match.
8. **should-fix, page 14:** "That's a rise in costs... not in spending."
   - Problem: Throughout the script, "costs more" has meant the shop price ("the basket costs more") or the price of a loan ("loans cost more"). So "a rise in costs" is heard as "prices went up", and the contrast is lost. "Spending" is a new name at minute six. Two lines earlier, "Without the rise in petrol and diesel... two point nine" drops both the verb and "percent". The listener whose first language isn't English and the builder both lost the second figure. Nothing tells the listener whether four point one counts as "too fast".
   - Suggested fix: "Without the rise in petrol and diesel, they'd have gone up two point nine percent." (C2-N05)
"That's a rise in what it costs to make and move things... not new money being spent."
- This keeps the existing names "new money" and "spent".
9. **should-fix, page 6:** "More money... and fewer things to buy."
   - Problem: There is a near miss the script never separates. Britain's sunk ships (fewer things, caused from outside) are filed under "close to the flat-out factory". Page 14's fuel rise (also from outside) is filed as "a rise in costs" that hits "both factories". On a drought case with no new money, the simulated listeners split between "flat-out factory, like the sunk ships" and "a rise in costs".
   - Suggested fix: On page 6, name both pushes: "Two things pushed prices up at once... more money, and fewer things to buy." On page 14, point back to it: the fuel rise is the second kind of push, the kind the sunk ships were. Have a fact checker review the wording against C2-XA01/XA02, which I did not open.
10. **should-fix, page 7:** "Homes worked like the flat-out factory too."
   - Problem: Four problems:
- The factory picture silently changes from "a whole country" to "one kind of thing".
- There is no new money in this story, so the student asked "what's this got to do with new money?".
- The ending (Parliament brought rents back) has no stated point, so listeners didn't know why the story stops there or who gained and who paid.
- The listener whose first language isn't English was thrown by "Homes worked".
   - Suggested fix: Open with: "A factory can also stand for one kind of thing... like homes." Close with who gained and who paid: "Landlords gained. Families paid... until they refused, and won." This also prepares the viewer for "spent on what".
11. **should-fix, page 15:** "And who pays to stop it?"
   - Problem: "It" could mean the warning, the new money, or prices going up. "Pays" has meant rent, paying back a loan and wages earlier in the script. Here it is used in a figurative sense, and the listener whose first language isn't English heard "who gives money to stop it".
   - Suggested fix: Reuse page 12's exact words: "And who pays for the cure?"
12. **should-fix, page 3:** "Now new money is spent on what it makes."
   - Problem: "It" can point back to "new money", which is the subject of the same sentence: money spent on what the money makes. The listener whose first language isn't English took it that way. The sentence also hides who spends. It is the core action of the picture and is said twice (again on page 4).
   - Suggested fix: "Now new money is spent buying what the factory makes." On page 4: "The same new money is spent buying what this factory makes."
13. **minor, page 4:** "Call it the flat-out factory."
   - Problem: The builder and the student know "flat out". For a listener whose first language isn't English, "flat" can mean unchanging ("prices stayed flat"), which is the opposite meaning. In New Zealand it can also mean a rented home. Japan's picture is an almost flat line. The meaning given just before the name helps.
   - Suggested fix: Keep the name. Put the meaning on screen with it (FLAT OUT = WORKING AS HARD AS THEY CAN). Never use the word "flat" on Japan's line.
14. **minor, page 5:** "When there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up."
   - Problem: The listener has to fill in what "aren't" refers to across a pause. The listener whose first language isn't English lost it the first time (it is repeated on page 15).
   - Suggested fix: "When there aren't any spare...". Change it the same way on both pages so the rule stays word for word.
15. **minor, page 13:** "Banks like our Reserve Bank see putting people out of work as unfortunate... but not the goal."
   - Problem: By ear, "Banks like our Reserve Bank" first sounds like "banks are fond of our Reserve Bank". The listener whose first language isn't English took it that way before recovering.
   - Suggested fix: "Our Reserve Bank, and banks like it, see..."
16. **minor, page 11:** "In our picture, the factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate."
   - Problem: After "two factories", "the factory" doesn't say which one. The listener has to work out that the flat-out factory is slowing.
   - Suggested fix: "In our picture, the flat-out factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate."
17. **minor, page 9:** "One reason economists give... most of the new money just sat in the banks."
   - Problem: The builder asked "why make it if it just sits there?", and nothing answers that. On page 8, "the money it had created" was heard as "all of Japan's money". That doesn't hurt the lesson, but it is vague.
   - Suggested fix: This is acceptable as it stands, since it is honestly given as "one reason". Optionally, on page 8: "set out to double, in two years, all the money it had created" (C2-XB03).
18. **minor, page 1:** "the usual cure makes borrowing cost more. And that can put people out of work."
   - Problem: The listener has to carry this link unexplained for about four minutes until page 11. It works as a hook, but page 11 explains it with different words ("loans cost more", not "borrowing"), so the answer is not clearly heard as the payoff.
   - Suggested fix: Use one name. Either say "makes loans cost more" on page 1, or say "borrowing costs more" on pages 11-12.
19. **minor, page 7:** "So landlords raised the rent... in places by up to a quarter. By November, about twenty thousand families were refusing to pay the extra."
   - Problem: "By up to a quarter" and "By November" (no year) were hard for the listener whose first language isn't English. The on-screen +25% helps only if they are watching.
   - Suggested fix: Keep the +25% card in sync with the words. Optionally say "in places, a quarter more".
20. **minor, page 4:** "What do you think happens? Have a guess."
   - Problem: Three seconds was short for the listener whose first language isn't English to form a guess. This applies to the guesses on pages 4, 8 and 10.
   - Suggested fix: Hold for about 4 seconds, or put a small on-screen marker that the video is waiting for a guess.

## facts

I checked every factual sentence on all 15 pages, plus the numbers planned for the screen, against the ledger entries each page cites. I read each entry's claim, plain version, confidence and notes, as well as the 12 October second fact check and the Traps. Most lines hold: Britain's prices roughly doubling, the submarines, "partly with new money", Glasgow's "up to a quarter", "about twenty thousand families", "refusing to pay the extra" and "within weeks", Japan's doubling and "about half a percent", the usual cure step by step, the 170,000 total with its date, the 4.1 and 2.9 with Stats NZ's petrol-and-diesel wording, and Mattei marked as hers twice.

Four must-fixes:
(1) The rule on pages 5 and 15 says "new money mostly gets more things made". The ledger (C2-XA03) is about extra spending, and the script's own Japan page shows new money that sat in the banks and got nothing made.
(2) Page 10's "lowest since records began" has lost the 1986 qualification. The 12 October check had put LOWEST SINCE 1986 on screen because older counts show New Zealand had almost no unemployment in the 1950s to 70s. The combined script took that off the screen and doesn't say it either, though the helpers' plan had it.
(3) Page 11 goes from "close to the flat-out factory" straight to the cure for prices going up too fast. A newcomer will take it that New Zealand's 2021-22 price rise came from the economy running flat out. The ledger (C2-N16) says no one has split that rise, and world prices such as oil were a big part.
(4) Page 14's "That's a rise in costs... not in spending", which answers "And the checkout?", will be heard as the whole 4.1 percent. That is the C2-M18 "mostly fuel" trap in other words. The listener test's question 4 expects that same wrong answer.

Should-fixes:
- Page 2 answers "where does new money come from" with only the Reserve Bank. Most new money is made by ordinary banks lending.
- Page 2 says "a country" can't run out of its own money. The view is about a government that issues it.
- Page 6 says fewer than one in a hundred were out of work throughout the war. The entry only supports "fell below".
- Page 7 drops "homes were already short".
- Page 11's New Zealand answer is the review's judgement but is said in the narrator's voice.
- Page 13's "how the cure works" doesn't clash with the banks' view. Mattei's claim is that putting people out of work is the main aim.
- Page 14's figures will be replaced on 22 October.

Every fix is a few words. Together they add about 40 to 70 words, so they will use up the ready cuts. My work folder is SCRATCH/script-rewrite/work-test-facts. Nothing outside it was changed, and there are no files in it.

1. **must-fix, page 5:** "So when there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more things made. When there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up."
   - Problem: This claims more than the entry. C2-XA03's plain version is about extra spending ("With idle workers and machines, extra spending mostly raises output"). It says nothing about new money as such. The script's own Japan case (page 9, C2-C06, C2-XB03) had spare workers and machines (weak demand), doubled its new money, and most of it "just sat in the banks". So new money did not mostly get more things made there. The rule as worded is the sentence viewers are meant to remember, and the film's own evidence contradicts it.
   - Suggested fix: Say "new money that's spent": "So when there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money that's spent mostly gets more things made. When there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up." Make the same change on page 15 so the two stay word for word. Add IF SPENT to the rule cards on screen.
2. **must-fix, page 15:** "Does new money always push prices up? No. When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more things made."
   - Problem: This repeats page 5's rule straight after the Japan pages, where new money was created with spare capacity and mostly sat in the banks. As worded, the closing rule is stronger than C2-XA03 (which is about extra spending) and contradicts page 9.
   - Suggested fix: "When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money that's spent mostly gets more things made. When there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up." This matches the fixed page 5.
3. **must-fix, page 10:** "Only about three in a hundred were out of work... the lowest since records began."
   - Problem: The qualification has been dropped. C2-N09's note says "'Lowest ever recorded' means since the series began in 1986". The 12 October final reviewer warned that older official counts show New Zealand had almost no unemployment in the 1950s to early 1970s, so "viewers who remember that era could call this wrong". That check changed the screen to LOWEST SINCE 1986. The combined script removes 1986 from the screen (LOWEST SINCE RECORDS BEGAN) and doesn't say it either, so the correction has been undone. The helpers' plan (understanding.md) had "since records began in nineteen eighty-six".
   - Suggested fix: Narration: "...the lowest since this count began, in nineteen eighty-six." Screen: LOWEST SINCE 1986, as the 12 October check set. If one less figure is wanted, say "the lowest in more than thirty years" (screen: LOWEST IN 30+ YEARS). Either is supported by C2-N09.
4. **must-fix, page 11:** "Close to the flat-out factory. So what's the usual cure, when prices go up too fast?"
   - Problem: This states a cause by implication where the ledger supports less. The script never says that New Zealand's prices went up in 2021-22, or why. It goes from "close to the flat-out factory" with "So" to the cure for prices going up too fast, and then to the rate rises from late 2021. A newcomer applying the model will conclude that New Zealand's price rise came from the economy running flat out. Ledger C2-N16 says no published estimate splits it between government spending, Reserve Bank money and world prices, with "evidence on both sides": imported prices rose 8.2 percent against 6.6 percent for home-grown at the end of 2022 (C2-N03), and petrol rose 32 percent and diesel 74 percent in the year to June 2022 (C2-N02). Page 14's costs lesson is only about 2026, so the film ends up teaching "2021-22 was spending, 2026 is costs".
   - Suggested fix: After "Close to the flat-out factory." add: "And prices here went up fast... partly from that, and partly from world prices, like oil." Cite C2-N01 (fastest in thirty-two years), C2-N16 and C2-N02/C2-N03. The next line, "So what's the usual cure, when prices go up too fast?", then follows on. About 15 words.
5. **must-fix, page 14:** "So prices go up in both factories... new money or not. That's a rise in costs... not in spending."
   - Problem: This page answers "And the checkout?", the film's opening question, and its last line will be heard as the reason for the whole 4.1 percent. That is the C2-M18 trap in other words: fuel adds about 1.2 of the 4.1 points, and prices without petrol and diesel still rose 2.9 percent. The ledger gives no cause for that 2.9 percent, and it does not say the 2026 rise was "not in spending" (C2-N05: cause not researched; C2-M04: one bank's attribution, seen in search only). The file's listener test question 4 ("Was that new money? If they say yes, page 14 is too fast") expects viewers to conclude the whole rise wasn't new money, which the ledger doesn't support.
   - Suggested fix: Narration: "The fuel part is a rise in costs... not in spending." Screen card: THE FUEL PART: A RISE IN COSTS. Change listener test question 4 to "Was the fuel part of that rise caused by new money?"
6. **should-fix, page 2:** "Where does new money come from? Here, the Reserve Bank can create it."
   - Problem: The ledger supports less than the answer implies. Asked as a question and answered with only the Reserve Bank, it teaches that the Reserve Bank is where new money comes from. C2-M15 says most money is created by ordinary banks making loans, and the page's own research note admits this ("can"), but the narration doesn't. Page 1's warning also says "the government creates new money".
   - Suggested fix: Change the question so the answer isn't heard as the only source: "Who can create new money? Here, the Reserve Bank can." Rename the page "Who can create new money".
7. **should-fix, page 2:** "Economists who study how modern money works say a country like ours can't run out of its own money."
   - Problem: This shifts the view's subject. C2-M12's plain version is "A government that issues its own money can't run out of it", and it applies only to countries that borrow in their own currency. "A country" includes its households and businesses, which certainly can run out of money, so a newcomer hears a claim the economists don't make.
   - Suggested fix: "Economists who study how modern money works say a government like ours can't run out of the money it issues." The next line then becomes "What it can run out of...", which still works.
8. **should-fix, page 6:** "Britain, during the First World War. Fewer than one worker in a hundred was out of work."
   - Problem: This is stronger than the entry. C2-H02 gives annual figures under one percent only for 1915 to 1918 (3.16 percent in 1914), and its plain version says it "fell below one in a hundred". The 12 October check found early-1915 months slightly above one percent and called "under 1%" for 1915 right "but only just". "During the war... was" says throughout the war. The entry's reason ("millions were in uniform") is also dropped, though it is what makes workers not spare.
   - Suggested fix: "Britain, during the First World War. With millions in uniform, fewer than one worker in a hundred was soon out of work." Keep the screen card FEWER THAN 1 IN 100 OUT OF WORK.
9. **should-fix, page 7:** "Early in the war, crowds of war workers came to Glasgow, a British city. With builders and materials gone to the war, few new homes were built."
   - Problem: The entry's qualification is dropped and the cause narrowed. C2-XB01 says the influx was "exacerbating an already acute ... housing crisis", and its plain version says "where homes were already short". The 12 October check adds that Glasgow house building had already slumped before 1914. The script makes the war's pull on builders and materials the only reason homes were short, which also rests on a quote the checker saw second-hand in search results only (Merrett and Sykes). The helpers' plan had "where homes are already short".
   - Suggested fix: "Early in the war, crowds of war workers came to Glasgow, a British city, where homes were already short. And with builders and materials gone to the war, few new homes were built." About 6 words more.
10. **should-fix, page 11:** "Close to the flat-out factory."
   - Problem: The New Zealand answer is the independent review's judgement, said in the narrator's voice. With the review line cut, nothing on the page backs it except 3.2 percent. C2-N09's note says calling 3.2 percent "unsustainable" is the review's judgement and that "others would see a tight labour market as a good outcome". C2-N15 ("overheated", money from the Reserve Bank's bond buying "later contributed to excess demand") is a government-commissioned review, released in an election year. C2-N06's note also says the Reserve Bank's new money sat as bank reserves and "economists argue over how much this reaches spending". That is the very thing page 9 taught about Japan, and the page leaves it unanswered.
   - Suggested fix: Bring back the review line already drafted in combined.md, shortened, straight after the answer: "Looking back, two outside experts said the new money and the extra government spending were right at first... but too strong, for too long." Keep "looking back" and keep it away from house prices (C2-N15, C2-XC04). Pay for it from the four ready cuts.
11. **should-fix, page 13:** "disagrees. She argues that putting people out of work is how the cure works."
   - Problem: Mattei's claim is understated, so the two views don't actually clash. The banks' own account also says the cure works partly through fewer jobs (C2-M17: Powell, "softening of labor market conditions", "some pain"). They deny it is the goal, not that it happens. Mattei's claim in the ledger is about the aim: "The main objective of interest rate hikes is to cool down the labor market" and "unemployment serves as the best disciplinary device to suppress wages" (C2-H18). Some newcomers will hear "disagrees" and not know what she disagrees about.
   - Suggested fix: "She argues that putting people out of work is the main aim of the cure." Keep the next sentence, with "she says", unchanged. Screen: MATTEI ARGUES: THE MAIN AIM. It stays marked as hers (C2-H18).
12. **should-fix, page 14:** "In the year to June twenty twenty-six, prices here went up four point one percent."
   - Problem: This figure goes out of date soon. The September-quarter price figures come out on 22 October 2026, seven days from now (C2-N05, C2-XA05, C2-XC05). Tied to the June year, the line stays true, but it will no longer answer "And the checkout?" for a viewer. Page 1's "costs more than it did a year ago" also rests on it. Page 14's lesson needs a visible gap between prices with and without petrol and diesel, and the new figures may not show one.
   - Suggested fix: Before recording or publishing after 22 October, add the September figures to the ledger first. Then either switch to them, keeping Stats NZ's petrol-and-diesel wording, or keep the June year as worded. Never write "mostly fuel".
13. **minor, page 8:** "If the warning is right, prices should go up fast."
   - Problem: This leaves out context in C2-C06 and C2-C07. Japan's Bank was trying to push prices up, aiming for 2 percent a year within about two years, after about fifteen years of falling prices. Without that, a newcomer may think the Bank feared price rises and escaped them by luck. With it, the point is stronger: even trying, it fell short.
   - Suggested fix: Optional, if there is room: "It even wanted prices to go up. If the warning is right, they should go up fast." No figure is needed.
14. **minor, page 10:** "By the end of twenty twenty-one, almost everyone who wanted a job had one."
   - Problem: This goes a little beyond the entry. The 3.2 percent (C2-N09) counts people without a job who are looking for one. People who want a job but aren't looking aren't in it, so "wanted" is broader than the ledger supports.
   - Suggested fix: "By the end of twenty twenty-one, almost everyone looking for work had a job."
15. **minor, page 12:** "By the middle of twenty twenty-six, about a hundred and seventy thousand people were out of work."
   - Problem: There are two small risks. (1) The 171,000 is from the June 2026 quarter's adjusted series (C2-XD05), and that series gets revised at the next release on 4 November, as March's figure was. "About" will almost certainly survive, but check it. (2) Under "Who pays for the cure?" the whole 170,000 can be heard as the cure's toll, though about three in a hundred were out of work even at the 2021 low (C2-N09), and the rise went on while the rate was being cut (C2-XD05). "The interest rate isn't the only reason for that" covers this, so it is acceptable.
   - Suggested fix: After 4 November, re-check that the June-quarter count is still about 170,000. Never cut "The interest rate isn't the only reason for that."
16. **minor, page 11:** "And prices go up more slowly. Not down... just more slowly."
   - Problem: A qualification is dropped. The cure works with a delay: its biggest effect comes after one to two years (C2-M17, RBA), and C2-XD01's note says "It works with a lag". This isn't wrong as it stands, but page 12 then puts the 2021-23 rises next to 2026 jobs without saying the effects take time.
   - Suggested fix: Optional: "And, after a year or two, prices go up more slowly." Or leave it, since nothing on the page claims a speed.
17. **minor, page 7:** "Within weeks, Britain's Parliament brought rents on small homes back to what they were before the war."
   - Problem: The narration is correct (C2-XB02). But the file's description says the families "refused the extra and won", and C2-XB02 notes the law was meant to be temporary and was weakened after 1915. If "won" reaches the screen or a caption, it is stronger than the entry.
   - Suggested fix: Keep the narration. Keep the screen stamp as RENTS BACK TO BEFORE THE WAR and never write WON. In the file's description, change "and won" to "and rents were brought back down".
18. **minor, page 13:** "The economist Clara E. /məˈteɪi/, whose book this series follows"
   - Problem: There is no id for "economist". It is supported by chapter 1's ledger, C1-01 ("Economist Clara E. Mattei..."), which page 13 doesn't cite. Page 13 also cites C2-XD02, which is about the 1920 Bank of England rise and isn't used on this page.
   - Suggested fix: Add C1-01 to page 13's research ids. Keep C2-H18 as the source for both Mattei sentences. C2-XD02 can stay as background but isn't the source of any line.

## rules-and-voice

Source: Clara E. Mattei, The Capital Order (2022).

Result: the combined script keeps every hard rule. I found no rule breaks. It has 5 should-fix and 7 minor wording problems, mostly words that mean two things and lines a first-time listener could mishear. All the fixes together add 12 words and about 6 seconds.

WHAT PASSED (checked line by line, plus the project's voice checker and sound scan, re-run in my folder):
- The money view is never named, and there is no "MMT". Mattei is written /məˈteɪi/. Ruhr no longer appears, because Germany was cut.
- No capitals for emphasis, no brackets, no exclamation marks. The only bracket should be the one accent line added at recording time.
- Every number and year is written as said. The only non-English letters are the three sound symbols in /məˈteɪi/.
- There are 28 "..." pauses, all at real turns in the sentence.
- Never used: "I" or "we", party politics, "cost" or "price" as a figure of speech, "unemployment", "central bank", "printing", bare "rates".
- Mattei's view is marked as hers twice ("She argues", "she says").
- Naomi Klein: none of her titles, coined phrases or near-copies. The only hits are two uses of "double", which is plain English and was already cleared in the voice brief. Her spirit is there: stakes on page 1, the viewer's own questions answered within a breath, landlords against families, people counted as people, and a closing pair of questions.
- Pinker: his principles without his manner. None of his habits ("to be sure", "consider", witty asides).
- Stock phrases: "It matters... because" (page 1) and "next time you hear the warning" (page 15) are familiar formulas, but each carries specific content, so keep them.
- The figures in the script's notes are right: about 24 spoken figures, sentences average 9 words, the longest is 19, and about 5,400 characters to send to the voice (5,439 with the accent line, about US$1).

ONE NAME PER THING: these are the swaps I found.
1. "banks" means two things: ordinary banks on page 9 ("sat in the banks") and banks like the Reserve Bank on page 13 ("Banks like our Reserve Bank").
2. "more people at the gate" (page 13) against "workers wait at the gate" (pages 3 and 11).
3. The noun "rise", used twice on page 14, against "go up" everywhere else.
4. "spending" (page 14) as a new name for "new money... spent".
5. Who creates new money: "the government" (page 1, the warning) against "the Reserve Bank" (page 2).

These are fine and need no change:
- "the cure" as a short form of "the usual cure";
- "more money" on pages 5 and 6, which describes an amount;
- "the money it had created" on page 8, which is Japan's whole amount and so a different thing;
- the job wording around "out of work";
- "the factory" used in general on pages 9 and 11.

Every other term is explained in plain words where it first appears, or is avoided.

1. **should-fix, page 15:** "Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?"
   - Problem: This is the line viewers carry away, and its "it" is unclear. "Spent on what?" is about the new money, so "stop it" can be heard as "stop the new money", which is the wrong idea. It could also attach to "the warning", the nearest noun. It also doesn't repeat page 12's question in the same words, so the refrain is lost.
   - Suggested fix: "Spent on what? And who pays for the cure?" This is the same 6 words and says page 12's question again word for word. Change the card WHO PAYS TO STOP IT? to WHO PAYS FOR THE CURE?
2. **should-fix, page 13:** "The economist Clara E. /məˈteɪi/, whose book this series follows, disagrees. She argues that putting people out of work is how the cure works."
   - Problem: Two problems. First, the aside splits "The economist" from its verb "disagrees", which the teaching plan's rules for the ear forbid. Second, the disagreement can't be heard. Page 11 has already told viewers, as plain fact, that the cure works through fewer jobs ("hire fewer people... more workers wait at the gate"). So "she argues ... is how the cure works" sounds like something the film already said, and the listener test's question ("a fact, or someone's argument?") may fail.
   - Suggested fix: "Clara E. /məˈteɪi/ is the economist whose book this series follows. She argues that putting people out of work isn't just unfortunate... it's how the cure works." This adds 3 words and 1 pause. It reuses the banks' own word, so the contrast can be heard. The claim is still the ledger's plain wording for C2-H18 ("works by putting people out of work"); the fact tester should confirm.
3. **should-fix, page 13:** "Banks like our Reserve Bank see putting people out of work as unfortunate... but not the goal."
   - Problem: The word "banks" names two different things. On page 9, "most of the new money just sat in the banks" means ordinary banks. Here, "Banks" means banks like the Reserve Bank. That breaks the one-name rule the other way round: one word for two things.
   - Suggested fix: "Our Reserve Bank, and others like it, see putting people out of work as unfortunate... but not the goal." Leave page 9's everyday "the banks" alone. Card: OUR RESERVE BANK AND OTHERS LIKE IT: UNFORTUNATE, NOT THE GOAL.
4. **should-fix, page 14:** "Without the rise in petrol and diesel... two point nine."
   - Problem: This is a squeezed "what if" that a newcomer may not unpack: what was the price without what? It has no "percent", and the noun "rise" breaks the script's one name, "go up". The listener test's question 4 aims right at this line.
   - Suggested fix: "If petrol and diesel prices hadn't gone up... two point nine percent." This adds 2 words. It is also closer to Stats NZ's own wording ("If petrol and diesel prices had not changed", from the second fact check).
5. **should-fix, page 3:** "Now new money is spent on what it makes."
   - Problem: The nearest noun before "it makes" is "new money", so the line can be heard as "what the money makes". This line sets up "whether it's spent... and on what" and "Spent on what?". Page 4 says it again: "The same new money is spent on what it makes."
   - Suggested fix: "Now new money is spent on what the factory makes." Make the same change on page 4: "The same new money is spent on what the factory makes." That is 1 extra word in each place.
6. **minor, page 2:** "Where does new money come from? Here, the Reserve Bank can create it."
   - Problem: Straight after a question, "Here," can be heard as "Here's the answer" rather than "in New Zealand". The Reserve Bank is never introduced, although the teaching plan asked for it on page 2. Page 1 has just said the government creates new money, so a newcomer may wonder who actually does it.
   - Suggested fix: "Here in New Zealand, our Reserve Bank can create it." This adds 3 words. "Our" is the script's own word for facts New Zealanders share.
7. **minor, page 13:** "With more people at the gate, she says, the workers inside have less power to ask for more pay."
   - Problem: This swaps the name in the picture's refrain. Pages 3 and 11 say "workers wait at the gate". This page says "people at the gate".
   - Suggested fix: "With more workers waiting at the gate, she says, those inside have less power to ask for more pay." Still 19 words. "Those inside" avoids saying "workers" twice.
8. **minor, page 7:** "Homes worked like the flat-out factory too. Early in the war, crowds of war workers came to Glasgow, a British city."
   - Problem: The general idea comes before the scene. That goes against the rules' "a concrete case before each general idea" and Klein's habit of opening on the scene. "Homes worked" can also stumble on the ear, as if the homes did some work.
   - Suggested fix: "Early in the war, crowds of war workers came to Glasgow, a British city. Its homes were like the flat-out factory too. With builders and materials gone to the war..." The word count stays about the same.
9. **minor, page 7:** "By November, about twenty thousand families were refusing to pay the extra."
   - Problem: With the year gone and only "Early in the war" before it, a listener who knows the war began in August 1914 will place this in November 1914. It was November 1915 (C2-XB01). This is a small date slip caused by the wording.
   - Suggested fix: Either "By November nineteen fifteen, about twenty thousand families..." (2 more words and one more figure), or accept it knowingly. Ask the fact tester to decide.
10. **minor, page 14:** "That's a rise in costs... not in spending."
   - Problem: Two new names for the chapter's two causes arrive in the last 7 words: "rise" as a noun, and "spending". Everywhere else the cause is "new money... spent", and its effect "pushes prices up".
   - Suggested fix: "That's costs pushing prices up... not spending." This uses the rule's own verb. Card: COSTS PUSHING PRICES UP / NOT SPENDING.
11. **minor, page 12:** "By the middle of twenty twenty-six, about a hundred and seventy thousand people were out of work."
   - Problem: This is the "who pays" page, where the voice brief wants the urgency, and it has no pause at all. The biggest number of people in the film gets no room to land. The voice brief asks for a pause after a number that needs to land.
   - Suggested fix: "By the middle of twenty twenty-six... about a hundred and seventy thousand people were out of work." This adds 1 pause (0.6 s) and no words.
12. **minor, page 11:** "Businesses sell less, and hire fewer people."
   - Problem: "Businesses sell less" is a run of five s and z sounds in three words. A synthetic voice may blur it.
   - Suggested fix: Listen for it in the take. If it blurs, use "Businesses sell less... and hire fewer people."
13. **minor, page 0:** "About 410 s at a strict 140 words a minute with page turns, holds and fade."
   - Problem: The script's length figure leaves out the 28 "..." pauses. Counted the way this test asks (140 words a minute plus 0.6 s for each pause), it comes to about 7:07 to 7:11, not 6:50: 878 to 886 words, 393 to 397 s of speech, plus 34 s of page turns, holds and fade. The pauses also make pages 1, 5, 11, 14 and 15 about 1.5 to 2.5 s longer than their listed seconds. The claim that the cost in jobs comes "within 30 seconds" holds only at the faster recorded pace: at 140 a minute, page 1's last line ends at about 34 s.
   - Suggested fix: Update the figures: about 7:10 at 140 words a minute, about 6:00 at the recording's 164. With all the fixes above, about 7:17 and 898 spoken words. Picture timing gets rebuilt from the recorded word times anyway.
