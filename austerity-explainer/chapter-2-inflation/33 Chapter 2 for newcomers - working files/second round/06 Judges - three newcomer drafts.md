# 06 Judges - three newcomer drafts of chapter 2

Source: Clara E. Mattei, *The Capital Order* (2022).

Three judges, each with one lens, scored the three drafts in "05 Three drafts" (first round) from 0 to 10.

## newcomers

Lens: will a newcomer follow it? I heard each draft once at speaking speed as three first-time viewers who haven't seen chapter 1: S, a 17-year-old student; B, a 60-year-old retired builder who left school at 16; and E, an adult whose first language isn't English. All three drafts are far easier to follow than the recording. None has digits, capitals, brackets, "MMT", banned Klein words or "I". Mattei is always marked as hers. The longest sentence in each is 19 words.

Ranking for "can a newcomer explain the idea and use it on a new case":
1. one-model, 7.5
2. viewer-question, 7
3. people-first, 5.5

one-model wins because it builds the two-factories picture first, says what it stands for ("think of each one as a whole country"), and then places every later case on it out loud. Japan "never reached the factory", the cure makes "the factory slow, more workers wait at the gate", fuel raises "prices in both factories", and Mattei's "those workers at the gate". That gives a viewer a tool they can carry to a new case. Its weak spots are page 10 (homes) and page 13 (prices now). people-first is the most human, but it is the heaviest to take in, it drops the costs idea and it plants "the cure makes prices fall".

Load per 30 seconds. I counted new places, years or dates, numbers and terms, first mention only, tagged by hand. The script turns each tag's word position into seconds at 140 words a minute, with 1.5 s page turns and 3 s guess holds.

Time order:
- one-model runs strictly forward.
- viewer-question runs forward, apart from a small step back to 2021 for the cure.
- people-first jumps back from New Zealand 2021 to Britain 1920, then returns. It says why, but it is still a zigzag.

Reader-comprehension probes (toolkit):
- **Main claim:** all three state the rule in plain words twice. viewer-question and people-first word it as conditions ("if there are spare workers, materials and machines..."). one-model words it with the factory names, which only works because it has mapped the factories to a country.
- **Evidence, inference and qualification:** these are easiest to hear in one-model. "Picture two factories" is followed by "This really happened", and it says "most economists agree", "one reason economists give", "a Royal Commission found... part of the reason", "economists argue" and "she argues".
- **Transfer:** no draft gives the viewer a clue and then asks them to apply the rule. viewer-question's "which factory was New Zealand? Have a guess" (page 10) comes closest, but the clue (3.2 percent unemployment) only comes after the guess. one-model and people-first apply the rule for the viewer.
- **Teaching plan's key question** (new money paying builders): one-model and viewer-question risk the answer "homes always push prices up", because homes are taught as something we can't make more of in a hurry. people-first sets this up best: homes are slow "not with the builders and materials gone to war".
- **Smaller goal**, telling a rise from spending apart from a rise in costs: people-first cut fuel entirely, so its viewers can't do this. viewer-question teaches costs but doesn't place them on the factories. one-model does both.

Not tested: the three listeners are imagined, as in the audit. No real newcomer has heard any draft and Malina hasn't read them, so the timings are estimates.

### one-model: strengths

Newcomer say-backs:
- S: "Think of a country as a factory: with workers waiting, new money gets more made; flat out, it pushes prices up. Money that isn't spent doesn't count. Fuel pushes prices up either way. The cure slows the factory, and Mattei says the jobless are the point."
- B: "Spare hands and machines, the money gets used making stuff; everyone flat out, prices go up."
- E: "When all the workers are busy, new money makes prices go up."

Picture and checks:
- Page 3 says what the picture stands for: "Think of each one as a whole country... all its workers, materials and machines."
- Page 4's guess is a real prediction the viewer can reason out from the picture: "The same new money is spent there. What do you think happens?"
- Page 5 is the only draft that hints at why prices rise: "Just more money, offered for the same things... so prices rise."
- Page 6's "This really happened" separates the imagined picture from real evidence.
- Page 7's Japan guess is a real surprise.

Every example's lesson is said aloud and placed on the picture:
- Britain (page 6): "close to the flat-out factory".
- Japan (page 8): "In our picture, it never reached the factory".
- New Zealand (page 9): "Very few workers were spare. That's close to the flat-out factory."
- The cure (page 11): "the factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate".
- Fuel (page 13): "prices rise in both factories... with or without new money".
- Mattei (page 14): "those workers at the gate".

Wording and order:
- Page 9 defines unemployment before giving the number.
- Page 11 says "home loans", not "mortgages".
- Page 12 makes clear the 170,000 is everyone out of work, not the rise: "Five point six percent is about a hundred and seventy thousand people."
- Page 15 repeats page 5's rule word for word.
- Time and place run forward only: Britain, Japan, New Zealand.
- It has the fewest new items overall: 39, about 2.9 every 30 seconds.

### one-model: faults

First places each listener gets lost:
- **B:** page 10. "House prices aren't in the shopping basket. But..." is a point about how prices are measured that he can't use. Then "A Royal Commission, an official inquiry, found the economic response to Covid was part of the reason. So were world events." has a definition squeezed mid-sentence, and the abstract "economic response" and "world events" follow it.
- **E:** also lost at page 10 ("inquiry", "response"). Before that, page 1's "Create new money... and prices will rise" reads as an order, and "central bank" is explained only by example.
- **All:** page 13 runs 37 seconds with four numbers in about 15 seconds (4.1, 2.9, one to three, fuel). "pushed the whole basket past that" has an unclear "that", and it ends on a question left hanging ("Can the usual cure fix a rise in costs? Economists argue about that.").

Terms left unexplained:
- "central bank": shown by example only.
- "inquiry" and "economic response to Covid".
- "the usual cure": promised on page 1, explained on page 11, about four minutes later.

Voice-brief breach: page 1 uses "cost" as a figure of speech ("can cost people their jobs").

Transfer risk: page 10's "You can't build a house in a hurry. So money spent on homes mostly pushes prices up... like the flat-out factory" invites "homes always push prices up". A viewer may then fail the builders question.

The rule is worded with the factory names rather than plain conditions, one step removed from a new case. No viewer application check: the narrator applies the picture to New Zealand.

### viewer-question: strengths

Newcomer say-backs:
- S: "New money doesn't always raise prices. With spare workers and machines it mostly gets more made; when everything's busy, prices go up. Japan's money mostly sat in banks. The fix, a higher interest rate, puts people out of work."
- B: "Printing money only drives prices up when there's nobody spare to do the work, like in the war."
- E: "Money makes prices go up when the factories are full."

Pace:
- Easiest to hear: 16 short pages and 7.6 words a sentence, about one new item every 30 seconds (3.1).
- Page 2 says plainly who can create new money (the Reserve Bank) and ends with a memory cue: "Hold on to that."
- Page 1's warning is the clearest for E: "If the government creates new money... prices will go up." Page 1 avoids "cost" as a figure of speech.

Tests and lessons:
- Pages 3 and 4: test one's lesson is said aloud and tied back to page 2: "So far, the warning seems right. But look closer. Britain was short of workers, materials and machines."
- Pages 5 and 6: test two is a guess most viewers will get wrong, then a plain verdict: "So no... new money doesn't always push prices up."
- Page 8 says Britain was "close to the flat-out factory".
- Page 10's test three is the only place in any draft where the viewer is asked to apply the picture to a new case: "A country can be either factory... which factory was New Zealand? Have a guess."

Wording:
- Page 11 gives unemployment example first: "Only about three in every hundred people who wanted a job couldn't find one. That's called unemployment... three point two percent."
- Page 13: "Interest is the extra you pay back when you borrow" and "Not fall... rise more slowly."

### viewer-question: faults

First places each listener gets lost:
- **S:** page 7 opens "So what made the difference?", but page 6 has just answered that (the money sat in the banks). The quiet factory ("more gets made... and prices hardly move") then follows Japan's flat prices, which invites "Japan was the quiet factory". The ledger doesn't support that, and Japan is never placed on the picture.
- **B:** page 6's "Mainstream economists", again on page 9. Then page 12: "If petrol and diesel prices hadn't changed... they'd have risen two point nine... So the fuel part isn't about spending. It's a cost." That is a hard grammatical form for B and E, with no 1 to 3 percent target to tell him whether 2.9 is good or bad.
- **E:** page 5's "Its version of our Reserve Bank" ("its" after a sentence fragment), page 7's "new orders" (orders can mean commands), and page 11's "an independent review said the support... too strong, for too long".

Terms left unexplained:
- "mainstream", twice.
- "independent review".
- "orders", which E may hear as commands.
- "the usual cure": promised on page 1, explained on page 13, about four and a half minutes later.

Mapping and wording problems:
- The factory is said to stand for a country only on page 10.
- Fuel isn't placed on the factories.
- Page 11: "The flat-out one" drops the "close to" used everywhere else.
- No price outcome is given for New Zealand 2021.
- Test three's guess comes before its clue, so it is a guess, not an application.
- Page 14: after "climbed from three point two percent to five point six", "That's a hundred and seventy-one thousand people" can be heard as the rise.
- Page 15: "The people who set interest rates" is a second name for the Reserve Bank.
- Page 9's homes line carries the same "homes always push prices up" risk as one-model.

### people-first: strengths

Newcomer say-backs:
- S: "New money mostly makes more stuff when there are spare workers and machines, and pushes prices up when there aren't. Japan's money sat in banks. Raising interest rates in 1920 put more than one in ten out of work."
- B: "In the war nobody was spare, so prices doubled and landlords put the rents up." B relates to the Glasgow homes and families.
- E: keeps the Glasgow story and the rule's first half.

Wording that helps newcomers:
- Page 1 names the cure straight away, so nothing is left hanging: "the usual cure is to make borrowing cost more. And that can put people out of work."
- Page 2 says what the Reserve Bank is: "It's the bank in charge of the country's money."
- It keeps one name throughout: "out of work", never "unemployment". Each foreign bank is "[country]'s version of our Reserve Bank".
- Page 9: "two outside experts reviewed what the Reserve Bank did" is the plainest way any draft gives the review.

Pictures and lessons:
- Page 4 ties the shortage of homes to busy builders: "You can't build homes in a hurry... not with the builders and materials gone to war." That is the right setup for the builders question.
- Page 6 states the rule as plain conditions and repeats it word for word on page 14.
- Page 8's recap says "whether it's spent... and on what".

The most human, in Klein's spirit: real people (Mary Barbour, twenty thousand families) and who gains and who pays side by side (landlords and tenants).

Qualifications are said aloud: "Historians still argue over how much the interest rate caused" and "Not all of that is because of the interest rate."

### people-first: faults

First places each listener gets lost:
- **E:** page 3. "Glasgow, in Scotland... nineteen fifteen. Britain is fighting the First World War" gives Glasgow, Scotland, Britain, 1915 and the war in about 10 seconds; E may not know Scotland is in Britain. Then "shells", which E may hear as sea shells, and page 4's "refuse to pay any more", which E may hear as "stop paying anything".
- **B:** page 11. "Not about two in every hundred... but more than one in ten" mixes "in a hundred" and "in ten". Then page 13 repeats the recording's worst habit: two pairs of decimals almost back to back ("from a quarter of a percent... to five and a half percent... from three point two to five point six").

Teaching faults:
- Page 11's "Prices do fall" plants the very misconception the teaching plan said to avoid, and no line says "rise more slowly, not fall" for New Zealand. B leaves thinking the cure makes prices fall.
- Fuel and costs were cut, so its viewers can't tell a rise from spending apart from a rise in costs. That is one of the plan's stated goals.
- Page 4's strike story says its lesson only later, as "And so were Glasgow's homes" on page 6.

Mapping:
- It never says the factory stands for a whole country.
- Japan isn't placed on the picture.
- The cure isn't placed on the picture.

Unexplained or figurative terms:
- "shells".
- "a cost, not the goal": a figurative cost, in a chapter where cost means prices.
- "part of the point": an idiom.
- "wage subsidy", for non-New Zealand ears.

Other:
- The densest draft: 50 new items. The opening minute's worst stretch has 8, and 10 at Malina's real pace.
- Page 13: "That's about a hundred and seventy thousand people" can be heard as the rise.

### Best parts to keep

Base: one-model's teaching spine, pages 3 to 15. It is the picture a newcomer can reuse. Graft these in, in order.

Opening:
- people-first page 1: "the usual cure is to make borrowing cost more. And that can put people out of work." It names the cure at once and avoids one-model's figurative "cost people their jobs".
- viewer-question page 1: "If the government creates new money... prices will go up." Clearer for E than "Create new money... and prices will rise".
- people-first page 2: "It's the bank in charge of the country's money." One plain line on what the Reserve Bank is. With it, one-model's "a country's central bank" can go.

The picture and the rule:
- one-model page 3: "Think of each one as a whole country... all its workers, materials and machines."
- one-model page 4: the guess "The same new money is spent there. What do you think happens?" with its 3-second hold.
- one-model page 5: "Just more money, offered for the same things... so prices rise. Most economists agree on this much." Plain "most", not "mainstream".
- viewer-question page 9 or people-first page 6: the rule worded as conditions, "If there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more made. If there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up". Easier to carry to a new case than the factory names alone. Repeat it word for word at the end.

Britain and Japan:
- one-model page 6: "This really happened."
- viewer-question page 4: "So far, the warning seems right. But look closer. Britain was short of workers, materials and machines." It goes with viewer-question page 2's "Hold on to that."
- viewer-question pages 5 and 6: "If the warning is right, prices should rise fast. What happened? Have a guess." and "So no... new money doesn't always push prices up."
- one-model page 8: "In our picture, it never reached the factory. So it isn't just how much new money is created. It's whether it's spent... and what it's spent on."

New Zealand:
- viewer-question page 11: "Only about three in every hundred people who wanted a job couldn't find one. That's called unemployment... three point two percent."
- viewer-question page 10: "A country can be either factory... which factory was New Zealand? Have a guess." Keep it as a real application check, but move the clue (very few workers spare) before the question.
- people-first page 9: "two outside experts reviewed what the Reserve Bank did". Use it if the review line comes back.
- people-first page 4's logic for homes: "You can't build homes in a hurry... not with the builders and materials gone to war." Tie the shortage to busy builders, not to homes as such.

The cure and who pays:
- viewer-question page 13: "Interest is the extra you pay back when you borrow." and "And prices rise more slowly. Not fall... rise more slowly."
- one-model page 11: "In our picture, the factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate."
- one-model page 12: "Five point six percent is about a hundred and seventy thousand people, looking for work." It is clearly everyone out of work, not the rise.
- one-model page 13: "When fuel costs more, prices rise in both factories... with or without new money."
- one-model page 14: "She argues those workers at the gate aren't a side effect. They're how the cure works."

Closing: one-model page 15 or viewer-question page 16, "Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?"

### Must fix

For the draft taken forward (one-model as base):

1. **Page 13, prices now: 37 seconds, the heaviest page.** It has four numbers in about 15 seconds, an unclear "past that", and a question left hanging. Split it into two pages, or drop the one to three percent target together with "Can the usual cure fix a rise in costs? Economists argue about that." If that question goes, page 12's "And this year, the Reserve Bank has raised its interest rate again" must go with it. Its own notes pair them, because of the ledger's "never mostly fuel" warning (C2-M18). Keep the 4.1 and 2.9 tied to the year to June twenty twenty-six. Keep Stats NZ's own wording.

2. **Page 10, homes: lightening and transfer.**
   - Take out the definition squeezed mid-sentence ("A Royal Commission, an official inquiry, found..."). Either give it its own sentence or cut the Royal Commission line.
   - Say "house prices aren't in the shopping basket" in a way B can use, or move it to the screen only. The ledger trap still applies: never put house prices next to the inflation figure.
   - Tie "can't build a house in a hurry" to busy builders and materials, as in people-first page 4. Otherwise viewers learn "homes always push prices up" and fail the builders question. Keep "part of the reason" and the world events (C2-M10, C2-N14) if the line stays.

3. **Page 1.** Name the cure at once ("make borrowing cost more"), and replace "can cost people their jobs" with "can put people out of work". The voice brief bans "cost" as a figure of speech. Use viewer-question's "If the government creates new money... prices will go up" in place of the imperative.

4. **Add one real application check.** Give the clue before the guess (for example, "Very few workers were spare" or the three-in-a-hundred line), then ask "which factory was New Zealand?", as in viewer-question page 10. Use "close to the flat-out factory" in the answer, never a flat "the flat-out one".

5. **Two names for one thing.** Use one plain wording for the bank's role, either "central bank" or the Reserve Bank explained in plain words, not both as separate terms. Never "mainstream", never "the people who set interest rates" as a second name.

If viewer-question is chosen instead:
- Place Japan on the picture ("it never reached the factory").
- Cut "So what made the difference?" after Japan's reason has been given, or move the two factories before Japan.
- Say the factory stands for a whole country on page 7.
- "Mainstream" becomes "most".
- Explain the review ("two outside experts").
- "The flat-out one" becomes "Close to the flat-out one".
- Make the 171,000 clearly the total.
- Rewrite the fuel line for the ear, avoiding "would have... if... hadn't".

If people-first is chosen instead:
- Fix "Prices do fall" with "not fall... rise more slowly" for New Zealand.
- Restore a short costs line from its ready insert.
- Split page 13's back-to-back decimal pairs.
- Thin page 3's place cluster.
- Replace "a cost, not the goal" and "part of the point".

Before recording, whichever draft is chosen: read it aloud to one or two of the people who got lost and ask the builders question. "Prices rose 4.1, and 2.9 without fuel: was that new money?" is the second test. No real newcomer has heard any draft yet.

## voice-and-sound

I read all three narrations aloud the way Malina would. Lens: voice and sound. All three pass the project's voice checker: no capitals, digits, brackets, exclamation marks or Naomi Klein phrases, Mattei is written /məˈteɪi/ and marked "she argues / she says", and no sentence runs over 19 words. All three put the stakes in the first half-minute and end on the same strong pair: "Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?" They differ in how alive they are and where they trip the tongue.

People-first (7.5) is the most alive and the closest to Klein in spirit. It has a real place and real people (Glasgow 1915, landlords raising rents, twenty thousand families refusing, Mary Barbour, Parliament bringing rents back down). It puts who gains and who pays side by side, ends a story on people winning something, and has the fewest stock bridges and "So" openers (6). Its sound problems sit on two pages: page 13 (seven spoken figures in four sentences, two kinds of percent back to back, and a jump forward to 2023 then back to 2021) and page 11 ("Not about two in every hundred... but more than one in ten"). It also keeps one figurative "cost".

One-model (7) has the best single device in any draft: "workers wait at the gate" comes back as a refrain in the quiet factory, the usual cure and Mattei's argument, which makes who pays something you can see. It falls down on page 1 ("The answer matters", plus a figurative "cost people their jobs") and page 13 (86 words, four figures, an apposition the voice may not mark, ending on "Economists argue about that"). "So were world events" is a limp landing.

Viewer-question (6.5) is the easiest on the tongue, with the shortest sentences (7.6 words on average) and three real guess holds. But it sounds most like a worksheet: eleven sentences start with "So", there are seven canned bridges ("So here's the question", "Hold on to that", "But look closer", "Here's the answer", "Back to the question"), there is no named person or lived scene, and it shows who pays but never who gains.

My recommendation for voice: use people-first as the base. Graft in one-model's "workers at the gate" refrain on the cure and Mattei pages. Replace people-first's page 13 with viewer-question's simpler New Zealand cure: no interest-rate figures, "again and again".

Not tested: Malina hasn't read any draft, and no listener has heard one. My read-aloud is simulated. Timings are the drafters' estimates plus the checker's 140 words a minute. ### people-first: strengths

The most urgent and alive draft, in Klein's spirit without her words. It is built from scenes you could film: page 3 is told in the present tense ('Britain is fighting the First World War. Crowds of workers come to make shells for the war'), and page 4 is the best-sounding page in any draft. On page 4 landlords raise the rent 'by up to a quarter', 'The families who rent say no', Mary Barbour leads them, 'about twenty thousand families are refusing', and Parliament brings rents back to before the war. That gives who gains and who pays side by side, plus people winning something, all sourced (C2-XB01, XB02). Page 11 gives the cure step by step, then 'Prices do fall' and a fair 'Historians still argue over how much the interest rate caused.' Mattei is marked twice. It avoids the rise/raise clash by having prices go on 'climbing' (page 10). It has the fewest 'So' openers (6) and the fewest stock bridges. It never shouts or preaches: the force comes from putting facts side by side.

### people-first: faults

Page 13 is the hardest page to hear in all three drafts. It has seven spoken figures in four sentences ('a quarter of a percent... five and a half percent by twenty twenty-three. Since late twenty twenty-one, the percentage out of work has climbed from three point two to five point six'). Those are two different percentages back to back, the time jumps forward to 2023 and back to 2021, 'the percentage out of work' is a clunky noun phrase, and 'percent' is dropped after the numbers. Then 'That's about a hundred and seventy thousand people' comes straight after the climb, so it can be heard as the size of the rise (the voice brief's trap; it is the total). Page 11: 'Not about two in every hundred... but more than one in ten' sounds like a correction, not a change over time, and the two fractions count out of different totals (a hundred, ten), so the ear can't compare them. Page 12: 'call lost jobs a cost, not the goal' uses 'cost' as a figure of speech, which the brief forbids and which the drafter's notes say was removed. Also, the officials' 'not the goal' has no ledger wording. Page 8: 'So it's not how much new money there is' drops the 'just' the other drafts keep, so it says more than the 'mostly' in C2-XA03 and C2-M09 allows. Page 6: 'Nothing more can be made' is absolute where the chapter's rule says 'mostly'. Page 9 crams Covid, billions, the wage subsidy, three point two, record low, the factory and the review into 80 words (34 seconds). 'New money mostly gets more made' runs four m-sounds together, and 'more made' leaves out what is made.

### one-model: strengths

The strongest refrain in any draft. 'Workers wait at the gate' (page 3) comes back as 'the factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate' (page 11). On page 14 it becomes 'She argues those workers at the gate aren't a side effect. They're how the cure works.' That makes who pays concrete and keeps Mattei's view clearly hers. It is Klein's move of setting the official reason beside what the policy did, done through a picture rather than a slogan. Good ear lines: 'Just more money, offered for the same things... so prices rise' (page 5) and 'In our picture, it never reached the factory' (page 8). It keeps 'close to the flat-out factory' each time. 'Five point six percent is about a hundred and seventy thousand people' (page 12) makes clear it is the total, not the rise. Its rhythm is calm on the teaching pages and leans in on the who-pays pages, as the brief asks.

### one-model: faults

Page 1: 'The answer matters' is the stock line the viewer-question drafter cut, and 'can cost people their jobs' uses 'cost' as a figure of speech, which the brief forbids. 'Prices will rise... rising prices' also echoes. Page 13 is the heaviest page in the chapter: 86 words, 37 seconds and four figures (four point one, two point nine, one to three percent). 'So one price tag, fuel, pushed the whole basket past that' is an apposition an AI voice may not mark (it can come out as 'one price tag fuel pushed'), and 'past that' asks the listener to do sums by ear. The page then ends on a question answered with 'Economists argue about that', an honest but deflating shrug. Page 10's 'the economic response to Covid' is an abstract noun phrase, and 'So were world events' is a vague, limp landing. Page 12 has three 'interest rate's in five sentences with no pause, which reads flat. On page 12, 'five point six. Five point six percent' back to back may sound like a re-take. 'This really happened' (page 6) is a stock bridge. Pages 11 and 1 put prices rising next to the bank raising. It shows who pays but never who gains, and it names no real person. At 886 words it is the longest.

### viewer-question: strengths

The easiest draft on the tongue: 7.6 words per sentence on average and about 27 spoken figures in all. Three real prediction holds ('Before the answer... have a guess'; 'Which factory was New Zealand? Have a guess') make the viewer an active listener. 'So far, the warning seems right' (page 4) states the other side fairly before turning. 'Test three. This one's ours' brings it home. 'And prices at the checkout lately?' (page 12) is a good callback to the opening. 'And prices rise more slowly. Not fall... rise more slowly' (page 13) is ideal ear-writing. 'Looking for work and not finding it' (page 14) turns the number into people. The New Zealand cure page drops the interest-rate figures ('again and again'), the cleanest of the three versions. It uses the independent review's own words. Its ending ('Does new money always push prices up? No.' then the rule and the two questions) lands.

### viewer-question: faults

It sounds more like a worksheet than like urgency. Eleven sentences open with 'So', which becomes a drumbeat on the ear, and there are many canned bridges: 'Getting this right matters', 'So here's the question', 'Hold on to that', 'But look closer', 'Here's the answer', 'Back to the question'.
