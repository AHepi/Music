# Script - chapter 2 - what really causes inflation - for newcomers (combined)

Source: Clara E. Mattei, *The Capital Order* (2022).

**What this is:** the final newcomer script for chapter 2, made from the three drafts and the three judges'
notes. It is built on the one-model draft, which the judges rated best for newcomers. Lines are taken
from the other two drafts, and every must-fix is applied. Every fact traces to an id in
`chapter-2-inflation/18 Research - chapter 2 - what really causes inflation.md`. The narration on its
own, ready for the voice checker, is in `narration-final.txt` in this folder.

**Untested:** no newcomer has heard it, and Malina hasn't read it.

## How it teaches

**The sentence a viewer should be able to say back:** whether new money pushes prices up depends on
what it's spent on. When there are spare workers, materials and machines, it mostly gets more things
made. When there aren't, it mostly pushes prices up. And the usual cure is paid for by people with
loans and people out of work.

**The model.** One picture is built before any real case: two factories, each standing for a whole
country, "all its workers, materials and machines".
- The quiet factory has machines standing still and workers waiting at the gate.
- The flat-out factory has every worker and machine already busy.
- New money is "spent on what it makes", so money that isn't spent never reaches the factory.

Every case after that is placed on the picture out loud:
- Britain at war: "close to the flat-out factory".
- Glasgow's homes: "worked like the flat-out factory too", because builders and materials had gone to
  the war.
- Japan: "most of it never reached the factory".
- New Zealand 2020-21: "close to the flat-out factory".
- The usual cure: "the factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate".
- Fuel: "prices go up in both factories... new money or not".
- Mattei's argument: "with more people at the gate".

**Checks for the viewer:**
- Three guesses, each at the end of a page so the 3-second hold is real silence: the flat-out factory,
  Japan, and New Zealand.
- The New Zealand guess is an application check. The clue comes first ("almost everyone who wanted a job
  had one... about three in a hundred were out of work"), then "Which factory was New Zealand closer to?"

**Recaps:**
- The rule is said in the same words on page 5 and page 15.
- "It's whether it's spent... and on what" follows Japan.
- The two closing questions repeat the chapter's refrain.

**Naomi Klein in spirit, never her words:**
- the stakes come within 30 seconds;
- the viewer's own questions are asked and answered;
- who gains and who pays sit side by side (landlords raised the rent; families refused and won);
- numbers are said as people ("about twenty thousand families", "about a hundred and seventy thousand
  people");
- the banks' view gets one fair line, then Mattei's, marked as hers twice;
- the urgency comes from facts, never from volume.

**The Pinker toolkit's principles, never his voice:**
- a concrete scene before each idea;
- the imagined picture announced as imagined ("Picture two factories"), then "Now a real case";
- what each example shows, said aloud;
- where the picture stops working, covered (unspent money, costs, "mostly", "close to");
- qualifications kept.

**Cut or moved to later chapters** (their facts and pictures stay ready):
- Bank of England after 2008;
- Germany 1923 (the Ruhr, marks);
- oil in 1973;
- Britain 1920 (7%, 2% to 11%, "prices do fall");
- New Zealand house prices (+30%) and the Royal Commission;
- the independent review's "too strong, for too long", cut for length (it is the first line to bring
  back);
- the interest rate going from a quarter of a percent to five and a half;
- the 3.2% to 5.6% pair;
- this year's rises in the interest rate;
- the 1-3% target.

### Page 1 - The question (about 33 s)

You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. You've heard the warning too. If the government creates new money... prices will go up. Is that always true? It matters... because when prices go up too fast, the usual cure makes borrowing cost more. And that can put people out of work.

- **Research:** C2-XA04, C2-M01, C2-XA05 (the basket costs more than a year earlier), C2-M14 (the
  warning's idea, as the other side holds it), C2-M17 and C2-XD01 (the usual cure: borrowing costs more,
  people spend less, businesses hire less; "can").
- **Terms explained:**
  - inflation ("prices in general, going up... not one price tag, the whole basket");
  - the usual cure (it "makes borrowing cost more"; explained in full on page 11).
- **Pictures:** page-1 reused with its two parts swapped (code only).

  - Remove the brass-shell bridge to the old shell-factory page.

### Page 4 - The flat-out factory, the first guess (about 20 s, then a 3 s hold)

### Page 5 - The rule (about 23 s)

No spare workers, materials or machines... so no more things get made. Just more money, offered for the same things... and prices go up. Most economists agree on this much. So when there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money mostly gets more things made. When there aren't... it mostly pushes prices up.

- **Research:** C2-XA03, C2-M05 ("most economists agree": a textbook idea accepted across schools),
  C2-M09 ("mostly").
- **Terms explained:**
  - spare;
  - the rule, which is recap 1 and is repeated word for word on page 15.
- **Pictures** (code only):
  - Works B's counter flips up. The three cut-outs return stamped NO SPARE, with MORE MONEY / SAME
    THINGS and a small MOST ECONOMISTS AGREE.
  - Then a wide shot of both works with two typed cards: SPARE → MOSTLY MORE THINGS MADE and NONE
    SPARE → MOSTLY PRICES UP.
  - This reuses the end of page-8 part A, with YES, IT CAN and the card house removed.

- **Terms explained:** out of work (plain words, shown as one in a hundred); the First World War is

### Page 7 - Glasgow's homes (about 32 s)

Homes worked like the flat-out factory too. Early in the war, crowds of war workers came to Glasgow, a British city. With builders and materials gone to the war, few new homes were built. So landlords raised the rent... in places by up to a quarter. By November, about twenty thousand families were refusing to pay the extra. Within weeks, Britain's Parliament brought rents on small homes back to what they were before the war.

- **Research:**

  - C2-H04;
  - C2-XB02 (small homes held at pre-war rents, about five weeks after the November peak, so "within
    weeks" follows the November line);
  - the second fact check of 12 Oct 2026 (house building "virtually ceased" with the war; labour and
    materials went to the war).
- **Terms explained:**
  - homes as something that can't be built quickly while builders and materials are busy elsewhere;

- **Pictures:** page-4's painted Glasgow tenement street (code only).

### Page 9 - Japan, the answer (about 26 s)

Prices barely moved. For about eight years, they went up about half a percent a year, on average. Why? One reason economists give... most of the new money just sat in the banks. In our picture, most of it never reached the factory. So it's not just how much new money there is. It's whether it's spent... and on what.

- **Research:**
  - C2-XB04 and C2-C06 (about half a percent a year on average, 2013-2021);
  - C2-C06 and C2-XB03 (most of the new money sat in the banks; it is one of several reasons, and
    monetarists say prices would have gone up even less without it, hence "one reason" and "most").
- **Terms explained:** money that is created but not spent ("sat in the banks... never reached the
  factory"); recap 2, "whether it's spent... and on what".
- **Pictures** (code only):

In our picture, the factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate.

## How each must-fix was handled

**Newcomers judge**
1. **Prices now was too heavy.** Page 14 has two figures, tied to "the year to June twenty twenty-six"
   in Stats NZ's sense. The 1-3% target is gone, and so is "Economists argue about that". The 2026 rate
   rise line went with it, as the pair rule asks (C2-M18).
2. **The homes page.** The New Zealand house-price page is gone, so there's no "not in the basket"
   point to use and no squeezed definition of the Royal Commission. Homes are taught through Glasgow,
   tied to "builders and materials gone to the war". That points a viewer at whether builders are spare,
   which is what the builders question tests.
3. **Page 1.** It names the cure at once ("makes borrowing cost more") and says "can put people out of
   work". The warning is a plain condition, not an order.
4. **An application check.** Page 10 gives the clue, then asks "Which factory was New Zealand closer
   to?" The answer is "Close to the flat-out factory."
5. **Two names for one thing.** "Central bank", "mainstream" and "the people who set interest rates"
   are never used.

**Voice and sound judge**
1. **No "cost" as a figure of speech.** The officials' line is now C2-M17's own description
   ("unfortunate... but not the goal"). The unsourced "unwanted side effect" is gone.
2. **The 170,000 is heard as a total.** It's said as a level ("were out of work") with its date, and no
   from-to pair sits beside it.
3. **Number-heavy pages thinned.** There are no interest-rate figures, and the costs page has two
   figures.
