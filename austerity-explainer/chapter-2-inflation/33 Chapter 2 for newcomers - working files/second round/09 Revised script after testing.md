# Script - chapter 2 - what really causes inflation - for newcomers (revised after testing)

Source: Clara E. Mattei, *The Capital Order* (2022).

**What this is:** the newcomer script for chapter 2 after three fresh testers checked the combined
version (a simulated first-time listener panel, a fact checker and a rules-and-voice reader). Each
of their findings was checked against the ledger and the script before anything changed. Every
confirmed must-fix and should-fix is applied, and so are the cheap, safe minor ones. Nothing else
changed. Every fact traces to an id in
`chapter-2-inflation/18 Research - chapter 2 - what really causes inflation.md`. The narration on
its own, ready for the voice checker, is `narration-revised.txt` in this folder.

**Untested:** no real newcomer has heard it, Malina hasn't read it, and no second fact checker has
read this revised version.

## How it teaches

**The sentence a viewer should be able to say back:** whether new money pushes prices up depends on
whether it's spent, and on what. When there are spare workers, materials and machines to make what it
buys, it mostly gets more things made. When there aren't, it mostly pushes prices up. And the usual
cure is paid for by people with loans and people out of work.

**The model.** One picture comes before any real case: two factories, each standing for "a whole
country... all its workers, materials and machines".
- The quiet factory: machines still, workers waiting at the gate.
- The flat-out factory: every worker and machine "working as hard as they can".
- New money is "spent on what the factory makes". Money that is never spent never reaches the factory.
- The rule, said the same way on pages 5 and 16: "When there are spare workers, materials and
  machines... new money, once it's spent, mostly gets more things made. When there aren't any spare...
  it mostly pushes prices up."

**The cases, each placed on the picture out loud:**
- Britain at war: "close to the flat-out factory" (millions in uniform, ships sunk, partly new money).
- Glasgow: "for homes, Glasgow was like the flat-out factory". This is the first "on what" case: one
  kind of thing with nobody spare to make more. Landlords and families sit side by side.
- Japan: "most of it never reached the factory", then the recap "whether it's spent... and on what.
  Are there spare workers, materials and machines to make what it buys?"
- New Zealand 2020-21, the application check: the clue (about three in a hundred out of work), the
  guess, then the answer page: "Close to the flat-out factory", prices went up fast "partly from that,
  and partly from world prices, like oil", and the biggest push came from homes, the "on what" case
  at home.
- The usual cure: "the flat-out factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate".
- Who pays: loans, and "nearly six in a hundred" out of work by the middle of 2026, against "about
  three in a hundred" on page 10. "The interest rate isn't the only reason for that."
- Two views that now clash out loud: the banks call putting people out of work "unfortunate... but
  not the goal"; Mattei "argues that putting people out of work is the main goal".
- Fuel at the checkout: almost everything made or moved costs more "in both factories", so "part of
  what you felt at the checkout wasn't new money being spent".

**Checks for the viewer:** three guesses, each at a page end with a 4-second hold: the flat-out
factory, Japan, New Zealand.

**Recaps:** the rule twice in the same words (pages 5 and 16); "whether it's spent... and on what"
after Japan; the closing pair "Spent on what? And who pays for the cure?", which repeats page 13's
question word for word.

**Naomi Klein in spirit, never her words:** stakes in the first 30 seconds; the viewer's own questions
asked and answered; who gains and who pays side by side; people counted as people; the banks get one
fair line, then Mattei's, marked as hers; urgency from facts, never volume.

**The Pinker toolkit's principles, never his voice:** a concrete scene before each idea; the imagined
picture announced as imagined, then "Now a real case"; what each example shows, said aloud; where the
picture stops working, covered (unspent money, one kind of thing, costs, "mostly", "close to");
qualifications kept; content repaired first, then presentation.

**Still cut or moved to later chapters:** Bank of England after 2008; Germany 1923; oil in 1973;
Britain 1920; New Zealand house prices and the Royal Commission; the 0.25% to 5.5% figures and the
2026 rate rises; the 1-3% target; the independent review's "too strong, for too long" (see the
rejected findings).

## The script

### Page 1 - The question (about 34 s)

You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. You've heard the warning too. If the government creates new money... prices will go up. Is that always true? It matters... because when prices go up too fast, the usual cure makes loans cost more. And that can put people out of work.

- **Research:** C2-XA04, C2-M01, C2-XA05, C2-M14 (the warning's idea), C2-M17 and C2-XD01 (the usual
  cure: loans cost more, people spend less, businesses hire less; "can").
- **Terms explained:** inflation ("prices in general, going up... the whole basket"); the usual cure (it
  "makes loans cost more"; in full on page 12).
- **Pictures:** as in the combined version (page-1 reused, parts swapped, code only), with the chamber
  card BORROWING COSTS MORE changed to LOANS COST MORE → OUT OF WORK.

### Page 2 - Who can create new money (about 23 s)

Who can create new money? Here in New Zealand, the Reserve Bank can. It also tries to stop prices going up too fast. Economists who study how modern money works argue that a government like ours can't run out of its own money. But it can run out of workers, materials and machines.

- **Research:** C2-M15 ("can": most money is made by ordinary banks lending; New Zealand began
  targeting inflation around 1990), C2-N04 and C2-M04 (the Reserve Bank's 1-3% target, behind "tries
  to stop prices going up too fast"), C2-N06 (the Reserve Bank created new money), C2-M12 (the view of
  economists who study how modern money works, said as theirs: "A government that issues its own money
  can't run out of it"; it applies to countries that borrow in their own money, as New Zealand does).
- **Terms explained:** who can create new money; the Reserve Bank and what it tries to do (so it is
  known before page 8 and page 12); the economists' view, marked "argue"; workers, materials and
  machines as the real limit.
- **Pictures** (code only): page-10's New Zealand street at dusk with the typed card THE RESERVE BANK
  (no logo) and a small CAN CREATE NEW MONEY; then TRIES TO STOP PRICES GOING UP TOO FAST; page-2's
  banknote bundles land: NEW MONEY; the card CAN'T RUN OUT OF ITS OWN MONEY / CAN RUN OUT OF..., then
  page-3's three cut-outs pop up: WORKERS / MATERIALS / MACHINES.

### Page 3 - The quiet factory (about 28 s)

Picture two factories. Think of each one as a whole country... all its workers, materials and machines. In the first, the machines stand still. Workers wait at the gate, hoping for a job. Call it the quiet factory. Now new money is spent on what the factory makes. The workers go in. The machines start. More things get made... and prices barely move.

- **Research:** C2-XA03, C2-M09.
- **Terms explained:** the two-factories picture and what it stands for; the quiet factory; new money
  "spent on what the factory makes".
- **Pictures:** unchanged (page-3's works A; A WHOLE COUNTRY: WORKERS, MATERIALS, MACHINES; THE QUIET
  FACTORY; coins rain in: NEW MONEY SPENT; the price counter stays put).

### Page 4 - The flat-out factory, the first guess (about 22 s, then a 4 s hold)

In the second factory, every worker and every machine is already busy... working as hard as they can. No one waits at the gate. Call it the flat-out factory. The same new money is spent on what this factory makes. What do you think happens? Have a guess.

- **Research:** C2-XA03, C2-M05.
- **Terms explained:** flat out; the flat-out factory.
- **Pictures:** page-3's works B, gate empty, labelled THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY with a small line under it:
  FLAT OUT = WORKING AS HARD AS THEY CAN. Coins rain in; the counter freezes. WHAT HAPPENS? held about
  4 seconds, with a small on-screen marker that the film is waiting for a guess.

### Page 5 - The rule (about 28 s)

No spare workers, materials or machines... so no more things get made. Just more money, offered for the same things... and prices go up. Most economists agree on this much. So when there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money, once it's spent, mostly gets more things made. When there aren't any spare... it mostly pushes prices up.

- **Research:** C2-XA03 (about extra spending, hence "once it's spent"), C2-M05 ("most economists
  agree"), C2-M09 ("mostly").
- **Terms explained:** spare; the rule (recap 1, repeated on page 16).
- **Pictures:** as before, with the two rule cards reading SPARE → NEW MONEY, ONCE SPENT: MOSTLY MORE
  THINGS MADE and NONE SPARE → MOSTLY PRICES UP.

### Page 6 - Britain at war (about 31 s)

Now a real case. Britain, during the First World War. Millions were in uniform. Soon, fewer than one worker in a hundred was out of work. German submarines sank ships carrying food and materials. And the war was paid for partly with new money. More money... and fewer things to buy. By the end of the war, prices had roughly doubled. Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory.

- **Research:** C2-H02 (millions in uniform; out of work "fell below one in a hundred", 1915-1918
  annual figures; "soon" because early-1915 months were just above one percent, per the second fact
  check of 12 Oct 2026), C2-H03 and C2-XA02 (submarines), C2-XA01 ("partly"), C2-H01 ("roughly
  doubled"), second fact check (close to the second factory).
- **Terms explained:** out of work (one worker in a hundred); why workers weren't spare (millions in
  uniform).
- **Pictures:** page-2's munitions hall, words matched (code only): BRITAIN, FIRST WORLD WAR; a typed
  card MILLIONS IN UNIFORM; the 100 dots, last nearly empty: FEWER THAN 1 IN 100 OUT OF WORK; SHIPS
  SUNK: FOOD, MATERIALS; PARTLY NEW MONEY; MORE MONEY / FEWER THINGS; bars 1914 ×1 and 1918 ABOUT ×2;
  quick cut to works B: CLOSE TO THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY.

### Page 7 - Glasgow's homes (about 36 s)

By nineteen fifteen, crowds of war workers had come to Glasgow, a British city, where homes were already short. With builders and materials gone to the war, few new homes were built. So for homes, Glasgow was like the flat-out factory. Landlords raised the rent... in places by up to a quarter. By November, about twenty thousand families were refusing to pay the extra. Within weeks, Britain's Parliament brought rents on small homes back to what they were before the war.

- **Research:** C2-XB01 (the influx of war workers "exacerbating an already acute... housing crisis";
  rises "up to 25 percent in some districts"; about 20,000 by November 1915; strikers refused the
  increase, not all rent), C2-H04 (1915), C2-XB02 (small homes held at pre-war rents about five weeks
  after the November peak), second fact check (influx in 1915 confirmed; house building "virtually
  ceased"; labour and materials went to the war; Glasgow building had slumped even before 1914).
- **Terms explained:** a factory standing for one kind of thing ("for homes"); Britain's Parliament.
- **Pictures:** page-4's Glasgow tenement street (code only), titled GLASGOW 1915 (the year is now
  spoken) with HOMES ALREADY SHORT; workers stream in; the building-site shot: BUILDERS AND MATERIALS
  GONE TO WAR / FEW NEW HOMES; a tag FOR HOMES: LIKE THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY; RENT RAISED / UP TO +25% IN
  PLACES; the counter to 20,000 FAMILIES REFUSING THE EXTRA; the stamp RENTS BACK TO BEFORE THE WAR.
  Never WON or RENTS FROZEN. No Mary Barbour card.

### Page 8 - Japan, the second guess (about 17 s, then a 4 s hold)

Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's version of our Reserve Bank set out to double the money it had created... in two years. If the warning is right, prices should go up fast. What do you think happened? Have a guess.

- **Research:** C2-XB03, C2-C06.
- **Terms explained:** Japan's version of our Reserve Bank (now meaningful, since page 2 said what ours
  does).
- **Pictures:** unchanged (page-5 part A, JAPAN 2013, ×2 MONEY / IN TWO YEARS, the dashed line IF THE
  WARNING IS RIGHT climbs under WHAT HAPPENED?), hold about 4 seconds.

### Page 9 - Japan, the answer (about 32 s)

Prices barely moved. For about eight years, they went up about half a percent a year, on average. Why? One reason economists give... most of the new money just sat in the banks. In our picture, most of it never reached the factory. So it's not just how much new money there is. It's whether it's spent... and on what. Are there spare workers, materials and machines to make what it buys?

- **Research:** C2-XB04 and C2-C06 (about half a percent a year, 2013-2021); C2-C06 and C2-XB03 (most
  of the new money sat in the banks; "one reason"); C2-XA03 and C2-M09 (the "on what" question: prices
  can go up where one kind of thing has nobody spare, before everything is busy).
- **Terms explained:** money created but not spent; recap 2, and what "on what" asks.
- **Pictures:** as before (the almost-level line ABOUT ½% A YEAR; coins piling up in front of the card
  bank: MOST SAT IN THE BANKS; NOT JUST HOW MUCH / WHETHER IT'S SPENT, AND ON WHAT), then one more
  typed card: SPARE WORKERS, MATERIALS, MACHINES TO MAKE WHAT IT BUYS?

### Page 10 - New Zealand, the third guess (about 29 s, then a 4 s hold)

Now, here. When Covid hit in twenty twenty, the Reserve Bank created new money. And the government spent more... including help with wages. By the end of twenty twenty-one, almost everyone looking for work had found it. Only about three in a hundred were out of work... the lowest in more than thirty years. Which factory was New Zealand closer to? Have a guess.

- **Research:** C2-N06 (new money from March 2020), C2-N14 (the government's Covid spending; its fund
  also covered wage subsidies for over a million workers), C2-N09 (3.2% in the December 2021 quarter,
  the lowest since the count began in 1986), second fact check (screen changed to LOWEST SINCE 1986
  because New Zealand had almost no unemployment in the 1950s-60s).
- **Terms explained:** out of work as a share ("about three in a hundred"); what part of the
  government's money went on (wages); "almost everyone looking for work had found it".
- **Pictures:** page-8 part B's New Zealand street (code only): NEW ZEALAND 2020; notes land: NEW
  MONEY; GOVERNMENT SPENT MORE / INCLUDING HELP WITH WAGES; 2021 with the 100-dot panel, 3 empty: ABOUT 3
  IN 100 OUT OF WORK / LOWEST IN 30+ YEARS, credited Stats NZ; wide shot of both factories: WHICH ONE?,
  held about 4 seconds.

### Page 11 - New Zealand, the answer (about 19 s) - new page

Close to the flat-out factory. Almost no one was waiting at the gate. And prices here went up fast... partly from that, and partly from world prices, like oil. The biggest push came from homes... building new ones, and rents.

- **Research:** C2-N09 (the clue behind "almost no one was waiting"), C2-N15 (the review found the
  economy overheated), C2-N01 (prices went up 7.3% in the year to June 2022, the fastest in 32 years,
  behind "went up fast"), C2-N16 (no published split between spending here, Reserve Bank money and
  world prices; evidence on both sides), C2-N02 and C2-N03 (petrol and diesel up sharply on world oil
  prices; imported prices up 8.2% against 6.6% for home-grown), C2-N02 (Stats NZ: the 7.3% was "driven
  mainly by construction of new homes and rents").
- **Terms explained:** none new. It says the outcome and gives both causes.
- **Pictures** (code only, no new paintings): quick cut to works B with the gate almost empty: CLOSE TO
  THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY. Then PRICES WENT UP FAST, splitting into two small cards: PARTLY: FEW SPARE and
  PARTLY: WORLD PRICES, LIKE OIL (page-7 part A's oil barrel, no counter). Then page-4's building-site
  shot relabelled NEW ZEALAND with BIGGEST PUSH: BUILDING NEW HOMES, AND RENTS. No house-price figures,
  no +30%, no review desk.

### Page 12 - The usual cure (about 30 s)

So what's the usual cure? The Reserve Bank raises its interest rate. Interest is the extra you pay back when you borrow. Many loans cost more... so people have less to spend. Businesses sell less, and hire fewer people. And prices go up more slowly. Not down... just more slowly. In our picture, the flat-out factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate.

- **Research:** C2-XD01 (loans cost more, people spend less, prices go up less quickly), C2-M17
  (businesses hire less).
- **Terms explained:** interest and interest rate; the usual cure in full; "prices go up more slowly.
  Not down".
- **Pictures:** as before, without the opening CLOSE TO THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY cut (now on page 11). Ends
  on works B: smoke thins, windows dim, the queue returns: MORE WORKERS AT THE GATE.

### Page 13 - Who pays, here (about 26 s)

Who pays for the cure? From late twenty twenty-one into twenty twenty-three, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again and again. Loans cost more, and jobs got harder to find. By the middle of twenty twenty-six... nearly six in a hundred were out of work. The interest rate isn't the only reason for that.

- **Research:** C2-N10 and C2-XD03 (rises from October 2021 to May 2023; count given as 10 or 12, so
  "again and again"), C2-XD01, C2-N11, C2-XD05 (5.6% in the June 2026 quarter, highest since 2015; it
  also climbed while the interest rate was being cut, so "isn't the only reason").
- **Terms explained:** none new. The share out of work is in the same unit as page 10.
- **Pictures:** the dial climbs in steps: LATE 2021 TO 2023: AGAIN AND AGAIN (no rate figures). The
  100-dot panel from page 10 returns with nearly 6 empty dots: NEARLY 6 IN 100 OUT OF WORK, Stats NZ,
  MIDDLE OF 2026, and a small NOT THE ONLY REASON. The ABOUT 170,000 card is removed.

### Page 14 - Two views (about 27 s)

Our Reserve Bank, and others like it, see putting people out of work as unfortunate... but not the goal. Clara E. /məˈteɪi/ is the economist whose book this series follows. She argues that putting people out of work is the main goal. With more workers waiting at the gate, she says, those inside have less power to ask for more pay.

- **Research:** C2-M17 (central banks frame job losses as unfortunate, not a goal; said without the
  figurative "cost"), C2-H18 (Mattei: "The main objective of interest rate hikes is to cool down the
  labor market"; "Fewer job openings and higher unemployment diminish the bargaining power of
  workers"), C1-01 (chapter 1 ledger: "Economist Clara E. Mattei"). C2-XD02 is background only (her
  view is contested by Glasner and Tomlinson, so it stays marked as hers).
- **Terms explained:** who Mattei is; the two views, now audibly opposed ("not the goal" against "the
  main goal").
- **Pictures:** page-9 part B (code only): OUR RESERVE BANK AND OTHERS LIKE IT: UNFORTUNATE, NOT THE GOAL
  (no quotation marks); her book with CLARA E. MATTEI, THE CAPITAL ORDER (2022); MATTEI ARGUES: THE MAIN
  GOAL; the queue grows and the iron weight drops on the pay packet: LESS POWER TO ASK FOR MORE PAY. No
  portrait.

### Page 15 - Back at the checkout (about 30 s)

And the checkout? In the year to June twenty twenty-six, prices here went up four point one percent. If petrol and diesel prices hadn't changed... prices would have gone up two point nine percent. When fuel like that costs more, almost everything that's made or moved costs more... in both factories. So part of what you felt at the checkout wasn't new money being spent.

- **Research:** C2-N05, C2-M04, C2-XA05 (4.1%); C2-XC05 and the second fact check (Stats NZ: "If petrol
  and diesel prices had not changed, the CPI would have risen 2.9 percent"); C2-M06 (when fuel costs
  more, almost everything made or moved costs more, whatever the government is spending); C2-M04 (fuel
  rise attributed to the Middle East war); Traps on C2-M18 (fuel is about 1.2 of the 4.1 points, hence
  "part", never "mostly fuel"). Nothing in the ledger explains the other 2.9, and the page doesn't try.
- **Terms explained:** a second cause of prices going up, in plain words (what it costs to make and move
  things), working in both factories.
- **Pictures:** a brief return to page-1's basket; page-7 part B's street with bars 4.1% and 2.9%,
  labelled IF PETROL AND DIESEL PRICES HADN'T CHANGED, credited STATS NZ, YEAR TO JUNE 2026; the
  two-factories wide shot, the oil barrel lands by each works and both counters flip up: BOTH
  FACTORIES; back to the basket with PART OF IT: NOT NEW MONEY SPENT.

### Page 16 - Spent on what? (about 25 s, then the 4 s fade)

Does new money always push prices up? No. When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money, once it's spent, mostly gets more things made. When there aren't any spare... it mostly pushes prices up. So next time you hear the warning... ask two questions. Spent on what? And who pays for the cure?

- **Research:** C2-XA03, C2-M09.
- **Terms explained:** none new. Recap 1 in the same words as page 5; recap 3 (the two questions, the
  second repeating page 13 word for word).
- **Pictures:** the two-factories wide shot with page 5's rule cards; page-10 part B's chamber with the
  invented politician (no party colours); SPENT ON WHAT? and WHO PAYS FOR THE CURE? land and hold; the
  4-second fade.

## Test findings: what was fixed, and what was rejected

Each finding was checked against the ledger and the script first. "Fixed" means the change is in the
script above.

### Must-fixes

1. **Fixed - the rule claimed new money itself gets things made (fact tester, pages 5 and 16).** C2-XA03
   is about extra spending, and the film's own Japan case had new money that got nothing made. Both
   pages now say "new money, once it's spent, mostly gets more things made".
2. **Fixed - "Spent on what?" was never taught (newcomer panel, page 16).** Glasgow is now marked as the
   first "on what" case ("for homes, Glasgow was like the flat-out factory"). Page 9's recap adds "Are
   there spare workers, materials and machines to make what it buys?" New Zealand's answer page ends on
   homes (C2-N02). The closing words stay short, because the meaning is now taught before them. I used
   "what it buys" rather than the tester's "to make it", because "make it" can be heard as "make money".
3. **Fixed - "the lowest since records began" dropped the 1986 limit (fact tester, page 10).** It now
   says "the lowest in more than thirty years", and the screen reads LOWEST IN 30+ YEARS. This avoids a
   new year figure.
