## newcomers

I heard the script once at about 140 words a minute. It has 965 spoken words, about seven and a half minutes counting the three guess holds and the fade. The work folder SCRATCH/script-rewrite/work-final-newcomers was created and left empty. As told, I opened only round3/viewer-version.md.

Overall, the newcomer version mostly works. All four viewers get the main claim, and all four reason the idle-builders case the right way. Where they get lost:
- the two closing questions;
- the jump from "a whole country" to "one city's homes";
- the vague page 11 line ("partly from that", "building new ones");
- a few idioms that trip the second-language viewer.

These rules held in the spoken text:
- no name for the money view;
- no Klein wording that I could spot;
- numbers written as said;
- no capitals for emphasis and no bracketed cues;
- /məˈteɪi/ is used;
- Mattei's view is said as hers.

THE 15-YEAR-OLD
- Main claim: "New money doesn't always push prices up. If people and machines are doing nothing, it gets more stuff made. If everyone's already busy, prices go up. And the fix for fast-rising prices puts people out of work."
- Terms: inflation, new money, spare, flat-out and interest are fine. The Reserve Bank is "the bank that makes money". They are unsure whether the Reserve Bank is the government. "Loans cost more" means nothing to them until page 12.
- First hesitation: page 1, "the usual cure makes loans cost more". The first real stumble is page 2, "a government like ours can't run out of its own money": is the Reserve Bank the government, and if it can't run out, why can't it pay for everything?
- Examples:
  - Britain is clear: no one spare, fewer things, prices doubled.
  - Glasgow: they follow the story but can't say where the new money comes in, and they notice it's one city, not "a whole country".
  - Japan is clear: the money wasn't spent.
  - New Zealand: they get the guess right but lose "partly from that".
- Case 1 (idle builders): quiet factory, so more homes and prices barely move. That's correct, but page 11's "biggest push came from homes... building new ones" makes them wobble.
- Case 2 (hospitals, builders all busy): flat-out, so prices go up. The direction is right, but they may say "so the hospitals don't get built at all".
- End: "Who pays?" yes: the people who lose their jobs. "Spent on what?" only partly: "what the money buys", and maybe not linked to spare workers.

THE 70-YEAR-OLD WHO LEFT SCHOOL AT 15
- Main claim: "Printing money only pushes prices up when everyone's already working. The way they fight rising prices costs workers their jobs, and this author says that's the point: it keeps wages down."
- Terms: all fine, except they don't believe "can't run out of its own money", which is given with no reason, and they can't say what "like ours" means.
- First hesitation: page 2, that same line.
- Examples:
  - Britain lands well.
  - Glasgow is the most memorable, but they notice it's "one city's homes", not a whole country.
  - Japan: they wonder why the money sat in the banks.
  - New Zealand: they lose "that" on page 11 and may take "building new ones" literally.
- Pages 13 and 15 seem to clash: "They raised rates again and again and prices still went up four point one percent, so the cure didn't work?"
- Case 1: correct.
- Case 2: correct, and they may add that builders and materials get dearer.
- End: "Spent on what?" yes, through Glasgow: money spent where there are no builders. "Who pays?" yes.

THE ADULT WHOSE FIRST LANGUAGE ISN'T ENGLISH

THE TWO CLOSING QUESTIONS
- "Who pays?" works for three of the four, but its answer is never said in one plain sentence.
- "Spent on what?" works reliably for only one or two of the four. Its meaning ("are there spare workers, materials and machines to make what it buys?") is said once, in the middle of page 9, and never tied to the question at the end.

1. **must-fix, page 16:** "So next time you hear the warning... ask two questions. Spent on what? And who pays for the cure?"
   - Problem: "Spent on what?" only works if the viewer remembers page 9's single line, "Are there spare workers, materials and machines to make what it buys?". At the end, page 16 repeats the general rule but never ties the question to it. The second-language viewer hears it literally ("what is the money used for?"). The busy parent can only repeat the words. Only one or two of the four can use it to reason about the hospitals case.
   - Suggested fix: Join the question to the test where it's asked: "Spent on what... and are there spare workers, materials and machines to make it?" On screen, add a small line under SPENT ON WHAT?: ANY SPARE TO MAKE IT?
2. **must-fix, page 11:** "The biggest push came from homes... building new ones, and rents."
   - Problem: By ear, and on the card BIGGEST PUSH: BUILDING NEW HOMES, AND RENTS over a building site, this can be taken as "building homes pushes prices up". The 70-year-old and the second-language viewer take it literally. That works against the right answer to the idle-builders case, and against the quiet-factory lesson.
   - Suggested fix: Say "the cost of building new ones, and rents", and change the card to COST OF BUILDING NEW HOMES, AND RENTS. Only if the ledger says builders were busy then, add "Builders were busy too" so the flat-out link is plain.
3. **should-fix, page 13:** "Who pays for the cure?"
   - Problem: The question is asked and followed by facts (loans cost more, jobs harder to find, nearly six in a hundred out of work), but the answer is never said in a plain sentence. "Pays" is used in a figurative sense, and the second-language viewer may hear it as "who pays money for the cure?". On page 16 it's also unclear why you'd ask about the cure when you hear a warning about prices.
   - Suggested fix: After "Loans cost more, and jobs got harder to find." add one plain line: "So the people who pay are people with loans... and people who lose their jobs." Keep the hedge "isn't the only reason". On page 16, make the step plain, e.g. "And if they call for the cure... who pays for it?"
4. **should-fix, page 2:** "Here in New Zealand, the Reserve Bank can."
   - Problem: The page 1 warning says "If the government creates new money". Page 2 says the Reserve Bank creates it, then says "a government like ours can't run out of its own money". Page 3 says new money "is spent" without saying who spends it. Page 10 lists "the Reserve Bank created new money" and "the government spent more" as two separate things. The teen and the second-language viewer ask: is the Reserve Bank the government? Both test cases say "the government pays", so viewers have to guess whether that counts as new money spent.
   - Suggested fix: On page 3, give the spending someone to do it, as a made-up example (no ledger fact needed): "Say the government spends new money on what the factory makes." Only if the ledger supports it, add one clause on page 2 saying how the Reserve Bank and the government are linked.
5. **should-fix, page 2:** "Economists who study how modern money works argue that a government like ours can't run out of its own money."
   - Problem: This is a 20-word sentence with a 9-word subject before the verb. "Like ours" and "its own money" are never explained. The 70-year-old, who has heard "the country can't afford it" all his life, doesn't believe a claim given with no reason. The second-language viewer can't tell whose money "its own" is.
   - Suggested fix: Split it and say what "like ours" means, as far as the ledger allows: "Some economists study how modern money works. They argue that a government with its own money, like New Zealand's dollar, can't run out of it."
6. **should-fix, page 3:** "More things get made... and prices barely move."
   - Problem: The film never says why prices barely move. The teen and the busy parent can't explain the quiet-factory half of the rule. Page 6 explains the other half neatly ("More money... and fewer things to buy"), but this half has no matching line.
   - Suggested fix: Use the same pattern as page 6: "More money... and more things to buy. So prices barely move."
7. **should-fix, page 5:** "Just more money, offered for the same things... and prices go up."
   - Problem: This is the core of the whole film, and it's abstract. "Offered" by whom? It never says who raises the price. The teen asks "but why do prices go up just because there's more money?"
   - Suggested fix: Make it concrete: "People have more money to spend... but there's nothing more to buy. So sellers can ask more... and prices go up."
8. **should-fix, page 7:** "So for homes, Glasgow was like the flat-out factory."
   - Problem: Page 3 said each factory is "a whole country". Now the factory stands for one city's homes, with no bridge. The 70-year-old and the busy parent notice the jump. Glasgow also never mentions new money or extra spending, so viewers can't say how it supports the rule. Both test cases (builders, hospitals) need this one-kind-of-thing reading.
   - Suggested fix: Add a bridge and a matching line before the tag: "The factory picture works for one kind of thing too. More people needing homes... and hardly any new ones. So for homes, Glasgow was like the flat-out factory."
9. **should-fix, page 7:** "where homes were already short"
   - Problem: "Short" meaning "not enough" is an idiom. The second-language viewer hears "short homes" and misses the starting point of the whole example. The card HOMES ALREADY SHORT repeats it.
   - Suggested fix: Say "where there already weren't enough homes", and change the card to NOT ENOUGH HOMES ALREADY.
10. **should-fix, page 6:** "Millions were in uniform."
   - Problem: "In uniform", meaning in the armed forces, is an idiom the second-language viewer may not know, and the card MILLIONS IN UNIFORM doesn't help. Viewers also aren't told why it matters: workers left their jobs.
   - Suggested fix: Say "Millions went off to fight." and change the card to MILLIONS WENT TO FIGHT. The next line (fewer than one in a hundred out of work) then makes the link.
11. **should-fix, page 11:** "partly from that, and partly from world prices, like oil."
   - Problem: "That" points vaguely back to "almost no one was waiting at the gate". By ear, the second-language viewer, the busy parent and the 70-year-old lose it. After Japan ("it's whether it's spent"), viewers also want to know whether money was being spent this time, but page 10 lists the Reserve Bank and government spending as two separate facts. The card PARTLY: FEW SPARE is clearer than the speech.
   - Suggested fix: Match the card and use page 10's own fact: "partly because more was being spent while almost no one was spare, and partly from world prices, like oil."
12. **should-fix, page 15:** "In the year to June twenty twenty-six, prices here went up four point one percent. If petrol and diesel prices hadn't changed... prices would have gone up two point nine percent."
   - Problem: "The year to June" is office language. "If... hadn't changed... would have gone up" is hard for the second-language viewer. There is also an accuracy question: a figure that leaves petrol and diesel out is not the same as one where they hadn't changed (the two differ slightly). If the ledger's entry is a "leave them out" figure, both the speech and the card say something stronger than the ledger. I could not check this.
   - Suggested fix: Check the ledger wording. If it's a "leave them out" figure, say: "In the twelve months to June twenty twenty-six, prices here went up four point one percent. Leave out petrol and diesel... and prices went up two point nine percent." Change the card to match.
13. **should-fix, page 13:** "From late twenty twenty-one into twenty twenty-three, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again and again."
   - Problem: Page 12 says the cure makes prices go up more slowly. Page 13 shows the cure used again and again. Page 15 then shows prices up four point one percent in the year to mid twenty twenty-six. The 70-year-old concludes either "the cure didn't work" or "the film contradicts itself". The film never says what happened to prices after the cure, or that fuel is outside what the cure can reach.
   - Suggested fix: On page 15, after the fuel line, add a short bridge in plain words, as far as the ledger and rules allow, e.g. "The cure works on spending here... it can't make world fuel cheaper." Or say what happened to prices after the rate rises, if the ledger has it.
14. **should-fix, page 14:** "She argues that putting people out of work is the main goal."
   - Problem: It isn't said whose goal, or the goal of what. Right after "our Reserve Bank... see putting people out of work as unfortunate", it can be heard as "the Reserve Bank secretly wants us unemployed". That may be stronger than, or different from, Mattei's claim in the ledger. I could not check this.
   - Suggested fix: Anchor it to the cure, using the ledger's wording: "She argues that putting people out of work is the cure's main goal." Check the ledger entry for how strong her claim is.
15. **should-fix, page 14:** "With more workers waiting at the gate, she says, those inside have less power to ask for more pay."
   - Problem: "Those inside" stretches the picture further; the second-language viewer may not read it as "people with jobs". The film says who pays, but never names who gains (the Klein-spirit rule: who gains and who pays). The 70-year-old works it out; the others don't.
   - Suggested fix: Say "those still in work have less power to ask for more pay." Only if the ledger has it, name who gains as her view, e.g. "That suits employers, she argues."
16. **minor, page 1:** "the usual cure makes loans cost more"
   - Problem: The teen has no loans, and how a loan "costs more" isn't explained until page 12, about five minutes later. This is a small early stumble in the hook.
   - Suggested fix: Give a familiar example: "the usual cure makes loans, like home loans, cost more."
17. **minor, page 5:** "new money, once it's spent, mostly gets more things made. When there aren't any spare... it mostly pushes prices up."
   - Problem: "Gets more things made" and the shortened "aren't any spare" are hard for the second-language viewer. By ear, the rule uses "spare" while the pictures were named "the quiet factory" and "the flat-out factory". Only the screen links them, so the busy parent misses it. "The quiet factory" is never named again after page 3. The same wording comes back on page 16.
   - Suggested fix: On pages 5 and 16: "When there are spare workers, materials and machines, like the quiet factory... new money, once it's spent, mostly means more things are made. When nothing is spare, like the flat-out factory... it mostly pushes prices up."
18. **minor, page 5:** "No spare workers, materials or machines... so no more things get made."
   - Problem: For the hospitals case, a literal viewer (the teen) concludes "the hospitals won't get built at all". Glasgow already shows the real swap (builders went to the war, so homes weren't built), but the film never says it in so many words.
   - Suggested fix: On page 7, make the swap plain: "Builders busy with the war... weren't building homes." That gives viewers what they need for "build hospitals and something else doesn't get built, and prices go up".
19. **minor, page 8:** "Japan's version of our Reserve Bank set out to double the money it had created... in two years."
   - Problem: "Set out to" is a phrasal verb that's hard for the second-language viewer. "The money it had created" can be heard as "all the money in Japan".
   - Suggested fix: "Japan's version of our Reserve Bank planned to double, in two years, the money it had created." Check the ledger for exactly what was to double.
20. **minor, page 9:** "most of the new money just sat in the banks"
   - Problem: The teen and the 70-year-old wonder which banks, and why the money would just sit there. The next line ("never reached the factory") helps, but "spent" isn't said here.
   - Suggested fix: Add three words: "most of the new money just sat in the banks... it wasn't spent."
21. **minor, page 12:** "The Reserve Bank raises its interest rate. Interest is the extra you pay back when you borrow. Many loans cost more"
   - Problem: "Interest" is explained but "rate" isn't. It's never said how the Reserve Bank's own rate makes your loan cost more. The teen and the second-language viewer miss the step.
   - Suggested fix: If the ledger has it: "The Reserve Bank raises its interest rate... and banks charge more on loans, like home loans."
22. **minor, page 13:** "nearly six in a hundred were out of work"
   - Problem: To see the rise, viewers have to remember page 10's "three in a hundred". The busy parent only gets the comparison from the returning dot panel, which they may not be watching.
   - Suggested fix: "nearly six in a hundred were out of work... up from about three."
23. **minor, page 15:** "So part of what you felt at the checkout wasn't new money being spent."
   - Problem: This can be heard as "and the rest was new money being spent", which the film doesn't claim and the ledger may not support.
   - Suggested fix: Name the cause instead: "So part of what you felt at the checkout came from world fuel prices... not from new money being spent here."

2. **must-fix, page 14:** "She argues that putting people out of work is the main goal."
   - Problem: This is stronger than C2-H18's own words. Mattei (The Nation, 2023) says: "The main objective of interest rate hikes is to cool down the labor market" and "Fewer job openings and higher unemployment diminish the bargaining power of workers". In RevDem she calls unemployment "the best disciplinary device to suppress wages". So her main objective is a cooler job market, meaning fewer openings as well as more people out of work. In her account, unemployment is the tool used to weaken workers. The ledger's plain version describes it as the means ("works by putting people out of work"), and the second fact check only confirmed "part of the point". The new line leaves out the fewer-openings half and turns her means into the goal. The card MATTEI ARGUES: THE MAIN GOAL goes with it.
   - Suggested fix: "She argues that the main goal is fewer jobs, and more people out of work." Keep the next sentence ("With more workers waiting at the gate, she says...") as it is.
