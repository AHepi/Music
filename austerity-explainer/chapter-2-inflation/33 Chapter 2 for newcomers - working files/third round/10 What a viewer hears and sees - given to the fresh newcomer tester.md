# Chapter 2 for newcomers - what a viewer hears and sees

Each page: what Malina says, then what is on screen. Nothing else.

## Page 1 - The question (about 34 s)

Malina says: You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. You've heard the warning too. If the government creates new money... prices will go up. Is that always true? It matters... because when prices go up too fast, the usual cure makes loans cost more. And that can put people out of work.

On screen: as in the combined version (page-1 reused, parts swapped, code only), with the chamber card BORROWING COSTS MORE changed to LOANS COST MORE → OUT OF WORK.

## Page 2 - Who can create new money (about 23 s)

Malina says: Who can create new money? Here in New Zealand, the Reserve Bank can. It also tries to stop prices going up too fast. Economists who study how modern money works argue that a government like ours can't run out of its own money. But it can run out of workers, materials and machines.

On screen: page-10's New Zealand street at dusk with the typed card THE RESERVE BANK (no logo) and a small CAN CREATE NEW MONEY; then TRIES TO STOP PRICES GOING UP TOO FAST; page-2's banknote bundles land: NEW MONEY; the card CAN'T RUN OUT OF ITS OWN MONEY / CAN RUN OUT OF..., then page-3's three cut-outs pop up: WORKERS / MATERIALS / MACHINES.

## Page 3 - The quiet factory (about 28 s)

Malina says: Picture two factories. Think of each one as a whole country... all its workers, materials and machines. In the first, the machines stand still. Workers wait at the gate, hoping for a job. Call it the quiet factory. Now new money is spent on what the factory makes. The workers go in. The machines start. More things get made... and prices barely move.

On screen: unchanged (page-3's works A; A WHOLE COUNTRY: WORKERS, MATERIALS, MACHINES; THE QUIET FACTORY; coins rain in: NEW MONEY SPENT; the price counter stays put).

## Page 4 - The flat-out factory, the first guess (about 22 s, then a 4 s hold)

Malina says: In the second factory, every worker and every machine is already busy... working as hard as they can. No one waits at the gate. Call it the flat-out factory. The same new money is spent on what this factory makes. What do you think happens? Have a guess.

On screen: page-3's works B, gate empty, labelled THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY with a small line under it: FLAT OUT = WORKING AS HARD AS THEY CAN. Coins rain in; the counter freezes. WHAT HAPPENS? held about 4 seconds, with a small on-screen marker that the film is waiting for a guess.

## Page 5 - The rule (about 28 s)

Malina says: No spare workers, materials or machines... so no more things get made. Just more money, offered for the same things... and prices go up. Most economists agree on this much. So when there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money, once it's spent, mostly gets more things made. When there aren't any spare... it mostly pushes prices up.

On screen: as before, with the two rule cards reading SPARE → NEW MONEY, ONCE SPENT: MOSTLY MORE THINGS MADE and NONE SPARE → MOSTLY PRICES UP.

## Page 6 - Britain at war (about 31 s)

Malina says: Now a real case. Britain, during the First World War. Millions were in uniform. Soon, fewer than one worker in a hundred was out of work. German submarines sank ships carrying food and materials. And the war was paid for partly with new money. More money... and fewer things to buy. By the end of the war, prices had roughly doubled. Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory.

On screen: page-2's munitions hall, words matched (code only): BRITAIN, FIRST WORLD WAR; a typed card MILLIONS IN UNIFORM; the 100 dots, last nearly empty: FEWER THAN 1 IN 100 OUT OF WORK; SHIPS SUNK: FOOD, MATERIALS; PARTLY NEW MONEY; MORE MONEY / FEWER THINGS; bars 1914 ×1 and 1918 ABOUT ×2; quick cut to works B: CLOSE TO THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY.

## Page 7 - Glasgow's homes (about 36 s)

Malina says: By nineteen fifteen, crowds of war workers had come to Glasgow, a British city, where homes were already short. With builders and materials gone to the war, few new homes were built. So for homes, Glasgow was like the flat-out factory. Landlords raised the rent... in places by up to a quarter. By November, about twenty thousand families were refusing to pay the extra. Within weeks, Britain's Parliament brought rents on small homes back to what they were before the war.

On screen: page-4's Glasgow tenement street (code only), titled GLASGOW 1915 (the year is now spoken) with HOMES ALREADY SHORT; workers stream in; the building-site shot: BUILDERS AND MATERIALS GONE TO WAR / FEW NEW HOMES; a tag FOR HOMES: LIKE THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY; RENT RAISED / UP TO +25% IN PLACES; the counter to 20,000 FAMILIES REFUSING THE EXTRA; the stamp RENTS BACK TO BEFORE THE WAR. Never WON or RENTS FROZEN. No Mary Barbour card.

## Page 8 - Japan, the second guess (about 17 s, then a 4 s hold)

Malina says: Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's version of our Reserve Bank set out to double the money it had created... in two years. If the warning is right, prices should go up fast. What do you think happened? Have a guess.

On screen: unchanged (page-5 part A, JAPAN 2013, ×2 MONEY / IN TWO YEARS, the dashed line IF THE WARNING IS RIGHT climbs under WHAT HAPPENED?), hold about 4 seconds.

## Page 9 - Japan, the answer (about 32 s)

Malina says: Prices barely moved. For about eight years, they went up about half a percent a year, on average. Why? One reason economists give... most of the new money just sat in the banks. In our picture, most of it never reached the factory. So it's not just how much new money there is. It's whether it's spent... and on what. Are there spare workers, materials and machines to make what it buys?

On screen: as before (the almost-level line ABOUT ½% A YEAR; coins piling up in front of the card bank: MOST SAT IN THE BANKS; NOT JUST HOW MUCH / WHETHER IT'S SPENT, AND ON WHAT), then one more typed card: SPARE WORKERS, MATERIALS, MACHINES TO MAKE WHAT IT BUYS?

## Page 10 - New Zealand, the third guess (about 29 s, then a 4 s hold)

Malina says: Now, here. When Covid hit in twenty twenty, the Reserve Bank created new money. And the government spent more... including help with wages. By the end of twenty twenty-one, almost everyone looking for work had found it. Only about three in a hundred were out of work... the lowest in more than thirty years. Which factory was New Zealand closer to? Have a guess.

On screen: page-8 part B's New Zealand street (code only): NEW ZEALAND 2020; notes land: NEW MONEY; GOVERNMENT SPENT MORE / INCLUDING HELP WITH WAGES; 2021 with the 100-dot panel, 3 empty: ABOUT 3 IN 100 OUT OF WORK / LOWEST IN 30+ YEARS, credited Stats NZ; wide shot of both factories: WHICH ONE?, held about 4 seconds.

## Page 11 - New Zealand, the answer (about 19 s) - new page

Malina says: Close to the flat-out factory. Almost no one was waiting at the gate. And prices here went up fast... partly from that, and partly from world prices, like oil. The biggest push came from homes... building new ones, and rents.

On screen: quick cut to works B with the gate almost empty: CLOSE TO THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY. Then PRICES WENT UP FAST, splitting into two small cards: PARTLY: FEW SPARE and PARTLY: WORLD PRICES, LIKE OIL (page-7 part A's oil barrel, no counter). Then page-4's building-site shot relabelled NEW ZEALAND with BIGGEST PUSH: BUILDING NEW HOMES, AND RENTS. No house-price figures, no +30%, no review desk.

## Page 12 - The usual cure (about 30 s)

Malina says: So what's the usual cure? The Reserve Bank raises its interest rate. Interest is the extra you pay back when you borrow. Many loans cost more... so people have less to spend. Businesses sell less, and hire fewer people. And prices go up more slowly. Not down... just more slowly. In our picture, the flat-out factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate.

On screen: as before, without the opening CLOSE TO THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY cut (now on page 11). Ends on works B: smoke thins, windows dim, the queue returns: MORE WORKERS AT THE GATE.

## Page 13 - Who pays, here (about 26 s)

Malina says: Who pays for the cure? From late twenty twenty-one into twenty twenty-three, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again and again. Loans cost more, and jobs got harder to find. By the middle of twenty twenty-six... nearly six in a hundred were out of work. The interest rate isn't the only reason for that.

On screen: the dial climbs in steps: LATE 2021 TO 2023: AGAIN AND AGAIN (no rate figures). The 100-dot panel from page 10 returns with nearly 6 empty dots: NEARLY 6 IN 100 OUT OF WORK, Stats NZ, MIDDLE OF 2026, and a small NOT THE ONLY REASON. The ABOUT 170,000 card is removed.

## Page 14 - Two views (about 27 s)

Malina says: Our Reserve Bank, and others like it, see putting people out of work as unfortunate... but not the goal. Clara E. /məˈteɪi/ is the economist whose book this series follows. She argues that putting people out of work is the main goal. With more workers waiting at the gate, she says, those inside have less power to ask for more pay.

On screen: page-9 part B (code only): OUR RESERVE BANK AND OTHERS LIKE IT: UNFORTUNATE, NOT THE GOAL (no quotation marks); her book with CLARA E. MATTEI, THE CAPITAL ORDER (2022); MATTEI ARGUES: THE MAIN GOAL; the queue grows and the iron weight drops on the pay packet: LESS POWER TO ASK FOR MORE PAY. No portrait.

## Page 15 - Back at the checkout (about 30 s)

Malina says: And the checkout? In the year to June twenty twenty-six, prices here went up four point one percent. If petrol and diesel prices hadn't changed... prices would have gone up two point nine percent. When fuel like that costs more, almost everything that's made or moved costs more... in both factories. So part of what you felt at the checkout wasn't new money being spent.

On screen: a brief return to page-1's basket; page-7 part B's street with bars 4.1% and 2.9%, labelled IF PETROL AND DIESEL PRICES HADN'T CHANGED, credited STATS NZ, YEAR TO JUNE 2026; the two-factories wide shot, the oil barrel lands by each works and both counters flip up: BOTH FACTORIES; back to the basket with PART OF IT: NOT NEW MONEY SPENT.

## Page 16 - Spent on what? (about 25 s, then the 4 s fade)

Malina says: Does new money always push prices up? No. When there are spare workers, materials and machines... new money, once it's spent, mostly gets more things made. When there aren't any spare... it mostly pushes prices up. So next time you hear the warning... ask two questions. Spent on what? And who pays for the cure?

On screen: the two-factories wide shot with page 5's rule cards; page-10 part B's chamber with the invented politician (no party colours); SPENT ON WHAT? and WHO PAYS FOR THE CURE? land and hold; the 4-second fade.
