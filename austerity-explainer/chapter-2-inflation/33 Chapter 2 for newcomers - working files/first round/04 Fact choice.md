# Fact choice - chapter 2 newcomer version

**What this is:** working notes on which facts to use in a newcomer version of chapter 2 ("what
really causes inflation"). It covers which cases to keep, merge, drop or put off, the exact plain
wording the research ledger allows (qualifications included), a plain version of every number a
newcomer will hear, and the facts that will change soon or are argued over. Source for the series:
Clara E. Mattei, *The Capital Order* (2022). Every id points to
`chapter-2-inflation/18 Research - chapter 2 - what really causes inflation.md`. No new facts were
added and nothing outside this folder was changed.

Read for this: the whole ledger (history, cases, New Zealand, how inflation works, the script fact
check additions, the second fact check of 12 October and the Traps), the recorded script, the
outlines, chapter 1 as recorded, the voice and house rules, the Pinker toolkit's teaching and
writing parts, and the first comment lines of page-1.js to page-10.js.

## 1. The short answer

The recorded chapter visits **five places** (Britain, Glasgow, Japan, Germany and its Ruhr, New
Zealand) plus an oil shock with no place named, **nine different years** (1915, 1920, 1923, 1973,
2008, 2013, 2021, 2023 and "this year", plus "the end of the war"), **four kinds of money** (pounds,
yen, marks, American dollars) and several terms it never explains (shares, investments, quadruples,
"the support", and the Reserve Bank on page 8, two pages before it is explained). All of that in
620 words.

The lean set below keeps **four places** (Britain, Japan, Germany, New Zealand, with Britain twice and
New Zealand three times), puts every rate and share in **percent** (explained once), and drops the
Bank of England after 2008, Glasgow and the 1973 oil shock. It gives each remaining case room to be
explained.

## 2. How each case was rated

**Ease for a newcomer** (5 = easiest): counts the new places, years, kinds of money and terms a
viewer has to take in. **Vivid** (5 = most vivid): people, objects and pictures you can see.
**Explaining:** how much has to be said before the case makes sense.

The argument has eight steps:

- **S0** The question, and what inflation is.
- **S1** Creating money isn't the same as spending it.
- **S2** The rule: spent where things are idle, more gets made; spent where everything is flat out,
  prices rise.
- **S3** Things we can't make more of in a hurry (homes).
- **S4** The far edge: new money while less and less is made.
- **S5** A different cause: costs, not spending.
- **S6** The honest edge here, in New Zealand.
- **S7** The usual cure, and who pays: here and now.

## 3. The lean set, step by step, with the wording the ledger allows

The order below follows the argument's logic (does money alone do it? no; then where it's spent;
then the edges; then who pays). The teaching plan may re-order; the facts don't depend on the
order, except that the 3.2% figure should come before the ending reuses it.

Each line is wording the ledger allows. Brackets after a line say what must stay or what to avoid.

### Step 1. The question, and what inflation is (about 70 words)

- "Inflation means prices in general going up." [C2-XA04, C2-M01. "In general" matters: one price,
  like petrol, isn't inflation on its own, though it feeds the average.]
- "To measure it, the statistics office fills an imaginary shopping basket with what households
  usually buy... food, rent, petrol, power... and checks the price of that same basket every three
  months." [C2-M02. Don't use the "2,800 shops" figure anywhere.]
- Keep the opening dateless: "The same basket costs more than it did a year ago." If a number is
  wanted: "about four percent more than a year earlier" [C2-XA05], but that dates on 22 October.

### Step 2. The starting point, for viewers who missed chapter 1 (about 35 words)

- "Economists who study how modern money works say a country that issues its own money can't run out
  of it. What it can run out of is workers, materials and machines." [C2-M12. Said as theirs.
  "Issues its own money" carries the ledger's limit: it applies to countries that borrow in their
  own money, which New Zealand does. Never name the school.]

### Step 3. Creating money isn't the same as spending it: Japan (about 75 words)

- "In twenty thirteen, Japan's central bank, the bank that can create the country's money, set out
  to double the amount of money it had created... in two years." [C2-XB03; Bank of Japan statement
  confirmed in the second fact check.]
- "For about eight years, prices barely moved: about half a percent a year, on average." [C2-XB04,
  C2-C06. Eight years = 2013 to 2021, before the 2022 surge. On screen write "ABOUT ½%", never an
  exact "0.5%": the plain yearly average for 2013 to 2021 is nearer 0.6 to 0.7%; the Bank of Japan's
  own figure is 0.5%; without the 2014 tax rise it is lower.] A safer alternative that fits every figure: "less than one percent a year".
- "Partly because most of that new money just sat in the banks." [C2-C06, C2-XB03: the
  mainstream explanation. Keep "partly": the ledger also lists settled expectations, an ageing
  population and weak spending, and some economists say prices would have fallen without it.]
- The film's point, said plainly: creating money isn't the same as spending it. [The second fact
  check's reviewer says this supports the "spent on what?" point.]

### Step 4. The rule: two factories (about 90 words)

- No real place, no figures: an idle factory and a flat-out factory get the same new money.
- The ledger's plain rule: "With idle workers and machines, extra spending mostly raises output.
  When everything is already running flat out, it mostly raises prices." [C2-XA03. Keep "mostly".
  C2-M09: prices can start rising before everything is flat out, as particular industries hit
  bottlenecks, so say "barely move", "mostly" or "tends to", never "prices don't move".]
- Optional half-line: this is textbook economics, not a fringe idea. [C2-XA03 "mainstream textbook
  framing"; C2-M05 "accepted across schools".]

### Step 5. The real flat-out factory: Britain in the First World War (about 75 words)

- "Britain paid for the war partly with new money." [C2-XA01, C2-H03. Keep "partly": only about a
  quarter came from taxes, and the rest was new money plus borrowing, not split out.]
- "During the war years, fewer than one worker in a hundred was out of work." [C2-H02. The yearly
  figures for 1915 to 1918 are all under one percent. For 1915 alone it is only just under (0.95%),
  and early 1915 months were slightly over, so "the war years" is safer than "1915".]
- "Factories were turned over to war work, and German submarines sank ships carrying food and
  materials." [Second fact check; C2-H03, C2-XA02. Don't tie the sinkings to 1915: the worst year
  was 1917. Never use the "40% of shipping" figure.]
- "More money, chasing fewer things to buy." [C2-H03: a simplification; how much was shortage and
  how much money is argued.]
- "By the end of the war, everyday prices had roughly doubled." [C2-H01. "Roughly" stays.]

### Step 6. The far edge: Germany, 1923 (about 75 words; first to cut)

- "Germany, nineteen twenty-three. Prices were already rising fast." [C2-XC02. The occupation turned
  high inflation into runaway inflation; it didn't start it. Keep "already".]
- "Then French and Belgian soldiers marched into Germany's coal and steel heartland." [C2-C02.
  Leaving out the name Ruhr removes a place and the sound-symbol word. Don't use the 80% steel and
  71% coal shares: one 1936 history. If page 6 is reused, its "THE RUHR" label must go, because the
  words on screen can't say more than Malina does.]
- Optional: "after Germany fell behind on what it owed after the war". [C2-C02: missed deliveries,
  chiefly coal. Adds an idea, so leave it out unless asked.]
- "The government told workers to stop work in protest... and kept paying them, with new money."
  [C2-C02 and second fact check: strictly, the protest meant refusing to work for the occupiers,
  and the government paid companies too, but the line is a fair plain summary.]
- "Less and less was being made." [C2-XC02. No size: the "a good third" fall was seen only in
  search excerpts.]
- One number only. Either "By November, one American dollar cost more than four trillion German
  marks." [C2-C03; official rate 4.2 trillion on 20 November, confirmed twice, and it matches
  page 6's counter.] Or "A loaf of bread cost billions of marks." [C2-C03's note: "billions" is the
  safe word. Easier for a newcomer, but it was never re-checked in the second fact check.] My pick:
  the dollar, because it was checked twice.

### Step 7. Here, in twenty twenty-one: flat out, and homes (about 95 words)

- "By the end of twenty twenty-one, unemployment here was three point two percent... the lowest
  since records began in nineteen eighty-six." [C2-N09, second fact check. "Lowest ever recorded"
  means since 1986. If "unsustainable" is used, it is the review's word, not a fact: others see a
  tight job market as good for workers.]
- "House prices rose about thirty percent in the twelve months to the middle of twenty twenty-one."
  [C2-XC03. This is the house price index, and only for the year to mid-2021. Over the calendar
  year 2021 it was nearer a quarter.]
- House prices are not in the shopping basket, so they are not part of the inflation figure (ledger
  Trap). One clause keeps a newcomer from mixing them up: "House prices aren't even in the
  basket... but they rose about thirty percent." Step 1's basket makes that clause easy.
- Why house prices rose is argued (C2-N08: new money, low interest rates, government spending and
  a housing shortage all named; no estimate splits them). If the script ties them to the pandemic
  response, it must say "in part": "A Royal Commission, an official inquiry, found the pandemic
  response was part of the reason, along with big forces from overseas." [C2-M10, C2-N14.]
- "An independent review of the Reserve Bank said the support from the Reserve Bank and the
  government together was right at first... but, looking back, too strong for too long."
  [C2-XC04, C2-N15. "Looking back" (the review's "with the benefit of hindsight") must stay. The
  review does not discuss house prices: don't place its verdict as a verdict on houses. On screen,
  no quotation marks, since the review itself was read only through interest.co.nz.]
- "The support" needs a few plain words the first time. The ledger allows: the Reserve Bank cut its
  interest rate to a quarter of a percent in March 2020 [C2-XD03] and created new money [C2-N06],
  and the government spent more [C2-N14: a Covid fund of about sixty billion dollars]. Keep it to "the Reserve Bank's and the government's
  support during Covid".

### Step 8. A different cause: costs, not spending (about 65 words)

- "When fuel gets dearer, almost everything that's made or moved gets dearer too... whatever the
  government is spending." [C2-M06.] "So sometimes the cause isn't spending at all. It's costs."
  ["Sometimes" stays: in 2021-22 costs and spending both played a part.]
- "In the year to June twenty twenty-six, prices here rose four point one percent. If petrol and
  diesel prices hadn't changed, they'd have risen two point nine." [C2-N05, C2-M04, C2-XA05,
  C2-XC05, and Stats NZ's own wording in the second fact check. "Without fuel" is an acceptable
  short form.] Rounded for the ear: "about four percent... and without the rise in petrol and
  diesel, just under three."
- Never "mostly fuel" or "a price rise that's mostly fuel": wrong arithmetic (ledger Trap; C2-M18 as
  worded). Fuel adds about 1.2 points of the 4.1; prices without fuel still rose 2.9%.
- Don't say why fuel rose: the ledger didn't research it.
- Optional: "Petrol cost more than a quarter more than a year before." [C2-N05: 27.5%.] Diesel was
  up 71%. Each is one more number.

### Step 9. The usual cure, and who pays: Britain, 1920 (about 115 words)

- Why there was a cure at all: "After the war, prices kept climbing." [C2-H06, C2-H01. Optional
  number: about fifteen percent in 1920 alone.]
- How the cure works, the central banks' own account: "When the interest rate goes up, many
  mortgages and loans cost more. People spend more on those, and less on other things. When people
  spend less, businesses find it harder to raise prices." [C2-XD01.] "...and businesses hire fewer
  people." [C2-M17.] It works with a delay [C2-XD01, C2-M17: biggest effect after one to two
  years].
- "In nineteen twenty, the Bank of England, Britain's central bank, pushed its interest rate up to
  seven percent, and held it there for about a year." [C2-H07. If "seven percent" means nothing to a
  newcomer, "pushed its interest rate up and held it high for about a year" is also allowed.]
- "Within a year, unemployment jumped from about two percent to about eleven." [C2-H08, second
  fact check. Say what followed, not what caused it: whether the interest rate caused the slump or
  deepened one already coming is argued. Never "then came the crash".]
- "Prices did fall." [C2-H09. Optional: by about a fifth in two years.]
- The other side, in one line: "Central banks call the lost jobs a cost, not the goal." [C2-M17.]
- Mattei, said as hers: "Mattei argues that was part of the point. With fewer jobs to go round, she
  says, workers lose the power to ask for more." [Lean on C2-XD02 and C2-H18 (high: her own
  words). C2-H17 is medium, a reviewer's paraphrase: backup only. Her reading is disputed (Glasner;
  Tomlinson).]
- Optional who-gains line, if the voice brief wants one: "Britain's central bank found its new money
  pushed up the prices of shares... and the richest five percent of households held about forty
  percent of those." [C2-XB05. Needs "shares" explained. "The biggest gains went to the richest" is
  an inference, not the study's finding; the Bank also says most people would have been worse off
  without it.]

### Step 10. Here and now, and the closing questions (about 85 words)

- "Here, the Reserve Bank cut its interest rate to a quarter of a percent in twenty twenty, then
  raised it to five and a half percent by twenty twenty-three." [C2-XD03.]
- "Since the end of twenty twenty-one, unemployment has climbed from three point two percent to five
  point six, the highest in about ten years." [C2-N11, C2-XD05. Say "about ten years" or "more than
  a decade", not "a ten-year high" (sources differ: since September 2015, or since March 2014).]
- "That's about a hundred and seventy-one thousand people out of work." [C2-XD05. This is how many
  are unemployed now, not how many more than in 2021: the ledger has no 2021 head count, and
  "tens of thousands more" is unsourced.]
- Don't say the interest rate caused all of it: unemployment kept rising through 2024 and 2025
  while the interest rate was being cut [C2-XD05], and the 2023 slowdowns also involved cyclones
  [C2-N11]. Putting the two facts side by side is fine; "because" is not.
- "In July and September this year, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again." [C2-XD04.
  Past tense, with the months: this stays true whatever happens on 28 October. The recorded
  "is raising its interest rate again" is the present tense the ledger says to avoid.]
- If the script questions using the interest rate against a fuel price rise, it can only say that
  is debated [C2-N12, C2-M17], and it must not suggest the Reserve Bank's only reason is fuel: the
  Bank names wider worries [C2-M18].
- Closing: "Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?" (no fact needed).

Terms that need a few plain words at first use (definitions, not facts):

- **New money:** money a country's central bank creates. One name all the way; never "printing
  money", "money creation" or "stimulus", even though the Germany entries say "printing
  banknotes". (Strictly, most money is created by ordinary banks lending, C2-M15, so say a central
  bank "can create" money, not that it "makes all our money".)
- **Central bank:** the bank that can create a country's money and sets its main interest rate.
  Japan's central bank (don't name the Bank of Japan); "the Bank of England, Britain's central
  bank"; "the Reserve Bank, our central bank".
- **Interest rate:** "the price of borrowing" (as recorded). Always "interest rate", never "rates"
  on its own: in New Zealand "rates" also means the council's property tax.
