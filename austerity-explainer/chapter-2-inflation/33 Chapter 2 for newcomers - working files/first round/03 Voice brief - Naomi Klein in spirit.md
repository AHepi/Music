# 33 Voice brief - Naomi Klein in spirit - working notes

**What this is:** a voice brief for whoever rewrites chapter 2 ("what really causes inflation") for
newcomers. It tells writers how to sound like Naomi Klein in spirit without using her words, and how to
fit that to Malina, a warm, steady New Zealand narrator on a phone video. Source for the series: Clara
E. Mattei, *The Capital Order* (2022). Facts: only the ids in `18 Research - chapter 2 - what really
causes inflation.md`. Written 14 October 2026. The brief is sections 1 to 9; the working notes (what I
read, what I ran, what I didn't check) are sections 10 and 11.

**The one rule over everything:** the research ledger wins. Facts, numbers, qualifications ("about",
"up to", "partly", "can") and who-said-what (Mattei's views said as hers) come first. Then a newcomer
must be able to follow it. Only then the urgency, and last the rhythm.

---

## 1. What makes Naomi Klein recognisable (my own description)

This describes her public books, essays and talks in my own words. It quotes nothing of hers, on
purpose.

1. **How she opens.** She puts you somewhere first: a named place, a date, a group of people, often a
   scene she saw as a reporter, or a news moment the reader lived through and still remembers. The big
   idea waits until you are standing in the scene. In talks she often begins by naming what the room
   is worried about that week, so the audience feels spoken to, not lectured.
2. **How she makes the stakes personal.** Early on she shows what the issue does to a home, a pay
   packet, a town or a child's future. The reader is treated as someone with something to lose, not a
   spectator. She uses "we" for the people who share the consequences, and she will put herself in
   the story (her doubts, her family) to close the distance.
3. **Who gains and who pays.** Her signature move: follow a decision to its results and set the
   winners and the losers side by side, by name where she can. She often puts the official reason for
   a policy next to what the policy actually did, and lets the gap make the argument.
4. **Concrete scenes, places and people.** Her arguments are built from things you could photograph:
   a building, a document, a queue, a price, a meeting and who was in the room. She likes to quote
   officials and experts in their own words, especially when their words give the game away.
5. **From a scene to a pattern.** One case is a story. She lines up a second and a third, from other
   places and decades, then names what links them, then brings the pattern home to the present. The
   pattern is the payoff.
6. **Rhetorical questions.** She asks the question the reader is already half-forming, or one that
   exposes a contradiction ("if that's true, why...?"), and then answers it. Questions mark the turns
   in her argument. In talks she often leaves the audience with a question to carry out the door.
7. **Rhythm.** A longer sentence that gathers detail, then a short one that lands. Lists of three.
   Fragments for emphasis. A key phrase repeated like a refrain, especially in speeches. Plain words,
   very little jargon, now and then a dry, ironic aside.
8. **Moral clarity.** She says plainly what she thinks is wrong, why, and who has the power to change
   it. She doesn't hide behind "some say". But she usually states the other side's case in its own
   terms before taking it apart, and she tends to end on agency: people who organised and won
   something, or a choice that is still open.
9. **Evidence and uncertainty.** Her books are heavily sourced, leaning on documents, statistics and
   interviews, and she uses numbers to make scale land. Her confidence is high: she states a thesis
   and drives it home. Some reviewers have argued that she sometimes draws the line from a crisis to a
   deliberate plan more firmly than the evidence allows. Whatever one thinks of that, it is the one
   move this film must never copy.
10. **How she sounds when she speaks.** Measured and conversational, rarely loud. The urgency is in
    what she says and how the argument moves, not in volume. That suits Malina exactly.

## 2. Turning that into Malina's narration

Malina is an AI voice with a warm, steady New Zealand accent, heard on a phone, by people with no
economics background, many of whom never saw chapter 1. The urgency lives in the words, never in the
voice.

| Klein's move | For Malina |
|---|---|
| Opens in a scene | Open on a moment the viewer has lived: the checkout, a rent rise, news of the interest rate, a job ad. Then their question in their words. Never start with "So..." pointing back to chapter 1: restate the question so a first-time viewer is with you. |
| Stakes made personal | Within about twenty seconds, say what the answer decides for the viewer: who gets asked to pay when prices rise. "You" is the viewer. "We", "our" and "here" only for facts New Zealanders share ("our Reserve Bank"). Malina has no life story: no "I", no "when I was in Glasgow". |
| Who gains, who pays | Put them in neighbouring sentences. Use numbers that are people (about twenty thousand households; about a hundred and seventy thousand people looking for work) rather than adjectives. Let institutions speak through their own documents ("the Bank's own study", "an independent review"), always from the ledger. |
| Concrete scenes | Place and year as a short opener ("Glasgow, nineteen fifteen."), then people doing something you could film. Real people only from the ledger, and on screen only as typed name cards. Imagined examples are announced as imagined ("Picture two factories") and never given real-looking numbers. |
| Scene to pattern | After each scene, one sentence that says what it showed, in the chapter's fixed words. Then bring it home ("And here?"). One case per page. Repeat the pattern in the same words two or three times across the chapter: that's her refrain and the toolkit's memory aid at once. |
| Rhetorical questions | Ask the viewer's own question, then answer within a sentence or two. One open question at a time. The closing pair of questions is the one deliberate exception. |
| Rhythm | Short spoken sentences (aim under fifteen words; never over twenty). A short line after a longer one. Fragments are fine ("Not one price tag. The whole basket."). A list of three at most once a page. One number per sentence. A pause ("...") before a turn, or after a number that needs to land: about one to three a page. |
| Moral clarity | Name the actor and the action ("The Bank of England raises its interest rate"). Say who loses work. Let facts placed side by side carry the judgement. Any claim about intent belongs to Mattei, said as hers, every time. |
| Evidence and uncertainty | Qualifiers short and early in the sentence ("about", "up to", "partly", "can"). Say the honest edge plainly ("So yes... new money can push prices up"). Give the other side one fair line, then make the point. |
| Calm delivery | No capitals, no bracketed cues, no exclamation marks (my call: they may push the voice the way capitals do). No volume words: outrageous, shocking, scandal, obscene, rigged, greed. |

**New Zealand touches.** New Zealand spellings (labour, organise). Years as said: "nineteen fifteen",
"two thousand and eight", "twenty twenty-one". Explain foreign institutions by comparing them with ours:
"the Bank of England, Britain's version of our Reserve Bank". Say "Britain's Parliament", so nobody
pictures Wellington. Mattei is written /məˈteɪi/ and Ruhr /rʊə/.

**Stay neutral before the election.** The ledger notes a general election on 7 November 2026 (C2-M18).
No parties, party colours, ministers or "this government". Describe the Reserve Bank and the review;
don't campaign against them.

**Where the urgency goes in this chapter.**
- Lean in: the opening stakes, the people pages (Glasgow), the who-pays pages (Britain 1920, New
  Zealand now) and the closing questions.
- Stay calm and plain: the teaching pages, where a newcomer is building the idea: the two factories and
  any test case such as Japan. Urgency there hurries the listener, and that hurry is what lost people
  last time.

## 3. Do and don't

**Do**
- Open on something the viewer has felt, in the first sentence.
- Say why the answer matters to them in the first twenty seconds.
- Restate the question so someone who missed chapter 1 can follow.
- Give each case its own page, then one sentence on what it shows.
- Use active verbs with named actors: who raised the rent, who raised the interest rate.
- Put who gains and who pays side by side, each with its ledger id.
- Quote institutions' own findings only where the ledger has them, and keep their wording close.
- Ask the viewer's question, then answer it within a breath or two.
- Keep one idea and at most one number per sentence; round only as far as the ledger allows.
- Explain every needed term in a few plain words the first time, then use the same word every time.
- Mark Mattei's views every time: "Mattei argues", "she says", "Mattei sees it differently".
- Give the other side one fair line before making the point.
- Keep the honest edge: new money can push prices up when it chases things we can't make more of in a
  hurry.
- End on agency or a question the viewer can carry away, as long as the ledger supports it (Glasgow's
  tenants won a rent freeze).
- Read every page aloud at Malina's pace before it goes to the voice.

**Don't**
- Don't use her titles, coined phrases or near-copies (section 4). Never quote her or name her.
- Don't use the word "shock" at all, nor "disaster" or "doctrine".
- Don't claim intent the ledger doesn't hold ("on purpose", "engineered", "it's no accident"), except as
  Mattei's argument, said as hers.
- Don't say a rate rise caused a slump where the ledger says that's debated: say what followed.
- Don't pile up cases to build force. Eight cases in four and a half minutes is what lost people.
- Don't stack questions or leave several hanging.
- Don't use capitals for emphasis, brackets, exclamation marks, or digits in the narration.
- Don't use "we" to mean a political side, and don't ask viewers to act, vote or protest.
- Don't give Malina memories, opinions or a life story.
- Don't invent people, quotes or numbers, or present an imagined person as real.
- Don't swap synonyms for variety: not "rates", "the OCR", "cash rate" or "borrowing costs" for
  "interest rate"; not "printing money" or "money creation" for "new money".
- Don't use jargon without a plain explanation: quantitative easing, monetary base, CPI, tradables,
  demand-pull, cost-push, output gap, hyperinflation, stimulus, wage-price spiral, bargaining power,
  deflation, reparations, bonds, assets, basis points, fiscal, monetary.
- Don't say "MMT" or name the school; say "economists who study how modern money works".
- Don't use irony that only works if the voice signals it: on the ear it may be taken literally.
- Don't use "price", "cost" or "dear" as figures of speech ("the price was paid in jobs"): in this
  chapter those words mean what things cost. The chapter's one figure of speech is "who pays", used
  on purpose as its refrain.

**Suggested fixed names** (these match the recorded script; the writer makes the final call): inflation
("prices in general, going up", explained once); new money; the Reserve Bank (other countries: "their
central bank, their version of our Reserve Bank"); interest rate ("the price of borrowing", explained
once); unemployment ("out of every hundred people who want a job, how many can't find one", explained
once if the rewrite thinks it's needed); the usual cure (raising the interest rate); things we can't
make more of in a hurry; workers, materials and machines (chapter 1's words); economists who study how
modern money works.

**Phrases she coined or made famous**

- disaster apartheid, coronavirus capitalism, Screen New Deal, Blockadia, mirror world, end times

**Other people's lines she made famous, also out**
- Milton Friedman's line that only a crisis brings real change, and paraphrases such as "a crisis is an

**Words that would make the narration sound like an imitation of her**
- extractivism, sacrifice zone, Green New Deal, "the leap", any "[something] capitalism" coinage

**Near-copies most likely to slip into this chapter**
- Any "shock": oil shock, price shock, inflation shock, cost-of-living shock, "in shock", "shocking".

- "rate-hike capitalism", "austerity capitalism", "inflation capitalism", or any new "-ism" label.
- "disaster", "doctrine", "crisis profiteers", "profiting from the crisis", "using the crisis".
- "this changes everything", "it changes everything".
- "saying no isn't enough", "it's not enough to say no".
- "on fire", "burning" (prices on fire, the economy burning).
- "no space, no choice, no jobs", or any "no this, no that, no jobs" list of three.
- "the battle for...", "the rise of...", "winning the world we need".
- "mirror", "mirror image", or a person's "double" in the sense of her book.
- "the take" as a noun.
- "greed" or "greedflation" (not hers, but the ledger rules it out: C2-M08, C2-C17, C2-N13).

## 5. Five example passages

Written fresh for this chapter's subject, as they would be sent to the voice (the accent line is added
later). They show the voice; they are not the rewrite. Every fact traces to the ledger ids listed. The
checker in section 8 passes all five: no never-use words, no capitals, digits or brackets, and no
sentence over twenty words.

### Passage 1 - the opening: stakes first, the viewer's own question (71 words, about 30 seconds)

You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's
inflation... prices in general, going up. And you've probably heard the warning that comes with it. If
the government creates new money, prices will rise. It sounds like common sense. But the answer
matters... because the usual cure for rising prices can cost people their jobs. So is the warning
right?

- Facts: C2-XA04 and C2-M01 (inflation means prices in general going up, measured on a basket);
  C2-XA05 (the basket costs about four percent more than a year earlier); C2-M17, C2-XD01 and C2-N11's
  note (the usual cure is a higher interest rate; it slows spending and hiring; fewer jobs is the
  mainstream direction of the effect, hence "can").
- What it shows: starts from something felt; explains the one term in five words; puts the question in
  the viewer's own words, so chapter 1 isn't needed; states the stakes in the first twenty seconds;
  keeps "can".
- Caution: "the usual cure" is a promise. The script must say what it is (raising the interest rate)
  within about a minute.

### Passage 2 - a real place, real people, then the pattern (86 words, about 37 seconds)

Glasgow, nineteen fifteen. War work pulls crowds of workers into the city... where homes are already
short. And you can't build a house in a hurry. So landlords raise the rent. In some parts of the city,
by up to a quarter. The families renting keep paying the old rent... and refuse the rise. By November,
about twenty thousand households are refusing. Within weeks, Britain's Parliament freezes rents.
Remember that. When more people want something we can't make more of in a hurry... its price climbs.

- Facts: C2-XB01 (war work drew crowds into Glasgow, where homes were already short; rises of up to a
  quarter in some districts; strikers paid the old rent and refused the rise; about twenty thousand by
  November, with "households" a fair unit); C2-H04; C2-XB02 (the freeze came about five weeks after the
  November peak); "you can't build a house in a hurry" was confirmed in the second fact check; the
  pattern sentence is the chapter's fixed phrase (C2-XA03, C2-M05).
- What it shows: place and year first; real people doing something you can picture; who raised the
  price and who refused it; people organising and winning (her turn to agency, here fully sourced);
  the scene ends on the pattern in one sentence a viewer could repeat.
- Cautions: "within weeks" is only true counted from November, so it must follow the November line.
  "Up to a quarter" and "in some parts of the city" stay (C2-XB01; not every rent rose a quarter).
  Mary Barbour may appear only as a typed name card.

### Passage 3 - who gains: follow the new money (114 words, about 49 seconds)

Britain, after the crash of two thousand and eight. The Bank of England, Britain's version of our
Reserve Bank, creates hundreds of billions of pounds of new money. So where does it go? The Bank's own
study, in twenty twelve, gives an answer. The new money pushed up the prices of shares and other
investments. Now look at who holds them. The typical household had about fifteen hundred pounds in
savings and investments, not counting pensions. The richest five percent had, on average, about a
hundred and seventy-five thousand. The Bank says most people would have been worse off without the new
money. But the gains went to the people who owned the most.

- Facts: C2-C08 and C2-XB06 (hundreds of billions of pounds of new money after 2008); C2-C10 and
  C2-XB05 (the Bank's 2012 study: prices of shares and other investments pushed up; a median household
  of about £1,500 against a top-five-percent average of £175,000; the benefits go to the households
  holding the most; the Bank's own view that most people would have been worse off without it).
- What it shows: follow the money; the institution's own study as the evidence; two numbers set side
  by side instead of an adjective; the other side's case stated fairly before the point.
- Cautions: "not counting pensions" must stay; dropping it would make typical households look poorer
  than the study says. "Savings and investments" is my plain version of "financial assets held
  outside pensions" (the ledger shortens it to "gross assets"): check that wording against the paper
  before recording. Don't say the new money left shop prices alone (UK prices rose about five percent
  in 2011, C2-C08's note), and don't mention houses (the study covers financial assets only).

### Passage 4 - who pays: the usual cure, and Mattei's argument as hers (98 words, about 42 seconds)

So what's the usual cure for rising prices? Britain, nineteen twenty. The Bank of England raises its
interest rate, the price of borrowing, to seven percent. Loans cost more. People spend less. Prices do
fall... but within a year, unemployment jumps from about two in every hundred workers to eleven. Some
historians say the economy was heading down anyway, as a short boom after the war ran out. /məˈteɪi/
sees it differently. She argues the lost jobs were part of the point. With fewer jobs to go round, she
says, workers lose the power to ask for more.

- Facts: C2-H07 (seven percent from April 1920); C2-XD01 (loans cost more, people spend less); C2-H09
  (prices fell); C2-H08 and the second fact check (unemployment about two percent in 1920, eleven in
  1921, on the Bank of England whole-economy series); C2-H06 (a short boom in 1919 and 1920); C2-H08's
  and C2-XD02's notes (whether the rate rise caused the slump is debated; historians point to the end
  of the post-war boom); C2-H17, C2-H18 and C2-XD02 (Mattei's argument, as hers).
- What it shows: the mechanism told with actors and verbs; what followed, not what caused it; the other
  side in one line; Mattei's claim about intent marked twice ("she argues", "she says"); moral force
  from the order of the facts, not from adjectives.

### Passage 5 - here and now, and the question to carry away (115 words, about 49 seconds)

And here? The Reserve Bank raised its interest rate to bring prices down. Again and again... from a
quarter of a percent to five and a half percent. Higher interest rates slow spending. And when people
spend less, businesses hire less. Since the end of twenty twenty-one, unemployment has climbed from
three point two percent to five point six. Not all of that is down to the interest rate. But five point
six percent is about a hundred and seventy thousand people, looking for work and not finding it. So
next time you hear that new money causes inflation... ask two questions. Spent on what? And who's being
asked to pay to bring prices down?

- Facts: C2-N10 and C2-XD03 (a run of rises from a quarter of a percent to five and a half percent, by
  May 2023); C2-M17 and C2-XD01 (higher interest rates slow spending; businesses hire less); C2-N09,
  C2-N11 and C2-XD05 (3.2% at the end of 2021, 5.6% in the June 2026 quarter, 171,000 people
  unemployed; unemployment also rose while the rate was being cut, so not all of it is the interest
  rate).
- What it shows: brings the pattern home; turns a percentage into people; says the qualification out
  loud in plain words, which makes the line more believable, not less; ends on two questions the
  viewer can carry away, repeating the chapter's refrain.
- Cautions: say the total (about 170,000), never "so many more people": the size of the increase isn't
  sourced (C2-N11). Don't give the number of rises ("again and again" is safe; sources say ten or
  twelve). The 2026 figures are the latest as of 12 October 2026: re-check after 22 October (prices),
  28 October (interest rate) and 4 November (unemployment). For this year's rises use "has raised",
  not "is raising" (C2-XD04).

## 6. Where her campaigning tone and the toolkit's plain style pull against each other

**What "classic style" means here:** the toolkit's word (Thomas and Turner's idea, which Pinker
promotes) for writing that points an equal's attention at something true in the world, calmly, without
showing off or arguing for a side. Klein's tone is campaigning: she argues, takes sides and asks you to
care. The toolkit itself says not to force classic style wholesale onto campaigning writing. So the job
is to keep both, with the facts in charge.

**Where they already agree** (why the blend works): both start from something concrete; both make the
actors visible (who did what to whom); both treat the audience as intelligent equals; both cut jargon
and throat-clearing; both use repetition on purpose (her refrain, the toolkit's one fixed name per
thing).

**Where they pull apart, and how to keep both**

| Pull | Her tone wants | The plain style and the ledger want | How to keep both |
|---|---|---|---|
| Certainty | Firm, unhedged claims | "About", "up to", "partly", "can" kept | Get the confidence from concrete detail, not from dropping a qualifier. Put the qualifier early and small: "about twenty thousand households". |
| Intent | Outcomes read as aims: who benefited must have planned it | What followed, not what caused it, where cause is debated (ledger Traps on C2-H08) | Tell the sequence plainly; let Mattei carry the claim about intent, said as hers, with the other side in one line (passage 4). |
| Number of cases | Line up case after case for force | Few cases, each fully explained, with what it shows | One case per page. One sentence after each saying what it showed. The pattern repeated in the same words. |
| Labels | Vivid new names and slogans | One plain name per thing, never a synonym | Put the vividness in scenes and verbs, never in labels. No coined labels at all, which also keeps her phrases out. |
| Villains or mechanisms | Name the wrongdoers | Show how the thing works | Mechanism first (loans cost more, people spend less, businesses hire less), then who pays. Clear mechanism is the moral clarity. |
| Questions | Provocative, sometimes left hanging | Every question answered soon | Ask, then answer within a breath or two. Only the closing pair is left with the viewer. |
| "We" | "We" as the people fighting back | A calm guide talking to an equal | "We" only for shared New Zealand facts. No calls to act. Neutral before the 7 November election. |
| Irony | Dry asides | Words that mean what they say | Skip irony: on the ear, and in a synthetic voice, it may be heard literally. Use plain contrast instead. |
| Personal witness | "I was there" | A narrator with no life story | Malina shows; she never remembers. Real witnesses only from the ledger. |
| Ending | A call to action | A conclusion within what's known | End on a question the evidence earns ("Spent on what?"), or on people the ledger shows winning something (Glasgow). |

**The same line, three ways (too campaigning, too much like a lecture, both kept)**
- Intent. Too campaigning: "They threw people out of work on purpose." Both kept: "Within a year,
  unemployment jumps from about two in every hundred workers to eleven. /məˈteɪi/ argues that was part
  of the point." (C2-H08, C2-H17)
- Who gains. Too campaigning: "The new money went straight into the pockets of the rich." Both kept:
  "The Bank's own study says the gains went to the people who owned the most." (C2-XB05)
- House prices. Too campaigning: "All that new money sent house prices through the roof." Both kept:
  "House prices rose about thirty percent in twelve months. A Royal Commission found the pandemic
  response was part of the reason... World events played a big part too." (C2-XC03, C2-M10,
  C2-N14; house prices are not in the inflation figure, so keep them apart.)
- Then and now. Too campaigning: "It's nineteen twenty all over again." Both kept: "/məˈteɪi/ said in
  twenty twenty-three that the same pattern was back, in America and Europe." (C2-H19: she was talking
  about America and Europe, not New Zealand.)
- The cause of inflation. Too much like a lecture: "Demand-pull inflation occurs when aggregate demand
  exceeds productive capacity." Both kept: "When people try to buy more than the economy can make...
  prices climb." (C2-M05, C2-XA03)
- Fake urgency. Too campaigning and empty: "Make no mistake, this is a crisis." Both kept: "That's about
  a hundred and seventy thousand people, looking for work and not finding it." (C2-XD05)

**Order of priority when they clash**
1. The ledger: facts, numbers, qualifications, and who said what (Mattei's views as hers; the review's
   and the Bank's words as theirs).
2. Newcomer understanding: every term explained, one name per thing, one idea per sentence, pace.
3. Urgency in her spirit: stakes, who gains and who pays, concrete scenes, direct questions.
4. Rhythm and flourish.

If a line can't be both urgent and accurate, keep it accurate and find the urgency somewhere else: a
number of people, a place, an institution's own words, all from the ledger.

## 7. A listener test (from the toolkit: real feedback, not guesswork)

Nobody has heard these lines yet. After a newcomer watches the rewrite once, ask:
1. In your own words, when does new money push prices up, and when doesn't it?
2. Who paid to bring prices down in Britain after nineteen twenty?
3. Is it a fact that those job losses were deliberate, or someone's argument? Whose?
4. Was there any word you didn't know?

If question 3 comes back "a fact", the urgency around Mattei's claim is drowning out the "she argues":
reword that page. If question 4 names a word, explain it or replace it.

## 8. A quick check for each page

Questions for the writer:
1. Does a viewer know what's at stake for them within the first two sentences of the chapter?
2. Is there a real place, year and group of people (or a picture clearly marked as imagined)?
3. Is it clear who decided, who gained and who paid, and is each in the ledger with its id?
4. Is every claim about intent Mattei's, said as hers?
5. Did every qualification survive ("about", "up to", "partly", "can")?
6. Would someone who missed chapter 1 follow it? Is every term explained at first use?
7. Read aloud at Malina's pace: is there any sentence you'd need to hear twice?
8. Does any phrase sound like a book title or a slogan? Check it against section 4.

The checker: `voice_check.py` in this folder. Run `python3 -I voice_check.py <narration file>`, with
each page starting with a line beginning `## `. It flags the never-use words, capitals, digits,
brackets, exclamation marks, Mattei or Ruhr not written in sound symbols, newcomer terms to check,
sentences over twenty words, pauses and questions per page, and spoken length at about 140 words a
minute. It is a first filter only. It cannot judge facts, qualifications or attributions, and its time
estimate runs high: on the recorded chapter 2 it estimates 4 minutes 53 seconds, against about 4
minutes 25 seconds actually spoken.

## 9. Traps

- "Shock" is the easiest slip: the usual name for 1973 is "the oil shock", and the ledger's own
  sources use it. Say "when oil prices jumped".
- The urgency pulls towards intent ("on purpose", "it's no accident"). Every claim about intent belongs
  to Mattei, said as hers, every time.
- Her habit of piling up cases is exactly what lost viewers. One case per page.
- Don't apply Mattei's 2023 comments about America and Europe to New Zealand as if she'd said them
  about us (C2-H19).
- The 2026 New Zealand figures go stale on 22 October, 28 October and 4 November 2026.
- "Within weeks" (Glasgow) only works after the November line.
- "Not counting pensions" stays with the fifteen hundred pounds.
