## Passage 1 - the opening: stakes first, the viewer's own question

You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. And you've probably heard the warning that comes with it. If the government creates new money, prices will rise. It sounds like common sense. But the answer matters... because the usual cure for rising prices can cost people their jobs. So is the warning right?

## Passage 2 - a real place, real people, then the pattern

Glasgow, nineteen fifteen. War work pulls crowds of workers into the city... where homes are already short. And you can't build a house in a hurry. So landlords raise the rent. In some parts of the city, by up to a quarter. The families renting keep paying the old rent... and refuse the rise. By November, about twenty thousand households are refusing. Within weeks, Britain's Parliament freezes rents. Remember that. When more people want something we can't make more of in a hurry... its price climbs.

## Passage 3 - who gains: follow the new money

Britain, after the crash of two thousand and eight. The Bank of England, Britain's version of our Reserve Bank, creates hundreds of billions of pounds of new money. So where does it go? The Bank's own study, in twenty twelve, gives an answer. The new money pushed up the prices of shares and other investments. Now look at who holds them. The typical household had about fifteen hundred pounds in savings and investments, not counting pensions. The richest five percent had, on average, about a hundred and seventy-five thousand. The Bank says most people would have been worse off without the new money. But the gains went to the people who owned the most.

## Passage 4 - who pays: the usual cure, and Mattei's argument as hers

So what's the usual cure for rising prices? Britain, nineteen twenty. The Bank of England raises its interest rate, the price of borrowing, to seven percent. Loans cost more. People spend less. Prices do fall... but within a year, unemployment jumps from about two in every hundred workers to eleven. Some historians say the economy was heading down anyway, as a short boom after the war ran out. /məˈteɪi/ sees it differently. She argues the lost jobs were part of the point. With fewer jobs to go round, she says, workers lose the power to ask for more.

## Passage 5 - here and now, and the question to carry away

And here? The Reserve Bank raised its interest rate to bring prices down. Again and again... from a quarter of a percent to five and a half percent. Higher interest rates slow spending. And when people spend less, businesses hire less. Since the end of twenty twenty-one, unemployment has climbed from three point two percent to five point six. Not all of that is down to the interest rate. But five point six percent is about a hundred and seventy thousand people, looking for work and not finding it. So next time you hear that new money causes inflation... ask two questions. Spent on what? And who's being asked to pay to bring prices down?
