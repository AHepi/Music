## Page 1: the question

You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. The whole basket. You've heard the warning too. If new money is created... prices will go up. Is that always true? It matters... because when prices go up too fast, the usual cure makes many loans cost more. And that can put people out of work.

## Page 2: who can create new money

Who can create new money? Here in New Zealand, the Reserve Bank can. It also tries to stop prices going up too fast. There are economists who study how modern money works. They argue that our government can't run out of its own money. But it can run out of workers, materials and machines.

## Page 3: the quiet factory

Picture two factories. Think of each one as a whole country... all its workers, materials and machines. In the first, the machines stand still. Workers wait at the gate, hoping for a job. Call it the quiet factory. Now the government spends new money on what the factory makes. The workers go in. The machines start. More money... and more things to buy. So prices barely move.

## Page 4: the flat-out factory

In the second factory, every worker and every machine is already busy... working as hard as they can. No one waits at the gate. Call it the flat-out factory. The same new money is spent on what this factory makes. What happens to prices? Have a guess.

## Page 5: the rule

No spare workers, materials or machines... so no extra things get made. More money... and the same things to buy. So businesses can ask more... and prices go up. Most economists agree on this much. When new money is spent... and there are spare workers, materials and machines... it mostly gets more things made. When nothing is spare... it mostly pushes prices up.

## Page 6: Britain at war

Now a real case. Britain, during the First World War. Millions went to fight. Soon, fewer than one worker in a hundred was out of work. German submarines sank ships carrying food and materials. And the war was paid for partly with new money. More money... and fewer things to buy. By the end of the war, prices had roughly doubled. Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory.

## Page 7: Glasgow's homes

By nineteen fifteen, crowds of war workers had come to Glasgow. There were already too few homes. With builders and materials gone to the war, few new ones were built. Our picture works for one kind of thing too. For homes, Glasgow was like the flat-out factory. Landlords raised the rent... in places by up to a quarter. By November, about twenty thousand families were refusing to pay the extra. Within weeks, Britain's Parliament brought rents on small homes back to what they were before the war.

## Page 8: Japan, the second guess

Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's version of our Reserve Bank planned to double the money it had created... in two years. If the warning is right, prices should go up fast. What do you think happened? Have a guess.

## Page 9: Japan, the answer

Prices barely moved... for about eight years. On average, they went up about half a percent a year. Why? One reason economists give... most of the new money just sat in the banks. In our picture, most of it never reached the factory. So it's not just how much new money there is. It's whether it's spent... and on what. Are there spare workers, materials and machines to make what it buys?

## Page 10: New Zealand, the third guess

Now, here. When Covid hit in twenty twenty, the Reserve Bank created new money. And the government spent more... including help paying wages. By the end of twenty twenty-one, almost everyone looking for work had found it. Only about three in a hundred were out of work. Which factory was New Zealand closer to? Have a guess.

## Page 11: New Zealand, the answer

Close to the flat-out factory. Almost no one was waiting at the gate. And prices here went up fast. Partly because more was being spent... and partly because of prices overseas, like oil. At the peak, the biggest push came from the price of building new homes... and rents.

## Page 12: the usual cure

So what's the usual cure? The Reserve Bank raises its interest rate. Interest is the extra you pay back when you borrow. Many loans cost more... so people have less to spend. Businesses sell fewer things, and hire fewer people. And prices go up more slowly. Usually not down... just more slowly. In our picture, the flat-out factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate.

## Page 13: who pays, here

Who pays for the cure? From late twenty twenty-one into twenty twenty-three, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again and again. Many loans cost more, and jobs got harder to find. By the middle of twenty twenty-six... nearly six in a hundred were out of work. Up from about three. The interest rate isn't the only reason for that. So who pays for the cure? Many people with loans... and people put out of work.

## Page 14: two views

America's version of our Reserve Bank has said the cure would probably mean fewer jobs. It sees that as unfortunate... but not the goal. Clara E. Mattei is the economist whose book this series follows. She argues that fewer jobs are the cure's main goal. With more workers waiting at the gate, she says, people with jobs have less power to ask for higher wages.

## Page 15: back at the checkout

And the checkout? Imagine the basket cost a hundred dollars in June twenty twenty-five. A year later, it cost about a hundred and four. If petrol and diesel prices hadn't changed... it would have cost about a hundred and three. When petrol and diesel go up, the price of almost everything made or moved goes up too... in both factories. So part of what you felt at the checkout came from petrol and diesel.

## Page 16: spent on what?

Does new money always push prices up? No. When new money is spent... and there are spare workers, materials and machines... it mostly gets more things made. When nothing is spare... it mostly pushes prices up. So next time you hear the warning... ask two questions. Spent on what, and is anything spare to make what it buys? And who pays for the cure?
