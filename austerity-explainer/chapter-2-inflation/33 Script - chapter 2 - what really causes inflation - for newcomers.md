# 33 Script - chapter 2 - what really causes inflation - for newcomers

Source: Clara E. Mattei, *The Capital Order* (2022).

**What this is:** chapter 2 rewritten for people who haven't seen chapter 1 and don't know the
subject. Viewers got lost in the recorded version (`19 Script - chapter 2`): it moved too fast
(Malina read it at 164 words a minute, against the 140 planned) and used words most people don't
know (unemployment, central bank, heartland, dearer, and so on). This version teaches one idea, with
one picture and four real cases, explains every word it needs, stops three times for the viewer to
guess, and says the main rule twice in the same words.

It follows your Steven Pinker Teaching and Writing Toolkit (its principles, not Pinker's voice), and
is written in the spirit of Naomi Klein: the stakes up front, the viewer's own questions, who gains
and who pays, never her words. Every fact comes from the checked research ledger
(`18 Research - chapter 2 - what really causes inflation.md`); each page lists its ids.

**How it was made** (every step is in `33 Chapter 2 for newcomers - working files/`): an audit of
where newcomers get lost in the recording, a teaching plan, a voice brief, a choice of facts, three
drafts, three judges, one combined script, two rounds of testing by four fresh helpers each
(imagined newcomers, a fact checker, a rules-and-voice reader, and a skeptic comparing it with the
recording), fixes after each round, and a re-check of every changed sentence.

**Not tested yet:** no real newcomer has heard it, Malina hasn't read it, and nothing is filmed.
The narration file ready for recording is `33 Narration script - chapter 2 for newcomers.json`.

## The script

### Page 1 - The question (about 33 s)

You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. The whole basket. You've heard the warning too. If new money is created... prices will go up. Is that always true? It matters... because when prices go up too fast, the usual cure makes many loans cost more. And that can put people out of work.

- **Research:** C2-XA04, C2-M01, C2-XA05, C2-M14 (the warning's idea, which is about the amount of
  money, not who makes it), C2-M17 and C2-XD01 (the usual cure: "higher charges on many mortgages and
  loans", people spend less, businesses hire less; "can").
- **Terms explained:** inflation ("prices in general, going up... the whole basket"); the usual cure (it
  "makes many loans cost more"; in full on page 12).
- **Pictures:** as before (page-1 reused, parts swapped, code only). The politician still mouths the
  warning: NEW MONEY → PRICES UP?, then IS THAT ALWAYS TRUE?, then MANY LOANS COST MORE → OUT OF WORK.
  The one-tag-swings beat is shortened to match the cut line: every tag flips: THE WHOLE BASKET.

### Page 2 - Who can create new money (about 23 s)

Who can create new money? Here in New Zealand, the Reserve Bank can. It also tries to stop prices going up too fast. There are economists who study how modern money works. They argue that our government can't run out of its own money. But it can run out of workers, materials and machines.

- **Research:** C2-M15 ("can": most money is made by ordinary banks lending; New Zealand began
  targeting inflation around 1990), C2-N04 and C2-M04 (the Reserve Bank's 1-3% target), C2-M17
  (central banks fight prices going up too fast by raising interest rates; the most direct support
  for "tries to stop prices going up too fast"), C2-N06 (the Reserve Bank created new money), C2-M12
  (the view of economists who study how modern money works, said as theirs: "A government that issues
  its own money can't run out of it"; it applies to countries that borrow in their own money, which
  New Zealand does, so "our government" stays within the entry).
- **Terms explained:** who can create new money; the Reserve Bank and what it tries to do; the
  economists' view, in its own sentence and marked "argue"; workers, materials and machines as the
  real limit.
- **Pictures** (code only): page-10's New Zealand street at dusk with the typed card THE RESERVE BANK
  (no logo) and a small CAN CREATE NEW MONEY; then TRIES TO STOP PRICES GOING UP TOO FAST; page-2's
  banknote bundles land: NEW MONEY; the card CAN'T RUN OUT OF ITS OWN MONEY / CAN RUN OUT OF..., then
  page-3's three cut-outs pop up: WORKERS / MATERIALS / MACHINES.

### Page 3 - The quiet factory (about 30 s)

Picture two factories. Think of each one as a whole country... all its workers, materials and machines. In the first, the machines stand still. Workers wait at the gate, hoping for a job. Call it the quiet factory. Now the government spends new money on what the factory makes. The workers go in. The machines start. More money... and more things to buy. So prices barely move.

- **Research:** C2-XA03 (with spare capacity, extra spending mostly raises output), C2-M09 ("more is
  made, not just charged for"), C2-M05 (higher government spending as a source of extra spending).
  The government spending in the picture is a made-up example, not a claimed event.
- **Terms explained:** the two-factories picture and what it stands for; the quiet factory; who spends
  the new money in the picture; why prices barely move (more money, and more things to buy).
- **Pictures:** page-3's works A; A WHOLE COUNTRY: WORKERS, MATERIALS, MACHINES; THE QUIET FACTORY;
  coins rain in: THE GOVERNMENT SPENDS NEW MONEY; the workers go in and goods come out: MORE MONEY /
  MORE THINGS TO BUY (the same layout page 6 uses for MORE MONEY / FEWER THINGS); the price counter
  stays put.

### Page 4 - The flat-out factory, the first guess (about 21 s, then a 4 s hold)

In the second factory, every worker and every machine is already busy... working as hard as they can. No one waits at the gate. Call it the flat-out factory. The same new money is spent on what this factory makes. What happens to prices? Have a guess.

- **Research:** C2-XA03, C2-M05.
- **Terms explained:** flat out; the flat-out factory. The guess now names what to guess about.
- **Pictures:** page-3's works B, gate empty, labelled THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY with a small line under it:
  FLAT OUT = WORKING AS HARD AS THEY CAN. Coins rain in; the counter freezes. WHAT HAPPENS TO PRICES?
  held about 4 seconds, with a small on-screen marker that the film is waiting for a guess.

### Page 5 - The rule (about 30 s)

No spare workers, materials or machines... so no extra things get made. More money... and the same things to buy. So businesses can ask more... and prices go up. Most economists agree on this much. When new money is spent... and there are spare workers, materials and machines... it mostly gets more things made. When nothing is spare... it mostly pushes prices up.

- **Research:** C2-M05 ("more money chasing the same number of things"; "most economists agree": a
  textbook idea accepted across schools), C2-XD01 (the Bank of England's own account: how freely
  businesses can raise prices depends on how much people spend; behind "businesses can ask more"),
  C2-XA03 (about extra spending, hence "when new money is spent"), C2-M09 ("mostly").
- **Terms explained:** spare; why prices go up (businesses can ask more); the rule (recap 1, repeated word
  for word on page 16), now with "spent" first, in the same order as page 9's "whether it's spent...
  and on what".
- **Pictures:** works B's counter flips up; the three cut-outs return stamped NO SPARE, with MORE MONEY /
  SAME THINGS TO BUY, then BUSINESSES CAN ASK MORE, and a small MOST ECONOMISTS AGREE. Then the wide shot of
  both works with two rule cards: NEW MONEY SPENT + SPARE → MOSTLY MORE THINGS MADE and NOTHING SPARE
  → MOSTLY PRICES UP. **Hold the rule cards about 2 seconds after Malina stops**, with the music up,
  before the page turn.

### Page 6 - Britain at war (about 31 s)

Now a real case. Britain, during the First World War. Millions went to fight. Soon, fewer than one worker in a hundred was out of work. German submarines sank ships carrying food and materials. And the war was paid for partly with new money. More money... and fewer things to buy. By the end of the war, prices had roughly doubled. Britain at war was close to the flat-out factory.

- **Research:** C2-H02 (millions were in uniform, which is why so few were out of work; out of work
  "fell below one in a hundred", 1915-1918 annual figures; "soon" because early-1915 months were just
  above one percent, per the second fact check of 12 Oct 2026), C2-H03 and C2-XA02 (submarines),
  C2-XA01 ("partly"), C2-H01 ("roughly doubled"), second fact check (close to the second factory).
- **Terms explained:** out of work (one worker in a hundred); why workers weren't spare (millions went
  to fight).
- **Pictures:** page-2's munitions hall, words matched (code only): BRITAIN, FIRST WORLD WAR; a typed
  card MILLIONS WENT TO FIGHT; the 100 dots, last nearly empty: FEWER THAN 1 IN 100 OUT OF WORK; SHIPS
  SUNK: FOOD, MATERIALS; PARTLY NEW MONEY; MORE MONEY / FEWER THINGS; bars 1914 ×1 and 1918 ABOUT ×2;
  quick cut to works B: CLOSE TO THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY.

### Page 7 - Glasgow's homes (about 38 s)

By nineteen fifteen, crowds of war workers had come to Glasgow. There were already too few homes. With builders and materials gone to the war, few new ones were built. Our picture works for one kind of thing too. For homes, Glasgow was like the flat-out factory. Landlords raised the rent... in places by up to a quarter. By November, about twenty thousand families were refusing to pay the extra. Within weeks, Britain's Parliament brought rents on small homes back to what they were before the war.

- **Research:** C2-XB01 (the influx of war workers "exacerbating an already acute... housing crisis";
  rises "up to 25 percent in some districts"; about 20,000 by November 1915; strikers refused the
  increase, not all rent), C2-H04 (1915), C2-XB02 (small homes held at pre-war rents about five weeks
  after the November peak), second fact check (influx in 1915 confirmed; house building "virtually
  ceased"; labour and materials went to the war; Glasgow building had slumped even before 1914). The
  builders-and-materials line rests on that fact-check row, whose Merrett and Sykes quote was seen
  second-hand: open a source before recording (see the notes for the ledger's owner).
- **Terms explained:** too few homes (plain words in place of "homes were already short"); the picture
  standing for one kind of thing, said aloud before it is used; the swap (builders busy with the war,
  so few new homes); Britain's Parliament.
- **Pictures:** page-4's Glasgow tenement street (code only), titled GLASGOW 1915, with TOO FEW HOMES
  ALREADY; workers stream in; the building-site shot: BUILDERS AND MATERIALS GONE TO WAR / FEW NEW
  HOMES; a quick wide shot of works B shrinking to a row of tenements, with the small card ONE KIND OF
  THING; the tag FOR HOMES: LIKE THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY; RENT RAISED / UP TO +25% IN PLACES; the counter
  to 20,000 FAMILIES REFUSING THE EXTRA; the stamp RENTS BACK TO BEFORE THE WAR. Never WON or RENTS
  FROZEN. No Mary Barbour card. Never "NOT ENOUGH HOMES" (a Naomi Klein title).

### Page 8 - Japan, the second guess (about 17 s, then a 4 s hold)

Japan, twenty thirteen. Japan's version of our Reserve Bank planned to double the money it had created... in two years. If the warning is right, prices should go up fast. What do you think happened? Have a guess.

- **Research:** C2-XB03, C2-C06, second fact check (the Bank of Japan's statement of 4 April 2013: it
  "will double the monetary base... in two years"; the monetary base is the money the central bank
  has created).
- **Terms explained:** Japan's version of our Reserve Bank (meaningful, since page 2 said what ours
  does). "Planned to" replaces "set out to".
- **Pictures:** unchanged (page-5 part A, JAPAN 2013, ×2 MONEY / IN TWO YEARS, the dashed line IF THE
  WARNING IS RIGHT climbs under WHAT HAPPENED?), hold about 4 seconds.

### Page 9 - Japan, the answer (about 33 s)

Prices barely moved... for about eight years. On average, they went up about half a percent a year. Why? One reason economists give... most of the new money just sat in the banks. In our picture, most of it never reached the factory. So it's not just how much new money there is. It's whether it's spent... and on what. Are there spare workers, materials and machines to make what it buys?

- **Research:** C2-XB04 and C2-C06 (about half a percent a year, 2013-2021, on average; 2014 alone was
  about 2.8%, so "on average" stays); C2-C06 and C2-XB03 (most of the new money sat in the banks;
  "one reason"); C2-XA03 and C2-M09 (the "on what" question).
- **Terms explained:** money created but not spent; recap 2, and what "on what" asks. The two numbers
  are now in separate sentences.
- **Pictures:** as before (the almost-level line ABOUT ½% A YEAR; coins piling up in front of the card
  bank: MOST SAT IN THE BANKS; NOT JUST HOW MUCH / WHETHER IT'S SPENT, AND ON WHAT), then the typed
  card SPARE WORKERS, MATERIALS, MACHINES TO MAKE WHAT IT BUYS? **Hold that card about 2 seconds
  after Malina stops**, before the page turn.

### Page 10 - New Zealand, the third guess (about 26 s, then a 4 s hold)

Now, here. When Covid hit in twenty twenty, the Reserve Bank created new money. And the government spent more... including help paying wages. By the end of twenty twenty-one, almost everyone looking for work had found it. Only about three in a hundred were out of work. Which factory was New Zealand closer to? Have a guess.

- **Research:** C2-N06 (new money from March 2020), C2-N14 (the government's Covid spending; its fund
  also covered wage subsidies for over a million workers, hence "help paying wages"), C2-N09 (3.2% in
  the December 2021 quarter).
- **Terms explained:** out of work as a share ("about three in a hundred"); what part of the
  government's money went on (paying wages); "almost everyone looking for work had found it".
- **Pictures:** page-8 part B's New Zealand street (code only): NEW ZEALAND 2020; notes land: NEW
  MONEY; GOVERNMENT SPENT MORE / INCLUDING HELP PAYING WAGES; 2021 with the 100-dot panel, 3 empty:
  ABOUT 3 IN 100 OUT OF WORK, credited Stats NZ (the LOWEST IN 30+ YEARS card is removed with its

### Page 11 - New Zealand, the answer (about 23 s)

Close to the flat-out factory. Almost no one was waiting at the gate. And prices here went up fast. Partly because more was being spent... and partly because of prices overseas, like oil. At the peak, the biggest push came from the price of building new homes... and rents.

- **Research:** C2-N09 (the clue behind "almost no one was waiting"), C2-N15 (the review found the
  economy overheated, with excess demand), C2-N14 (the Royal Commission: the economic response "in
  part" contributed to cost-of-living pressures, with international forces also significant), C2-N16
  (no published split between spending here, Reserve Bank money and world prices; evidence on both
  sides; medium confidence, read from the user's report, not a full page), C2-N01 (7.3% in the year to
  June 2022, the fastest in 32 years: "went up fast" and "the peak"), C2-N03 (imported prices up 8.2%
  against 6.6% for home-grown), C2-N02 (petrol and diesel up sharply on world oil prices; Stats NZ's
  release, opened by the third-round fact checker: "The main driver for the 7.3 percent annual
  inflation to the June 2022 quarter was the housing and household utilities group, due to rising
  prices for construction and rentals for housing", with transport, petrol and diesel, next).
- **Terms explained:** none new. It says the outcome and gives both causes in plain words: "more was
  being spent" (the page 10 spending, with almost no one spare) and "prices overseas". "At the peak"
  ties the homes line to the year prices went up fastest.
- **Pictures** (code only, no new paintings): quick cut to works B with the gate almost empty: CLOSE TO
  THE FLAT-OUT FACTORY. Then PRICES WENT UP FAST, splitting into two small cards: PARTLY: MORE SPENT
  and PARTLY: PRICES OVERSEAS, LIKE OIL (page-7 part A's oil barrel, no counter). Then page-4's
  building-site shot relabelled NEW ZEALAND with AT THE PEAK: THE PRICE OF BUILDING NEW HOMES, AND
  RENTS. Never BUILDING NEW HOMES on its own. No house-price figures, no +30%, no review desk.

### Page 12 - The usual cure (about 31 s)

So what's the usual cure? The Reserve Bank raises its interest rate. Interest is the extra you pay back when you borrow. Many loans cost more... so people have less to spend. Businesses sell fewer things, and hire fewer people. And prices go up more slowly. Usually not down... just more slowly. In our picture, the flat-out factory slows... and more workers wait at the gate.

- **Research:** C2-XD01 (loans cost more, people spend less, prices go up less quickly), C2-M17
  (businesses hire less), C2-H09 (prices did fall after Britain's 1920 rate rise, so "usually not
  down", not "not down").
- **Terms explained:** interest and interest rate; the usual cure in full; "prices go up more slowly.
  Usually not down".
- **Pictures:** as before. Ends on works B: smoke thins, the lights dim, the queue returns: MORE WORKERS
  AT THE GATE.

### Page 13 - Who pays, here (about 35 s)

Who pays for the cure? From late twenty twenty-one into twenty twenty-three, the Reserve Bank raised its interest rate again and again. Many loans cost more, and jobs got harder to find. By the middle of twenty twenty-six... nearly six in a hundred were out of work. Up from about three. The interest rate isn't the only reason for that. So who pays for the cure? Many people with loans... and people put out of work.

- **Research:** C2-N10 and C2-XD03 (rises from October 2021 to May 2023; count given as 10 or 12, so
  "again and again"), C2-XD01 ("many"), C2-N11, C2-XD05 (5.6% in the June 2026 quarter; it also
  climbed while the interest rate was being cut, so "isn't the only reason"), C2-N09 (about three in
  a hundred in late 2021, for "up from about three"), C2-M17 (the cure raises repayments and brings
  fewer jobs: the answer to who pays).
- **Terms explained:** who pays, said plainly at the end, so "pays" is heard as "who carries the harm",
  not "who hands over money for it". The share out of work is in the same unit as page 10, and the
  comparison is now said aloud.
- **Pictures:** the dial climbs in steps: LATE 2021 TO 2023: AGAIN AND AGAIN (no rate figures). The
  100-dot panel from page 10 returns with nearly 6 empty dots: NEARLY 6 IN 100 OUT OF WORK, Stats NZ,
  MIDDLE OF 2026, with a faint UP FROM ABOUT 3, and a small NOT THE ONLY REASON. Then WHO PAYS? lands
  over two cut-outs: MANY PEOPLE WITH LOANS / PEOPLE PUT OUT OF WORK.

### Page 14 - Two views (about 29 s)

America's version of our Reserve Bank has said the cure would probably mean fewer jobs. It sees that as unfortunate... but not the goal. Clara E. /məˈteɪi/ is the economist whose book this series follows. She argues that fewer jobs are the cure's main goal. With more workers waiting at the gate, she says, people with jobs have less power to ask for higher wages.

- **Research:** C2-M17 (Powell, chair of America's central bank, 26 Aug 2022: "very likely be some

## Before recording: read it to one or two of the people who got lost

1. When does new money push prices up, and when doesn't it?
2. Britain at war and Japan both created new money. Why did prices double in one and barely move in the

3. **The key one:** the government creates new money to pay builders for state houses, and many

## How it teaches

**The sentence a viewer should be able to say back:** whether new money pushes prices up depends on
whether it's spent, and on what. When new money is spent and there are spare workers, materials and
machines to make what it buys, it mostly gets more things made. When nothing is spare, it mostly
pushes prices up. And the usual cure is paid for by many people with loans and by people put out of
work.

**The model.** One picture comes before any real case: two factories, each standing for "a whole
country... all its workers, materials and machines".
- The quiet factory: machines still, workers waiting at the gate. The government spends new money on
  what it makes: "More money... and more things to buy. So prices barely move."
- The flat-out factory: every worker and machine "working as hard as they can". "More money... and
  the same things to buy. So businesses can ask more... and prices go up."
- Money that is never spent never reaches the factory.
