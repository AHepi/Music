**What this is:** three ways to make chapter 2 of the austerity explainer, for you to choose from
before anything is recorded or painted. Written 12 October 2026. Source for the series: Clara E.
Mattei, *The Capital Order* (2022). Facts: the entries C2-H, C2-C, C2-N and C2-M in

## What happened

I researched the facts first: 72 sourced facts across four topics (the history after chapter 1, real
cases, New Zealand now, and how inflation works). Then three outlines were drafted from three
different angles. Each was then checked against the facts, your rules and chapter 1, and fixed.

All three outlines:

- pick up from chapter 1's last line: "won't creating it just cause inflation? That's next."
- make your point, "it depends on where and how the money is spent", and also show the honest edge:
  creating money does push prices up when it chases things the economy can't make more of.
- run about 4 minutes in ten pages. That's a little longer than chapter 1's 3 minutes 41 seconds, so
  the script would trim about 30 words.
- reuse many of chapter 1's paintings. New paintings would cost about US$1.50 to US$1.75 of your
  US$10, with room for re-paints. Malina's recording would take about 3,500 ElevenLabs credits,
  about 75 US cents, like chapter 1.

## The picture all three share

Here is the moment that carries your point first, because each outline uses a version of it.

Two copies of chapter 1's little factory stand side by side. In the left one, the machines stand
still and a queue of workers waits at the gate. In the right one, every machine is already running
and the chimney smokes. The same coins drop into both.

- **Left:** the workers slide inside, smoke rises and shells stack up. The price tag stays put. The new
  money made more things.
- **Right:** nothing more can be made, so buyers bid against each other, and only the price tag
  climbs. The new money made higher prices.

That turns chapter 1's own line, "it can run out of workers, materials and machines", into a picture.
The right-hand factory is the honest edge.

## Outline A - Back to the shell factory

**About 4 minutes 8 seconds, 582 words, ten pages.**

**The one idea:** new money pushes prices up when it's spent where workers are already flat out, or
on things we can't make more of in a hurry. So the question is always: spent on what?

## Outline B - What happened next

**About 4 minutes 5 seconds, 580 words, ten pages.**

**The one idea:** new money pushes prices up when it's spent chasing things that have run out or
can't quickly be made, like workers in 1918 or houses in 2021. So what matters is where and how it's
spent.

| Page | What Malina says | What you see (words on screen in capitals) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 The price tag | Inflation means prices in general going up over time. And in chapter 1's story, they did: in Britain, everyday prices roughly doubled during the war. "By 1920 they were about two and a half times what they'd been in 1914... so was it the new money?" | Chapter 1's last frame, then a page turn. A basket and price tag pop up, and the tag grows twice. INFLATION = PRICES UP; x2 BY 1918; x2.5 BY 1920. |

## Outline C - Five price tags

**About 4 minutes 5 seconds, 570 words, ten pages.**

**The one idea:** new money mostly pushes prices up when it's spent on things the economy can't make
more of. So where and how it's spent matters more than whether it's created.

| Page | What Malina says | What you see (words on screen in capitals) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 The claim | "Won't creating money just cause inflation? You've heard it so often it sounds like a law." Inflation means prices in general going up over time. The politician's answer: create money and the price tag grows. So let's test it on five real price tags, with the same question each time: was it the money, a shortage, or where the money was spent? | Parliament. The politician's paper jaw moves, and the money stack and price tag grow side by side. Five tags flip up, and a verdict card with three boxes slides in. MONEY?; SHORTAGE?; WHERE IT WAS SPENT? |
| 2 Germany 1923 | Soldiers march into the Ruhr, and workers down tools, paid with new money. Prices that were already racing go through the roof: by November, one US dollar costs more than four trillion marks. "Verdict? Yes, the money... but money paid for nothing being made." | The pithead and the soldiers. A printing press drops notes into a wheelbarrow, and a loaf's price tag swells. The verdict card is stamped MONEY + NOTHING MADE. |
| 3 Japan 2013 | Japan's central bank set out to double the money it had created, in two years. "If new money alone pushed prices up... they should have soared. They barely moved": about half a percent a year for nearly a decade. Verdict: not the money. | A Tokyo noodle counter. The yen stack grows while the noodle price tag stays put. NOT THE MONEY. |
| 4 Oil 1973 | The price of oil nearly quadrupled in three months. Everything made or moved with fuel got dearer, and by 1980 American prices were rising almost fifteen percent a year. Verdict: a shortage drove the jumps, but America's own central bank says prices were already rising because it let money grow too fast. | A 1970s petrol queue. | 5 New Zealand 2022: what rose | Prices rose 7.3%, the fastest in thirty-two years. Petrol was up about a third, and the cost of building a new house nearly a fifth. "House prices aren't even counted in that 7.3... but they jumped about thirty percent in a year." | A New Zealand street. The pump's tag swells, and a timber house frame appears. | 6 New Zealand 2022: the verdict | Both. "Part came from the world... part came from here." The world meant fuel, shipping and war; here meant spending chasing too few builders and houses. | 7 The war | The fifth price tag is from chapter 1: Britain at war, paid for partly with new money. Almost everyone had a job, and ships were sunk. The queue fills every space, a ship sinks, and the banknotes grow. The tag doubles. FULL STRETCH. |
| 8 The rule | "It isn't whether money is created... it's where and how it's spent." Idle workers and machines: new spending gets them working. At full stretch, or poured into land and houses: the price tag climbs. That's chapter 1's point: a country can run out of workers, materials and machines. | The two factories. A farm plot stays the same size while money sacks pile on and its tag rises. WHERE AND HOW. |
| 9 The cure for blaming the money | If you blame the money, what's the cure? Britain showed in 1920: an interest rate of seven percent. Unemployment rose from about two in a hundred to more than one in ten, and prices fell about a fifth. Mattei argues that was part of the point. | Chapter 1's conference hall, where the NO NEW MONEY card slaps back down. The 7% dial and the iron weight crash in, and the dole queue grows. "So if not unemployment... what else can stop prices rising? That's next." | The dial, the iron weight and the dole queue. The politician returns. WHAT ELSE? |

## What was checked, what wasn't, what I'm unsure of

**Checked**

- Every fact in every outline was traced by a second helper to an entry in the research ledger.
  Problems that check found and fixed:
  - One line said today's price rise is "mostly fuel". The sum is wrong: without fuel, prices still
    rose 2.9%.
  - "Too strong for too long" was put on the wrong report.
  - Britain's money creation was shown beside 0% inflation, but that 0% (in 2015) came from fuel and
    food prices falling.
  - Germany was said to "stop" making coal and steel, but the sources say only that output fell.
- None of the outlines says MMT. Mattei's views are said as hers. No real person is drawn.
- All three name no party. They give only released figures, which matters with the election on
  7 November.

**Not checked**

- Mattei's book itself. As in chapter 1, her views come from her interviews, articles and reviews.
- Some official pages refused automated reading (the Reserve Bank's and Statistics New Zealand's). Those
  figures come from bank and news reports, and from your deficits report.
- The newest New Zealand figures (4.1%, 5.6%, the review, the Royal Commission) date from after my own
  knowledge ends. They come from your report and the news sources the researchers found. The September
  quarter's inflation figure comes out on 22 October and would replace 4.1% if it's out before
  recording.
- The medium-sure facts (how Britain paid for the war, rationing, the Glasgow rent strike) were partly
  read from search summaries. I'll open each page before its fact goes into the script.

**Unsure**

- **Length:** all three run about 4 minutes 5 seconds, so the script will trim them.
- **The date of the Glasgow rent law:** sources differ between late November and December 1915, so the
  script says "within weeks".
- **What caused the 1920-21 slump:** how much the interest rate caused it is argued. All three outlines
  say what followed, not what caused it.
- **How the cure lands with viewers:** any film that asks "who pays for the cure" will read as taking a
  view. That's the series' view, Mattei's, and it's marked as hers.
