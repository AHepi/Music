## Page 1 - the question (0:01–0:19)

So... won't creating new money just cause inflation? You've felt it at the checkout. The same basket of shopping costs more than it did a year ago. That's inflation... prices in general, going up. Not one price tag. The whole basket. To see what really drives it, go back to the shell factory.

## Page 2 - back to the shell factory (0:21–0:42)

Britain, 1915. Unemployment, the share of workers without a job, is under one percent. The war is paid for partly with new money. Submarines are sinking supply ships, and factories are turned over to war work. So more money is chasing fewer things to buy... and by the end of the war, prices have roughly doubled since it began.

Research: C2-H01, C2-H02, C2-H03, C2-XA01, C2-XA02

## Page 3 - two factories (0:43–1:14)

Now picture two factories. In the first, the machines stand idle, and workers wait at the gate. New money arrives... the workers go in, and more shells come out. Prices barely move. In the second, every machine and every worker is already flat out. The same money arrives... but no more shells can be made. So buyers bid against each other, and prices climb. Britain at war was close to the second factory... short of workers, materials and machines.

Research: C2-H02, C2-H03, C2-XA02, C2-XA03

## Page 4 - things you can't make more of in a hurry (1:15–1:34)

Homes work the same way. In 1915, workers poured into Glasgow for war work, and landlords pushed rents up... by as much as a quarter. By November, about twenty thousand households were refusing to pay the rise. Within weeks, Parliament froze rents. Homes can't be built in a hurry.

Research: C2-H04, C2-XB01, C2-XB02

## Page 5 - test one: lots of new money, flat prices (1:35–2:07)

So test it. Japan, twenty thirteen. Its central bank, the bank that creates the yen, sets out to double the money it has created, in two years. If new money on its own pushed prices up, they'd soar. For eight years, they barely move... about half a percent a year. After the 2008 crash, the Bank of England, Britain's central bank, creates hundreds of billions of pounds. Its own study finds the new money pushed up the prices of shares and other investments... and the gains went to whoever owned the most.

Research: C2-C06, C2-C08, C2-C10, C2-XB03 to C2-XB06

## Page 6 - test two: when less is being made (2:09–2:35)

Germany, 1923. Prices are already racing. Then French and Belgian soldiers march into the /rʊə/, Germany's coal and steel heartland. The government tells workers to stop work in protest... and keeps paying them, with new money. By November, one American dollar costs more than four trillion German marks. This time, new money really did push prices up... while less and less was being made.

Research: C2-C02, C2-C03, C2-XC02

## Page 7 - test three: costs, not spending (2:36–2:59)

Late 1973. Prices are already climbing when the price of oil nearly quadruples, in about three months... and almost everything made or moved with fuel gets dearer. So sometimes the cause isn't spending. It's costs. And here? The latest figures say prices rose four point one percent in the year to June. Leave fuel out... and it's two point nine.

Research: C2-C14, C2-N05, C2-M04, C2-M06, C2-XC01, C2-XC05

## Page 8 - the honest edge (3:01–3:27)

So yes... new money can push prices up, when it's spent where workers and machines are already flat out, or on things we can't make more of in a hurry. In twenty twenty-one, our unemployment hit its lowest since records began. House prices rose about thirty percent in twelve months. An independent review of the Reserve Bank says the support from the bank and the government together was right at first... but too strong, for too long.

Research: C2-M05, C2-M09, C2-N08, C2-N09, C2-N15, C2-XC03, C2-XC04

## Page 9 - who pays to fight it (3:29–3:57)

So what's the usual cure? Britain, 1920. The Bank of England pushes its interest rate, the price of borrowing, up to seven percent. Loans cost more, and people spend less. Within a year, unemployment jumps from about two percent to eleven percent. Prices do fall... but people pay with their jobs. /məˈteɪi/ argues that was part of the point. With fewer jobs to go round, she says, workers lose the power to ask for more.

## Page 10 - spent on what? (3:59–4:26)

Here, our central bank, the Reserve Bank, pushed its interest rate from a quarter of a percent to five and a half percent by twenty twenty-three, to bring prices down. Since the end of twenty twenty-one, unemployment has climbed from three point two percent to five point six. And this year, the Reserve Bank is raising its interest rate again. So next time someone tells you new money causes inflation... ask them. Spent on what? And who pays to stop it?
